# Graph Minors VIII: corrections

N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. VIII. A Kuratowski theorem for general surfaces*, J.
Combin. Theory Ser. B **48** (1990), 255–288.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM08/GM08.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM08/GM08.pdf) in the
repository [GraphMinorPapers](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers).

The corrections are in the order of the pages.

## 1. Proof of (6.2), page 264: two $O$-arcs on a sphere

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that "the proof of this is straightforward point-set topology,
and we omit it". The two $O$-arcs may meet in infinitely many points, so the part of $F_2$ outside
the disc bounded by $F_1$ may have infinitely many components.

**Proof.** By the Jordan curve theorem, for $i = 1, 2$ the closure of the component of
$\Sigma - F_i$ that does not contain $z$ is a closed disc $D_i$ with boundary $F_i$. We use several
times that a connected set that misses the boundary of a closed disc lies either in its interior or
outside it. If $D_1 \subseteq D_2$, we may take $F_3 = F_2$, since then the component of
$\Sigma - F_2$ containing $z$ is $\Sigma - D_2$, which contains no point of $F_2$ or of
$F_1 \subseteq D_1$. So we may assume that some point of $D_1$ is not in $D_2$.

First we find a part of $F_1$ that we shall keep. Since $\Sigma - D_2$ is connected, it contains a
path from $z$ to a point of $D_1 - D_2$, and the first point of this path in $D_1$ is a point $p$ of
$F_1 - D_2$. Let $Z$ be the component of $\Sigma - (D_1 \cup D_2)$ containing $z$. Then $p$ is in
the closure of $Z$, since the part of the path before $p$ is connected, contains $z$, and misses
$D_1 \cup D_2$. Choose an arc $Q \subseteq F_1 - D_2$ with $p$ in $Q$ but not an end of $Q$, and let
$A$ be the closure of $F_1 - Q$, which is also an arc.

Since $F_2 \cap D_1$ includes $F_1 \cap F_2$, which has at least two points, each component $J$ of
$F_2 - D_1$ is an open subarc of $F_2$ whose two ends are distinct points of $F_1$, and its closure
$\bar{J}$ meets $D_1$ only in these ends. We call $\bar{J}$ an *excursion*. It is a chord of the
closed disc $\overline{\Sigma - D_1}$, and so it splits this disc into two closed discs. Adding to
$D_1$ the one that does not contain $z$ gives a closed disc $R(J)$ with $D_1 \subseteq R(J)$, whose
boundary consists of $\bar{J}$ and an arc of $F_1$. The rest of $F_1$ is an open arc, which we say
is *removed* by $J$, and it lies in the interior of $R(J)$. No point of $Q$ is removed. For $Z$ is
connected, contains $z$, and misses $D_1 \cup F_2$, which includes the boundary of $R(J)$, and so
$Z$ lies outside $R(J)$. Hence $p$, which is in the closure of $Z$, is not removed by $J$. The ends
of the arc removed by $J$ are in $F_2 \subseteq D_2$, and so not in $Q$. Since $Q$ is connected and
contains $p$, it misses the removed arc. Thus every removed arc is included in $A$.

There may be infinitely many excursions, but for each $\epsilon > 0$ only finitely many of them have
diameter at least $\epsilon$, in a fixed metric on $\Sigma$. This holds since a map from a circle
onto $F_2$ is uniformly continuous, so an excursion of diameter at least $\epsilon$ comes from an
interval of the circle of length at least some $\delta > 0$, and these intervals are disjoint. If
$R(J) \subseteq R(J')$, then the arc removed by $J$ lies in the interior of $R(J')$, and so it is
included in the arc removed by $J'$, whose ends are the ends of $\bar{J'}$. These ends lie in the
arc $A$ on the two sides of the arc removed by $J$, and so, by compactness, their distance is at
least a positive number that depends only on $J$. Hence only finitely many sets $R(J')$ include
$R(J)$, and $R(J)$ is included in a maximal one. Next, let $J \ne J'$, and suppose that the arcs
removed by $J$ and $J'$ meet. We claim that one of $R(J)$, $R(J')$ includes the other. The set $J'$
is connected and misses the boundary of $R(J)$. If $J'$ is in the interior of $R(J)$, then the
boundary of $R(J')$ is included in $R(J)$, and $\Sigma - R(J)$ is connected and contains $z$, so it
lies outside $R(J')$, and $R(J') \subseteq R(J)$. Otherwise $J'$ lies outside $R(J)$. The closure
$K$ of $R(J) - D_1$ is a closed disc, whose interior misses $F_1$ and $\bar{J'}$, and so misses the
boundary of $R(J')$. A common point of the two removed arcs lies in the interiors of $R(J)$ and
$R(J')$, and the points near it outside $D_1$ lie in the interior of $K$, and so the interior of $K$
meets the interior of $R(J')$. Hence $K \subseteq R(J')$, and $R(J) \subseteq R(J')$. This proves
the claim. So the arcs removed by $J$ and $J'$ are disjoint when $R(J)$ and $R(J')$ are distinct
maximal ones.

Now let $F_3$ be obtained from $F_1$ by replacing the arc removed by $J$ with $J$, for each maximal
$R(J)$. Then $F_3 \subseteq F_1 \cup F_2$ and $Q \subseteq F_3$. We claim that $F_3$ is an $O$-arc.
It is the image of a one-to-one map from a circle, since the removed arcs are disjoint, and the open
arcs $J$ are disjoint from each other and from $F_1$. This map is continuous, since each replacement
agrees with $F_1$ at its ends, and for each $\epsilon > 0$ only finitely many replacements have
diameter at least $\epsilon$. This proves the claim. Let $E$ be the closure of the component of
$\Sigma - F_3$ that does not contain $z$, a closed disc with boundary $F_3$.

We show that $D_1 \subseteq E$. The interior of $D_1$ misses $F_3$, since $F_3$ consists of points
of $F_1$ and of open arcs $J$, which are outside $D_1$. The set $Z$ is connected, contains $z$, and
misses $F_3 \subseteq D_1 \cup D_2$, and so it lies outside $D_1 \cup E$. Since $p$ is in $Q$ but
not an end of $Q$, there is a closed disc $N$ containing $p$ that meets $F_1 \cup F_3$ only in a
subarc of $Q$, and this subarc splits $N$ into two connected sides. Since $p$ is in the closure of
$Z$, one side meets $Z$. This side misses $F_1$ and $F_3$, and so it lies outside both $D_1$ and
$E$. Every point of $Q$ is a limit of points in the interior of $D_1$, and also of points in the
interior of $E$, since $Q$ is in the boundary of both. Hence the other side meets the interiors of
both $D_1$ and $E$. Since the interior of $D_1$ is connected, misses $F_3$ and meets the interior of
$E$, it is included in $E$, and so $D_1 \subseteq E$.

Each maximal $R(J)$ is included in $E$. For its boundary is included in
$D_1 \cup \bar{J} \subseteq E$, and $\Sigma - E$ is connected and contains $z$, so it lies outside
$R(J)$. Every point of $F_2$ is in $D_1$ or in some excursion, and so in $D_1$ or in a maximal
$R(J)$. Hence $F_1 \cup F_2 \subseteq E$. The component of $\Sigma - F_3$ containing $z$ is
$\Sigma - E$, and so it contains no point of $F_1 \cup F_2$, as required.

## 2. Proof of (8.1), page 268: the adaptation of Graph Minors III, (4.1)

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that (8.1) "may be proved by a slight adaptation of the proof of
[7, Theorem (4.1)] the details of which we omit". Here [7] is Graph Minors III. The uncrossing in
that proof keeps an inner and an outer circuit fixed, while here the paths end on the $O$-arc $F$,
which is not a circuit of $\Gamma$.

**Proof.** We give the adaptation. If $r = 0$ then $s = 0$, and there is nothing to prove, so we
assume that $r \ge 1$. Extend $\Sigma$ to a sphere, and let $D_F$ be the closed disc in $\Sigma$
bounded by $F$. Since $F$ is centred, there are an $(r, s)$-nest $(C_1, \dots, C_s)$ with each $C_i$
inside $F$, and $r$ mutually disjoint paths of $\Gamma$ between $C_1$ and $F$. Let $D_1$ be the
closed disc bounded by $C_1$ inside $F$. First we trim each of these paths, keeping its part from
its last vertex in $D_1$ to its first later vertex in $F$. (The last point of the path in $D_1$ is a
vertex of $C_1$, and its first later point outside the interior of $D_F$ is in $F$, and so it is a
vertex, since $F$ is $\Gamma$-normal.) The trimmed paths are mutually disjoint, they meet $D_1$ only
in their first vertex, and they meet $F$ only in their last vertex. Let $\Gamma'$ be the subdrawing
formed by $C_1, \dots, C_s$ and the trimmed paths. It lies in $D_F$. Each vertex $v$ of $\Gamma'$ in
$F$ is the last vertex of just one trimmed path, since the circuits $C_i$ are inside $F$ and miss
it, and the only edge of $\Gamma'$ at $v$ is the last edge of this path, which is not a loop. A
circuit uses two edges or a loop at each of its vertices, and $F$ meets $U(\Gamma)$ only in
vertices. Hence no circuit of $\Gamma'$ meets $F$.

The uncrossing in the proof of [7, Theorem (4.1)] needs an inner and an outer circuit that it does
not change, and we add them as follows. The interior of $D_1$ contains no point of $\Gamma'$, since
$C_2, \dots, C_s$ are outside $C_1$ and the trimmed paths meet $D_1$ only in $C_1$. Also no point of
$\Gamma'$ is outside $D_F$. So we may add a circle $C_{in}$ in the interior of $D_1$, with a new
edge from each vertex of $C_1$ to a new vertex of $C_{in}$, and a circle $C_{out}$ outside $D_F$,
with a new edge from each vertex of $\Gamma'$ in $F$ to a new vertex of $C_{out}$, such that the new
edges are mutually disjoint and meet $\Gamma'$ only in their ends. Let $\Gamma''$ be the drawing so
obtained, and let $\Delta$ be the closed disc bounded by $C_{out}$ that includes $D_F$. Extending
each trimmed path by the two new edges at its ends gives $r$ mutually disjoint paths of $\Gamma''$
between $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$, and $C_{in}, C_1, \dots, C_s, C_{out}$ are circuits of $\Gamma''$,
each inside the next.

Now, as in the proof of [7, Theorem (4.1)], consider all choices of circuits $C'_1, \dots, C'_s$ of
$\Gamma''$ such that $C_{in}, C'_1, \dots, C'_s, C_{out}$ are each inside the next and the disc in
$\Delta$ bounded by $C'_j$ includes the disc bounded by $C_j$ $(1 \le j \le s)$, together with $r$
mutually disjoint paths $Q_1, \dots, Q_r$ of $\Gamma''$ between $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$. We have seen
that there is such a choice, and we choose one with the fewest edges in total. Then $Q_i \cap C'_j$
is a path for all $i, j$. For otherwise some $Q_i$ has a subpath with both ends in $C'_j$ and no
other vertex or edge in $C'_j$. If this subpath runs outside $C'_j$, it replaces a part of $C'_j$
and gives a circuit whose disc includes the disc of $C'_j$. If it runs inside $C'_j$, a part of
$C'_j$ replaces it in $Q_i$. In either case, by the proof of [7, Theorem (4.1)], we obtain another
choice as above with fewer edges in total, which is impossible.

The circuits $C'_1, \dots, C'_s$ miss $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$, since each is inside the next, and so
they use none of the new edges, since each new edge has an end in $C_{in}$ or $C_{out}$. So they are
circuits of $\Gamma'$, and they miss $F$, since no circuit of $\Gamma'$ meets $F$. Since $C'_j$ lies
in the interior of $D_F$, and the part of the sphere outside $D_F$ is connected and contains
$C_{out}$, the disc in $\Delta$ bounded by $C'_j$ lies in $D_F$. That is, $C'_j$ is inside $F$. Also
$D_1$ is included in the disc bounded by $C'_1$.

Each $Q_i$ starts in $C_{in}$, in the interior of $D_1$, and ends in $C_{out}$, outside $D_F$. Let
$Q'_i$ be its part from its last vertex in $D_1$ to its first later vertex in $F$. It is a path of
$\Gamma'$, since the new edges lie in the interior of $D_1$ or outside $D_F$, apart from their ends.
Since $Q'_i$ starts in $D_1$, which is included in the disc bounded by $C'_1$, and ends in $F$,
which is outside this disc, $Q'_i$ meets $C'_1$. Let $P_i$ be the part of $Q'_i$ from its first
vertex in $C'_1$ to its end in $F$. Then $P_1, \dots, P_r$ are mutually disjoint paths of $\Gamma$
between $C'_1$ and $F$. For the same reason $P_i$ meets $C'_s$, and its part from its start to its
first vertex in $C'_s$ is a path between $C'_1$ and $C'_s$. So $(C'_1, \dots, C'_s)$ is an
$(r, s)$-nest, with each $C'_j$ inside $F$. Finally, $P_i \cap C'_j$ is the intersection of the
subpath $P_i$ of $Q_i$ with the path $Q_i \cap C'_j$, and so it is a path or null. It is not null,
since $P_i$ runs from $C'_1$, which is $C'_j$ or inside $C'_j$, to $F$, which is outside $C'_j$.

## 3. Proof of (9.1), page 270: the case $k = 2$

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** The proof finds a ring $F_1$ with $|F_1 \cap V(\Gamma)| = \rho(C_1) < k$, keeps
the circuits $C_1, \dots, C_{\lceil k/2 \rceil}$ that $F_1$ does not meet, and says that they form a
$(k, \lceil \frac12 k \rceil)$-nest in the sleeve $\Gamma_1$, which is impossible "because for
example, its cyclomatic number is too small". This fails for $k = 2$. Then $\rho(C_1) \le 1$, and a
sleeve with one ring vertex is a circuit with one path to the ring. A $(2, 1)$-nest is a single
circuit with two vertices, so the circuit of the sleeve is a $(2, 1)$-nest in it, and the sleeve and
the nest have the same cyclomatic number.

**Correction.** Keep more circuits. Let $C_j$ be a circuit of the nest that $F_1$ does not enclose.
The inside of $F_1$ is connected and contains $C_1$, which is inside $C_j$, and a vertex of $C_s$,
which is outside $C_j$, so it meets $C_j$. So $C_j$ meets both sides of $F_1$, and it meets $F_1$ in
at least two vertices. The circuits are disjoint and $|F_1 \cap V(\Gamma)| < k$, so at most
$\lfloor (k-1)/2 \rfloor$ of them are not enclosed by $F_1$, and $F_1$ encloses
$C_1, \dots, C_{\lfloor k/2 \rfloor + 1}$ (they may touch $F_1$). Together with the paths of the
nest cut down to the sleeve, these circuits give a $(k, \lfloor k/2 \rfloor + 1)$-nest in
$\Gamma_1$. Counting edges and vertices shows that a sleeve with fewer than $k$ ring vertices has no
such nest, also for $k = 2$, where the nest has two circuits. So (9.1) holds as printed.

## 4. Definition of the derived portrait, page 272: $U'$ must contain $N'$

**Kind.** Degenerate case.

**What goes wrong.** The paper defines $U'$ as the union of the discs $\Sigma_1, \dots, \Sigma_t$ of
the foundation and the discs $D(e)$ for $e \in E(\Gamma_0)$, sets $N' = V(\Gamma_0)$, and says that
$(U', N')$ is a painting. A painting needs $N' \subseteq U'$, and this can fail for isolated
vertices of $\Gamma_0$. For a drawing with one vertex and no edges, the foundation is empty, so
$U' = \emptyset$, while $N'$ has one element.

**Correction.** Add $V(\Gamma_0)$ to the union:
$$U' = \Sigma_1 \cup \dots \cup \Sigma_t \cup \bigcup (D(e) : e \in E(\Gamma_0)) \cup V(\Gamma_0).$$
The added points are nodes. They give no new cells and do not change the roots on the boundary, so
the rest of the paper is not affected.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM08.IsDerivedPortraitVia` in `GraphMinor/GM08/Defs6to18.lean` (the
definition), and `GraphMinor.GM08.exists_derived_portrait_via` in
`GraphMinor/GM08/Section12Derived.lean`.

## 6. Proof of (11.1), page 273: applying the strengthened (3.8) of Graph Minors III to each $O$-arc

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper proves (11.1) from a strengthened form of [7, Theorem (3.8)] (Graph
Minors III), in which a tree-decomposition $(T, (Y_t : t \in V(T)))$ of $G$ of width $\le w$ gives a
tree-decomposition $(T, (X_t : t \in V(T)))$ of $H$ of width $\le w$ with
$Y_t \cap V(H) \subseteq X_t$ for every $t$ and $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\} \subseteq X_{t_0}$ for some
$t_0$. It says only that (11.1) "follows from this by applying it to each $G_i$ in turn". The
strengthened theorem is about a graph glued to a cylinder, while a truncation has a sleeve inside
each $O$-arc, so the sleeve must first be contracted onto the cylinder, and for this the paths of
the sleeve must meet its circuits in the same cyclic order.

**Proof.** We give the details of this step. Let $\Gamma^*$ be a truncation of $\Gamma$ induced by
$\{F_1, \dots, F_t\}$, with sleeves $\Gamma_1, \dots, \Gamma_t$, and let $w$ be its tree-width. For
$0 \le j \le t$ let $W_j$ be the set of vertices of $\Gamma^*$ inside none of $F_1, \dots, F_j$, and
let $H_j$ be the subgraph of the graph of $\Gamma^*$ induced on $W_j$. We show by induction on $j$
that there is a hypertree $(W_j, T, (X_x : x \in V(T)))$ with $|X_x| \le w + 1$ for all $x$, such
that each edge of $H_j$ has both ends in some $X_x$, and for $1 \le i \le j$ the set
$V(\Gamma) \cap F_i$ is included in some $X_x$. For $j = 0$ this holds since $\Gamma^*$ has
tree-width $w$. Now let $j \ge 1$, and let $(W_{j-1}, T, (X'_x : x \in V(T)))$ be the hypertree for
$H_{j-1}$.

The vertices of the sleeve $\Gamma_j$ are in $W_{j-1}$, since a point inside $F_j$ or in $F_j$ is
not inside any other $F_i$, because $F_i$ and $F_j$ bound disjoint open discs. Let the sleeve
consist of $C_1, \dots, C_s$ and $P_1, \dots, P_r$, where $r = |V(\Gamma) \cap F_j|$. The paths
$P_i$ meet all the circuits $C_l$ in the same cyclic order. For otherwise, for some $l < s$, two of
the paths are consecutive on $C_l$ but not on $C_{l+1}$. Then the ends on $C_{l+1}$ of these two
paths and of two other paths alternate on $C_{l+1}$, while inside $C_{l+1}$ the first two paths are
joined by their parts before $C_{l+1}$ and a part of $C_l$ between them, and the other two are
joined in the same way by the rest of $C_l$. These two connected sets are disjoint, and this is
impossible, since two disjoint connected subsets of a closed disc cannot join two pairs of points of
its boundary that alternate. Now for each path $P_i$ and each circuit $C_l$, contract the part of
$P_i$ from its first vertex in $C_l$ up to just before $C_{l+1}$ (up to $F_j$ if $l = s$), together
with the vertices of $C_l$ that follow $P_i \cap C_l$ up to the next path. Since the order is the
same on all the circuits, this shows that $H_{j-1}$ has a minor obtained from $H_j$ and the
$r \times s$ cylinder by identifying the vertices of its central circle $C_s$, in the notation of
Graph Minors III, (3.8), with the $r$ vertices of $\Gamma$ in $F_j$. The contracted sets are
disjoint and connected, and each vertex of $H_j$ is in the contracted set of just one vertex of this
graph. So taking for each $x$ the vertices of this graph whose contracted set meets $X'_x$ gives a
hypertree of width $\le w$ for it, whose sets include the sets $X'_x \cap W_j$. The strengthened
form of Graph Minors III, (3.8), then gives a hypertree $(W_j, T, (X_x : x \in V(T)))$ for $H_j$ of
width $\le w$, with $X'_x \cap W_j \subseteq X_x$ for all $x$, and with $V(\Gamma) \cap F_j$
included in some $X_x$. For $i < j$ the set $V(\Gamma) \cap F_i$ is included in $W_j$, and so it is
still included in some $X_x$. This completes the induction.

Every vertex of $\Gamma_i$ is inside $F_i$, or in $F_i$ and then in $\Gamma_0$, and so
$W_t = V(\Gamma_0) = N'$. A cell $f$ of $(U', N')$ with closure $\Sigma_i$ has
$\bar{f} - f = V(\Gamma) \cap F_i$, and a cell $f$ with closure $D(e)$ has as $\bar{f} - f$ the set
of ends of $e$, which is an edge of $H_t$. In both cases $\bar{f} - f$ is included in some $X_x$,
and so the tree-width of $(U', N')$ is at most $w$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM08.theorem_11_1` in `GraphMinor/GM08/Results.lean`, proved as
`GraphMinor.GM08.theorem_11_1_proof` from `GraphMinor.GM08.exists_truncation_decomposition` in

## 7. Proof of (11.2), page 273: the hypothesis of (8.4)

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** The proof says that, by (8.4), the drawing $\Xi$ of a forest in a simulation of
the portraits can be chosen to be a subdrawing of $\Gamma_2$, since each $O$-arc of the foundation
of $\Gamma_2$ is centred. But (8.4), page 269, assumes that $U(\Xi) \cap F \subseteq V(\Gamma)$, and
a simulation of portraits asks only that every edge of $\Xi$ is a subset of a cell of $(U', N')$
(condition (i), page 271). For a ring $F$ of the foundation, the cell inside $F$ contains every
point of $F$ that is not a vertex of $\Gamma_2$. Let $u, v$ be vertices of $\Gamma_2$ on $F$, and
let $A$ be an arc of $F$ between them with no other vertex of $\Gamma_2$. Then $\Xi$ may have $A$
minus its ends as an edge, and then $U(\Xi) \cap F$ is not contained in $V(\Gamma_2)$.

**Correction.** Apply (8.4) to the part of $\Xi$ inside each ring $F$, in the following stronger
form: (8.4) holds when its hypothesis $U(\Xi) \cap F \subseteq V(\Gamma)$ is replaced by
$V(\Xi) \cap F \subseteq V(\Gamma)$. The proof of (8.4), from (8.3) and Graph Minors VI, (3.6), uses
the hypothesis only for the vertices of $\Xi$. The weaker hypothesis holds here, since the vertex
set of $\Xi$ is $N' = V(\Gamma_0)$. So (11.2) holds as printed.

## 8. Proof of (11.2), page 273: condition (viii), not (vii)

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** The proof says that the insides of the foundation $O$-arcs are handled
correctly "because of condition (vii) in the definition of $\mathbf{Q}$-portrait simulation".
Condition (vii) on page 271 is about the roots on the boundary of the disc. It says nothing about

$G$ between $F_1$ and $L$ has the path-decomposition formed by $(G_0, A, J)$, where $G_0$ has vertex

of (5) for $L_1$, $L_2'$ and $J$. Here (5) applies, since $v_1$ lies between $L_1'$ and $R$ and not
on $L_1'$, and $v_2$ lies between $L_2'$ and $R$ and not on $L_2'$. These graphs contain all edges
between $F_1$ and $L$, since by (4) the points of $\Gamma$ between $L_1'$ and $L_1$ also lie between
$L_1$ and $R$, so they are on $L_1$, and in the same way those between $L_2'$ and $L_2$ are on
$L_2$. If $R \ne F_2$, the part between $R$ and $F_2$ has the path-decomposition formed by that of
(5) for $F_2$, $R'$ and $K$, followed by $(G_0', K, B)$, where $G_0'$ has vertex set $K$ and no
edges. Here (5) applies, since $w$ lies between $H_0$ and $R'$ and not on $R'$, and these graphs
contain all edges between $R$ and $F_2$, since by (4) the points of $\Gamma$ between $R$ and $R'$
also lie between $H_0$ and $R$, so they are on $R$. The part between $L$ and $R$ has that of (3).
The graphs of one part meet those of another only in vertices of $L$ or $R$, and $T = A \cup B$. So
these path-decompositions, in order, form one of $(G, T)$. Its pieces have width $\ge r + 1$. Their
index is $r$, except for those from (3), whose index is at most $2r + |B| \le k + 2r$, since
$B \subseteq T$. Its sets $Y_i$ other than the first and the last have $r$ elements. The second is
$J$, and the second to last is the vertex set of a hoop between $L$ and $R$, so by (1) there are $r$
disjoint paths between them. So $(G, T)$ is an $r$-train over the class of all tubular grafts of
width $\ge r + 1$ and index $\le k + 2r$.

So (15.1) holds as printed, and so do (15.2), (15.3) and (15.4).

## 11. Statement of (18.1), page 285: surfaces with two or more cuffs

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (18.1) is false when $\Sigma$ has at least two cuffs and the graft $(H, U)$ has
roots. Its proof derives outcome (iii) from Graph Minors VII, (9.1), and "the discussion in Section
8" of that paper, which includes the remark that every schismatic set includes a schism, so that
$\omega(\Gamma) \le \alpha(\Gamma)$. That remark is false, and so is the bound
$\omega(\Gamma) \le \alpha(\Gamma)$, as the example below shows (see also the correction to the
remark in Section 8, page 242, in [GM07.md](GM07.md)). The following example refutes both the step
and the statement.

Let $\Sigma$ be a pants with cuffs $C, E_1, E_2$. Let $(H, U)$ be a triangle whose three vertices
are its roots, and let $\Delta$ draw it with one root on each cuff and its edges in the interior of
$\Sigma$. Given $N$, let $W = N + 1$, choose $p \in C$ and $q_i \in E_i$ ($i = 1, 2$), and draw a
graph $G$ in $\Sigma$, with $V(G) \cap bd(\Sigma) = \{p, q_1, q_2\} = T$, made of the following
parts.

- For $i = 1, 2$, a part $K_i$: disjoint cycles $Z_i^1, \dots, Z_i^W$ that surround $E_i$, miss $p$,
  and are nested with $Z_i^1$ innermost, and paths $R_i^1, \dots, R_i^W$ from $q_i$ to $p$ that
  pairwise meet only at $q_i$ and $p$.
- Cycles $O^1, \dots, O^W$ through $p$ that pairwise meet only at $p$ and otherwise lie in the
  interior of $\Sigma$. For each $j$, $\Sigma - O^j$ has two components: one contains $C - \{p\}$,
  and the other contains $E_1$, $E_2$ and $K_1 \cup K_2 - \{p\}$.
- $K_1$, $K_2$ and the cycles $O^j$ pairwise meet only at $p$.

Such a drawing exists: in a plane picture with $C$ the outer circle, draw each $K_i$ in a disc
around $E_i$ that touches $C$ only at $p$, with the paths $R_i^j$ running from $q_i$ out through all
the cycles $Z_i^k$ to $p$, and draw the $O^j$ as nested loops from $p$ around both discs. Let
$\Gamma$ be this drawing. None of (i)–(iv) holds.

- (ii) fails, since each cuff contains exactly one vertex of $\Gamma$ and one of $\Delta$.
- (i) fails. Every edge of $G$ lies in one of the parts, so in $G - p$ no edge joins $K_1 - p$ to
  $K_2 - p$. Suppose $\sigma$ is a simulation of $(H, U)$ in $(G, T)$. By (S6) and (S2), the three
  sets $\sigma(u)$ contain $p$, $q_1$, $q_2$, one each. The two that contain $q_1$ and $q_2$ miss
  $p$, and they are connected, so they lie in $K_1 - p$ and in $K_2 - p$. By (S5), the edge of $H$
  between their roots is sent to an edge of $G$ that joins them, and there is none.
- A fact used below. Let $A$ be an $O$-arc that surrounds a cuff $C'$, with
  $A \cap bd(\Sigma) \subseteq C'$. The disc bounded by $A$ in $\Sigma + \hat{C'}$ contains the
  pasted disc, since otherwise $A$ would be planar in $\Sigma$, and it contains no point of another
  cuff. So every path in $\Sigma$ from a point of $C' - A$ to another cuff meets $A$.
- (iv) fails. Such an $O$-arc $F$ would have fewer vertices than the one vertex of $\Gamma$ on $C'$,
  so none. But the path $R_i^1$ (with $i$ such that $C' = E_i$, or $i = 1$ if $C' = C$) joins a
  point of $C' - F$ to another cuff, so it meets $F$, at a vertex since $F$ is $\Gamma$-normal.
- (iii) fails, because every $\Gamma$-normal schism $X$ has at least $W$ vertices. Each point of $X$
  on a path or cycle of $G$ is a vertex, since $X$ is $\Gamma$-normal, and the paths and cycles
  named below are disjoint apart from the points listed, so it is enough that $X$ meets each of them
  away from those points. - Type (i) does not occur: pasting discs on the three cuffs gives a
  sphere, and one side of an $O$-arc there contains at most one pasted disc, so every $O$-arc of
  $\Sigma$ is near-planar. - Types (iv), (v) and (vi): $X$ contains an $O$-arc $A$ that misses
  $bd(\Sigma)$ and surrounds a cuff $C'$. By the fact above, $A$ meets every $R_i^j$ (with $i$ as
  before), and not at its ends. - Type (ii): $X$ has an end in some $E_i$. Each $Z_i^k$ surrounds
  $E_i$ and misses $bd(\Sigma)$, so $X$ meets it, by the fact above. - Type (iii) with its ends on
  $E_i$: if $X$ lay in the disc of $\Sigma + \hat{E_i}$ bounded by $Z_i^W$, then $X \cup E_i$ would
  be near-planar. So $X$ meets $Z_i^W$, and the part of $X$ from $E_i$ to its first point on $Z_i^W$
  meets every $Z_i^k$ with $k < W$, since the cycles are nested. - Type (iii) with its ends on $C$:
  if $E_1$ and $E_2$ were on the same side of $X$, the other side together with the disc pasted on
  $C$ would be a disc containing $X \cup C$, which would then be near-planar. So $X$ separates $E_1$
  from $E_2$. If $p \notin X$, then $X$ meets each path $R_1^j \cup R_2^l$ from $q_1$ to $q_2$ at a
  point other than $p, q_1, q_2$, so it meets every $R_1^j$ or every $R_2^l$. If $p \in X$, then $p$
  is an end of $X$, and the other end is in $C - \{p\}$. If $X \cap O^j = \{p\}$ for some $j$, then
  $X - \{p\}$ is connected and lies in the component of $\Sigma - O^j$ that contains $C - \{p\}$, so
  the other component, which contains $E_1$ and $E_2$ and is connected, misses $X$, a contradiction.
  So $X$ meets every $O^j$ at a point other than $p$.

So for this $\Delta$ no number $N$ works. In the same drawing, take two loops at $p$, one around
$K_1$ and one around $K_2$, both inside every $O^j$ and meeting $U(\Gamma)$ only at $p$. Their union
is an $X_2$-arc, which is $\Gamma$-normal. It is not near-planar, since its two $O$-arcs surround
the distinct cuffs $E_1$ and $E_2$, while each of its $O$-arcs is near-planar. So it is schismatic
in the sense of Graph Minors VII, Section 8, with one vertex. Thus $\alpha(\Gamma) \le 1$, while
every $\Gamma$-normal schism has at least $W$ vertices, so $\omega(\Gamma) \ge W$.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that $\Sigma$ has at most one cuff or that $U = \emptyset$. With
it, (18.1) holds. The proof of (18.3) uses (18.1) for every surface, so for a general surface we
prove the following form, which is enough there. For a graft $(G, T)$, its root-leaf graft is
obtained by adding, for each $t \in T$, a new vertex adjacent only to $t$, and taking the new
vertices as the roots. The corrected (18.1) keeps outcomes (i) and (ii) as printed. Outcomes (iii)
and (iv) are asserted for some drawing of the root-leaf graft of $(G, T)$ in $\Sigma$ with the same
vertices on each cuff as $\Gamma$, and the schism in (iii) misses $bd(\Sigma)$. This drawing is
obtained by pushing $\Gamma$ off a collar of $bd(\Sigma)$ and joining each root to its old place by
a spoke of the collar. A simulation of root-leaf grafts gives a simulation of the original grafts,
so the proof of (18.3) can pass to root-leaf grafts and back. With this form, (18.3) and (6.1) hold
as printed. In the example above, the drawing of the root-leaf graft has a schism of type (iv) with
one vertex, the old root $p$.

## 12. Proof of (18.2), page 286: $c'$, not $e'$
