## 1. Proof of (1.2), page 2 of the preprint: a vital $5$-linkage in a simple graph

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** (1.2) says that if $L$ is a vital $5$-linkage in a simple graph $G$, then
either some two terminals in different components of $L$ are adjacent in $G$, or some terminal has
the same degree in $G$ and in $L$, or $G$ is null. The paper states (1.2) in Section 1 without
proof.

**Proof.** We give a proof of (1.2). Suppose that $G$ is not null and that the first two outcomes do
not hold. Then each terminal $t$ of $L$ has larger degree in $G$ than in $L$, and so there is an
edge $e \notin E(L)$ of $G$ incident with $t$; let its other end be $u$. Then $u \ne t$, since $G$
is simple.

(1) *For each such $t$ and $u$, $u$ is an internal vertex of a path of $L$ that does not contain
$t$.*

*Subproof.* Suppose that $u$ belongs to the component $P$ of $L$ that contains $t$. Then $P$ has an
edge, since $u \ne t$, and $t$ is an end of $P$; let $P$ have vertices $t = v_0, v_1, \dots, v_m$ in
order, and let $u = v_j$ where $j \ge 1$. If $j = 1$ then $e$ and the edge of $P$ between $v_0$ and
$v_1$ are parallel edges of $G$, which is impossible. If $j \ge 2$, let $P'$ be obtained from $P$ by
replacing its subpath between $v_0$ and $v_j$ by $e$. Then $P'$ is a path with the same ends as $P$,
and so replacing $P$ by $P'$ gives a linkage in $G$ with the same pattern as $L$, and different from
$L$ since it contains $e$. This contradicts that $L$ is vital. Hence $u \notin V(P)$. Since
$V(L) = V(G)$, $u$ belongs to another component $Q$ of $L$, and $u$ is not a terminal of $L$ since
the first outcome does not hold. Hence $u$ is an internal vertex of the path $Q$. This proves (1).

A path of $L$ that has an internal vertex has two distinct ends, both terminals of $L$. Since $L$
has at most five terminals, it follows that

(2) *There do not exist three vertices of $L$, none of them a terminal of $L$, that belong to three
different components of $L$.*

Since $G$ is not null, some vertex of $G$ is not a terminal of $L$ (if $v \in V(G)$ is a terminal,
apply (1) to $v$). Let $y$ be such a vertex, and let $P$ be the component of $L$ that contains it;
then $P$ is a path with distinct ends $a, b$. By (1), $a$ and $b$ are adjacent in $G$, by edges not
in $E(L)$, to internal vertices $x_1, y_1$ of paths of $L$ different from $P$. By (2) applied to
$y, x_1, y_1$, the vertices $x_1$ and $y_1$ belong to the same component $Q$ of $L$. Let $Q$ have
ends $c, d$. By (1) again, $c$ and $d$ are adjacent in $G$, by edges not in $E(L)$, to internal
vertices $x_2, y_2$ of paths of $L$ different from $Q$; and by (2) applied to $y, x_1, x_2$ and to
$y, x_1, y_2$, both $x_2$ and $y_2$ belong to $P$. Let $P'$ be the path formed by the edge between
$a$ and $x_1$, the subpath of $Q$ between $x_1$ and $y_1$, and the edge between $y_1$ and $b$; and
let $Q'$ be the path formed in the same way from $c$, the subpath of $P$ between $x_2$ and $y_2$,
and $d$. These are paths, since $a, b \notin V(Q)$ and $c, d \notin V(P)$. They are disjoint, since
$P'$ has no vertex outside $V(Q) \setminus \{c, d\}$ except $a, b$, and $Q'$ has no vertex outside
$V(P) \setminus \{a, b\}$ except $c, d$. Hence replacing $P, Q$ by $P', Q'$ gives a linkage in $G$
with the same pattern as $L$, different from $L$ since the edge between $a$ and $x_1$ is not in
$E(L)$. This contradicts that $L$ is vital, and so proves (1.2).

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_1_2` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Results.lean`, proved as
`GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_1_2_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Section1Two.lean`. In the same file,
`GraphMinor.GM21.not_reachable_of_extra_edge` and `GraphMinor.GM21.exists_inner_neighbour` give (1),
`GraphMinor.GM21.not_three_components` gives (2), `GraphMinor.GM21.exists_exchange_path` and
`GraphMinor.GM21.not_exchange` give the exchange, and `GraphMinor.GM21.vertexSet_eq_empty_of_vital`
puts these together. The shortcut in the proof of (1) is
`GraphMinor.GM21.no_chord_of_isVitalLinkage` in `GraphMinor/GM21/LinkagePaths.lean`.

## 2. Statement of (2.3), page 2 of the preprint: a vertex left alone

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (2.3) says that if $L$ is a linkage in $G$, $(A, B)$ is a separation of $G$,
and $L'$ is a linkage in $B$ with the same pattern as $L \cap B$, then $(L \cap A) \cup L'$ is a
linkage in $G$ with the same pattern as $L$. Suppose that a vertex $v$ of $V(A \cap B)$ is an
internal vertex of a path of $L \cap B$, and that $L'$ does not use $v$. Then $v$ has degree $0$ in
$L \cap A$, so it is a one-vertex component of $(L \cap A) \cup L'$, and the pattern changes. For
example, let $G$ be the 4-cycle $a, v, b, x$, let $L$ be the path $a, v, b$, let $A$ have vertices
$a, v, b$ and no edges, let $B = G$, and let $L'$ be the path $a, x, b$. Then $L \cap B = L$ and
$L'$ both have the pattern $\{\{a, b\}\}$, but $(L \cap A) \cup L'$ has the pattern
$\{\{a, b\}, \{v\}\}$.

**Correction.** In the first claim, delete from $L \cap A$ the vertices that are internal vertices
of paths of $L \cap B$. This deletes no edge, since such a vertex has both its edges of $L$ in $B$,
and so it has degree $0$ in $L \cap A$. The second claim of (2.3), that $L \cap B$ is a vital
linkage in $B$ if $L$ is a vital linkage in $G$, holds as printed. The later uses of (2.3) only need
a linkage inside $(L \cap A) \cup L'$ with the pattern of $L$, and the corrected union is one.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_2_3` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Results.lean`. The graph $L \cap A$
with these vertices deleted is `GraphMinor.GM21.prunedLeft`, in
`GraphMinor/GM21/SeparationGluing.lean`. The example is
`GraphMinor.GM21.Checks.theorem_2_3_literal_union_counterexample` in `Checks/GM21Examples.lean`.

## 3. Proof of (2.3), page 2 of the preprint: replacing $L \cap B$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After (2.2) the paper says "Again, the proof is clear, as is the proof of the
next lemma." The next lemma is (2.3), in the corrected form given above: with $L_A$ the graph
$L \cap A$ without the vertices that are internal vertices of paths of $L \cap B$, the graph
$L_A \cup L'$ is a linkage in $G$ with the same pattern as $L$, and if $L$ is vital then $L \cap B$
is a vital linkage in $B$. The first claim needs a check that $L_A \cup L'$ is a union of disjoint
paths, and that their ends are paired as in $L$.

**Proof.** We give the proof of (2.3). Let $L_A$ be obtained from $L \cap A$ by deleting the
vertices that are internal vertices of paths of $L \cap B$, and let $M = L_A \cup L'$. Since
$(A, B)$ is a separation, every edge of $L$ belongs to exactly one of $L \cap A$, $L \cap B$; so a
vertex that is internal in $L \cap B$ has degree $0$ in $L \cap A$, and $L_A$ has all the edges of
$L \cap A$. Since $L'$ and $L \cap B$ have the same pattern, they have the same terminals, and each
terminal has the same degree in both (degree $0$ if its block has one member, and $1$ otherwise). In
particular, every vertex of $V(L_A) \cap V(B)$ is a terminal of $L \cap B$ and of $L'$, since it is
in $V(L \cap B)$ and is not internal there.

(1) *Every vertex of $M$ has degree $\le 2$ in $M$; every vertex of $M$ that is in $V(L_A)$ or is a
terminal of $L \cap B$ has the same degree in $M$ and in $L$; and $M$ and $L$ have the same
terminals.*

*Subproof.* Let $v \in V(M)$. If $v$ is in $V(L_A)$ or is a terminal of $L \cap B$, then its edges
in $L_A$ are its edges in $L \cap A$, and its edges in $L'$ are as many as its edges in $L \cap B$,
by the remarks above (both numbers are $0$ if $v \notin V(L \cap B)$, because then $v \notin V(L')$,
as every vertex of $V(L') \cap V(L_A)$ and every terminal of $L'$ is in $L \cap B$). So its degree
in $M$ is its degree in $L$. Otherwise $v \in V(L')$ is not a terminal of $L'$, and has degree $2$
in $L'$ and in $M$. This proves the first two statements. For the third, a terminal $t$ of $L$ is
either in $V(L \cap A)$ and not internal in $L \cap B$, so in $V(L_A)$, or in $V(L \cap B)$ with
degree $\le 1$, so a terminal of $L \cap B$; and in both cases $t$ has degree $\le 1$ in $M$.
Conversely, a vertex of degree $\le 1$ in $M$ is in $V(L_A)$ or is a terminal of $L'$, that is, of
$L \cap B$, and so has the same degree in $L$. This proves (1).

(2) *Let $H \subseteq M$ have at least one edge, such that for each component $C$ of $L'$, every
vertex of degree $1$ in $H \cap C$ is an end of $C$. Then there exists $H_L \subseteq L$ with at
least one edge, such that a vertex has degree $1$ in $H_L$ if and only if it has degree $1$ in $H$.*

*Subproof.* Let $C$ be a component of $L'$ with an edge in $H$. The components of $H \cap C$ with an
edge are subpaths of $C$ whose two ends have degree $1$ in $H \cap C$, and so are ends of $C$; hence
$H \cap C = C$. Let $H_L$ be the union of $H \cap L_A$ and, for each component $C$ of $L'$ with
$C \subseteq H$, the path of $L \cap B$ with the same ends as $C$ (it exists since $L'$ and
$L \cap B$ have the same pattern). Then $H_L \subseteq L$, and $H_L$ has an edge, since $H$ has one.
An end of such a path has one edge in it and one edge in $C$, and its other edges in $H_L$ and in
$H$ are those of $H \cap L_A$. An internal vertex of such a path has degree $2$ in $H_L$; it is not
in $L_A$, and not an end of a component of $L'$, so it does not have degree $1$ in $H$. Every other
vertex of $H$ or $H_L$ has the same edges in $H_L$ as in $H \cap L_A$, and in $H$ it has in addition
either no edge of $L'$ or two (if it is internal in some $C \subseteq H$, and then it is not in
$L_A$, by the last remark of the first paragraph). This proves (2).

Now $M$ is a linkage in $G$. For by (1) it has maximum degree $\le 2$, and if it had a circuit $H$,
then (2) would apply to $H$: a vertex of degree $1$ in $H \cap C$ has an edge of $H$ in $L_A$, and
so is a terminal of $L'$, that is, an end of $C$. Then $H_L \subseteq L$ has an edge and no vertex
of degree $1$, which is impossible. By (1), $M$ and $L$ have the same terminals. Let $u \ne w$ be
terminals in the same component of $M$, and let $R$ be the path of $M$ between them. By the same
argument (2) applies to $R$, since $u, w$ are terminals of $L$, and so are terminals of $L'$ if they
belong to $L'$. So there exists $H_L \subseteq L$ in which $u, w$ are the only vertices of degree
$1$; since $L$ has maximum degree $\le 2$, the component of $H_L$ that contains $u$ is a path, and
its other end is $w$. Hence $u, w$ are in the same component of $L$. Since a terminal of degree $0$
in $M$ has degree $0$ in $L$ by (1), every block of the pattern of $M$ is a block of the pattern of
$L$, and since the two patterns partition the same set, they are equal.

For the second statement, suppose that $L$ is vital. Then $V(L \cap B) = V(B)$, and $L \cap B$ is a
linkage in $B$. Let $L'$ be a linkage in $B$ with the same pattern as $L \cap B$. By the first
statement, $M$ is a linkage in $G$ with the same pattern as $L$, and so $M = L$. An edge of
$L \cap B$ is therefore in $L_A$ or in $L'$, and not in $L_A$ since $E(A \cap B) = \emptyset$; so
$E(L \cap B) \subseteq E(L')$, and $E(L') \subseteq E(M) \cap E(B) = E(L \cap B)$. A vertex of
$L \cap B$ is either a terminal of $L \cap B$, and so of $L'$, or an internal vertex, which is in
$V(M)$ but not in $V(L_A)$, and so is in $V(L')$; and
$V(L') \subseteq V(M) \cap V(B) = V(L \cap B)$. Hence $L' = L \cap B$, and so $L \cap B$ is vital.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_2_3` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Results.lean`, proved as
`GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_2_3_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Section2.lean`. The rest is in
`GraphMinor/GM21/SeparationGluing.lean`: the graph $L_A$ is `GraphMinor.GM21.prunedLeft`, the first
claim is `GraphMinor.GM21.prunedLeft_union_isLinkage_and_pattern`, which uses
`GraphMinor.GM21.isLinkage_of_deg_le_two_of_exists_deg_one`,

`GraphMinor.GM21.eq_inter_right_of_prunedLeft_union_eq`.

## 4. Proof of (2.4), page 3 of the preprint: splitting a vertex

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** (2.4) says that if $G'$ is obtained from $G$ by splitting a vertex $v$, and $G$
has a vital $p$-linkage, then $G'$ has a vital $(p + 2)$-linkage. The proof takes the subgraph $L'$
of $G'$ with $V(L') = V(G')$ and $E(L') = E(L)$, and says "Then $L'$ is a vital $(p+2)$-linkage as
is easily seen." That $L'$ is a linkage, that it has at most $p + 2$ terminals, and that it is
vital, are not checked.

**Proof.** We give the details. A vertex of $G'$ other than $v_1, v_2$ has the same edges in $L'$ as
in $L$, and the degree of $v$ in $L$ is the sum of the degrees of $v_1$ and $v_2$ in $L'$. So $L'$
has maximum degree $\le 2$. If some $H' \subseteq L'$ had an edge and no vertex of degree $1$, then
the subgraph of $L$ formed by the edges of $H'$ and their ends would also have an edge and no vertex
of degree $1$ (the degree of $v$ in it is the sum of two numbers, each $0$ or $2$), which is
impossible. Hence $L'$ is a linkage in $G'$. Its terminals are the terminals of $L$ other than $v$,
and possibly $v_1, v_2$, and so it is a $(p+2)$-linkage; and $V(L') = V(G')$. In the same way,

(1) *If $v_1$ and $v_2$ both have degree $1$ in $L'$, they belong to different components of $L'$,*

since otherwise the path of $L'$ between them would give a subgraph of $L$ with an edge and no
vertex of degree $1$ (the vertex $v$ having degree $2$ in it).

Now let $M'$ be a linkage in $G'$ with the same pattern as $L'$. Then $M'$ and $L'$ have the same
terminals, each with the same degree in both, and a vertex of $M'$ that is not a terminal of $L'$
has degree $2$ in $M'$. Let $M$ be the subgraph of $G$ with $E(M) = E(M')$, whose vertices are the
ends of these edges in $G$, the vertices of $M'$ other than $v_1, v_2$, and also $v$ if $v$ is a
terminal of $L$. Then each vertex other than $v$ has the same edges in $M$ as in $M'$, and the
degree of $v$ in $M$ is the sum of the degrees of $v_1, v_2$ in $M'$.

(2) *$M$ is a linkage in $G$, with the same terminals as $L$, and each terminal of $L$ has the same
degree in $M$ and in $L$.*

*Subproof.* If $v_1$ and $v_2$ are both terminals of $L'$, they have the same degrees in $M'$ as in
$L'$, and so $v$ has the same degree in $M$ as in $L$. Otherwise one of them, say $v_1$, has degree
$2$ in $L'$; then $v_2$ has degree $0$ in $L'$ and in $M'$, $v$ is not a terminal of $L$, and $v$
has degree $0$ or $2$ in $M$ (and if $0$, then $v \notin V(M)$). Every other vertex of $M$ has
degree $\le 2$, and is a terminal of $M$ if and only if it is a terminal of $M'$, that is, of $L'$,
that is, of $L$; and it then has the same degree in all four. If $v$ is a terminal of $L$, then
$v_1, v_2$ are terminals of $L'$, and so $v$ is a terminal of $M$ with its degree in $L$. Next,
suppose that $C$ is a circuit of $M$. The edges of $C$ form a circuit of $M'$, which is impossible,
unless $C$ passes through $v$ with one edge incident with $v_1$ and the other with $v_2$ in $G'$;
and then they form a path of $M'$ between $v_1$ and $v_2$. In this case $v_1, v_2$ have degree $1$
in $M'$, since $v$ has degree $2$ in $M$; so they are terminals of $M'$ in the same component, and
$\{v_1, v_2\}$ is a block of the pattern of $M'$, and so of $L'$, contrary to (1). This proves (2).

(3) *$M$ has the same pattern as $L$.*

*Subproof.* Let $u \ne w$ be terminals of $M$ joined by a path $C$ of $M$, and let $C'$ be the
subgraph of $M'$ formed by the edges of $C$ and their ends in $G'$. The vertices of degree $1$ in
$C'$ are $u$ and $w$ (with $u$ or $w$ replaced by $v_1$ or $v_2$ if it equals $v$), and also $v_1$
and $v_2$ if $C$ passes through $v$ with one edge incident with each. All of them are terminals of
$M'$: $u$ and $w$ since they are terminals of $L$ (if $v \in \{u, w\}$, then $v_1, v_2$ are
terminals of $L'$, since $v$ has degree $\le 1$ in $L$), and $v_1, v_2$ in the last case since $v$
has degree $2$ in $M$. Hence each component of $C'$ is a component of $M'$, and its ends form a
block of the pattern of $M'$, and so of $L'$. Let $H$ be the subgraph of $L$ formed by the edges of
the paths of $L'$ between these pairs of ends. Then $u, w$ have degree $1$ in $H$, and every other
vertex has degree $0$ or $2$ (the vertex $v$ has degree $2$ in $H$ in the last case, since then
$v_1, v_2$ are ends of these paths of $L'$). Hence the component of $H$ that contains $u$ is a path
of $L$ from $u$ to $w$. Since a terminal of degree $0$ in $M$ has degree $0$ in $L$, by (2), every
block of the pattern of $M$ is a block of the pattern of $L$, and since $M$ and $L$ have the same
terminals, the two patterns are equal. This proves (3).

From (2), (3) and since $L$ is vital, $M = L$. Hence $E(M') = E(M) = E(L) = E(L')$. Every terminal
of $L'$ is in $M'$, and every other vertex of $L'$ is incident with an edge of $L'$, which is in
$M'$; so $V(M') = V(G') = V(L')$, and $M' = L'$. Thus $L'$ is vital.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_2_4` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Results.lean`, proved as
`GraphMinor.GM21.theorem_2_4_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM21/Section2.lean`. The rest is in
`GraphMinor/GM21/VertexSplitting.lean`: $L'$ is `GraphMinor.GM21.splitSpanningSubgraph`, and
`GraphMinor.GM21.isLinkage_splitSpanningSubgraph`,
`GraphMinor.GM21.terminals_splitSpanningSubgraph_ncard_le` and
`GraphMinor.GM21.not_reachable_split_vertices_of_deg_one` give the first two facts. The graph $M$ of
$G$ made from a linkage of $G'$ is `GraphMinor.GM21.unsplitSubgraph`, with its properties in
`GraphMinor.GM21.unsplitSubgraph_degree_and_terminals` and
`GraphMinor.GM21.unsplitSubgraph_linkagePattern_eq`, and vitality is
`GraphMinor.GM21.isVitalPLinkage_splitSpanningSubgraph`.

## 9. Proof of (4.1), page 11 of the preprint: cutting a surface along a line

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** (4.1) lists three facts about the surface $\Sigma'$ obtained by cutting
$\Sigma$ along a proper line or an $O$-arc $F$, and says "The proof is straightforward, and we omit
it."

**Proof.** We give the details. We use the classification of surfaces: every surface is homeomorphic
to the surface obtained from a sphere by adding $a$ handles and $b$ crosscaps and then deleting the
interiors of $c$ disjoint closed discs, for some $a, b, c \ge 0$, and then it has $c$ cuffs. In each
part, $\Sigma$ is obtained from $\Sigma'$ by pasting together the two copies of $F$ in
$bd(\Sigma')$.

## 33. Proof of (7.3), page 31 of the preprint: $L \cap G'$ is an exhaustive $p$-linkage

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The proof of (7.3) says that "$L \cap G'$ is a vital linkage in $G'$, by (2.3),
and it is a $p$-linkage since each component of $L$ includes at most one component of $L \cap G'$,
and it is exhaustive." Why each component of $L$ includes at most one component of $L \cap G'$, why
this bounds the number of terminals, and why $L \cap G'$ is exhaustive, are not shown.

**Proof.** We give the details. Let $Z = V(\Gamma) \cap bd(\Sigma)$; then
$Z \subseteq V(H_1 \cup \dots \cup H_r)$ by (V1). Let $W$ be the set of vertices
$w \in V(\Gamma) \setminus Z$ such that some terminal $t \in V(\Gamma) \setminus Z$ of $L$ is joined
to $w$ by a path of $(L \cap \Gamma) \setminus Z$. A vertex of $\Gamma$ not in $Z$ is in no $H_j$,
so all its edges in $L$ are in $\Gamma$, by (V1). We claim that
$\Gamma' = (L \cap \Gamma) \setminus W$. For let $Q$ be a path of $L \cap \Gamma$ with both ends in
$Z$ and no internal vertex in $Z$. Its internal vertices have degree $2$ in $L$, and so
$Q \setminus Z$ is a component of $(L \cap \Gamma) \setminus Z$ that contains no terminal of $L$;
hence $Q$ has no vertex in $W$. Conversely, let $x \in V(\Gamma) \setminus (Z \cup W)$, and let $P$
be the component of $L$ that contains $x$. Then $x$ is not a terminal of $L$, since $x \notin W$;
and following $P$ from $x$ in each direction, every vertex reached before the first vertex in $Z$ is
joined to $x$ in $(L \cap \Gamma) \setminus Z$, and so is not a terminal of $L$, and has both its
edges of $L$ in $\Gamma$. Hence both directions reach $Z$, and give a path of $L \cap \Gamma$
through $x$, containing both edges of $L$ at $x$, with both ends in $Z$ and no internal vertex in
$Z$. An edge of $L \cap \Gamma$ with both ends in $Z$ is itself such a path. This proves our claim.
Since $W \subseteq V(\Gamma) \setminus Z$ is disjoint from $H_1 \cup \dots \cup H_r$, it follows
that $L \cap G' = L \setminus W$.

Let $x, y \notin W$ be vertices of the same component $P$ of $L$, and suppose that some vertex $z$
of $P$ between $x$ and $y$ is in $W$. Then $z$ is joined by a path $R$ of
$(L \cap \Gamma) \setminus Z$ to a terminal $t$ of $L$; so $R$ is the subpath of $P$ between $z$ and
the end $t$ of $P$, and it passes through $x$ or $y$, which is then in $W$, a contradiction. Hence
the subpath of $P$ between $x$ and $y$ is a path of $L \setminus W$; that is, each component of $L$
includes at most one component of $L \setminus W$. So the terminals of $L \setminus W$ in a
component $P$ of $L$ lie in one path of $L \setminus W$, and there are at most two of them, and at
most one if $|V(P)| = 1$; and $P$ has two terminals of $L$ if $|V(P)| \ge 2$. Hence $L \cap G'$ has

terminal of $L$, and $x$ is incident with two edges of $L$, both in $\Gamma$. If the other end $y$

$(L \cap \Gamma) \setminus Z$, giving $x \in W$. So both edges are in $L \setminus W$, and $x$ is
not a terminal of $L \setminus W$, a contradiction.

## 47. Claim (2) in the proof of (11.1), page 45 of the preprint: $G/e$ is an intertwining

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** In the proof of Claim (2), the paper says "The graph $G/e$ ... is an
intertwining of $L_1/e$ and $L_2$, if we regard $L_2$ as a subgraph of $G/e$ in the obvious way".

the patterns of $L_1/e$ and $L_2$. The paper does not show this, and it needs that every such
linkage $L'_1$ comes from a linkage of $G$ with the pattern of $L_1$.

$G \setminus e$ by identifying $v$ with $u$. For a subgraph $K$ of $G$ with $v \notin V(K)$ or
$e \in E(K)$, let $K/e$ be the corresponding subgraph of $G/e$. Then $L_2$, which contains neither
$v$ nor $e$, is a linkage of $G/e$ with the same pattern, and $L_1/e$ is a linkage of $G/e$ whose
pattern is obtained from that of $L_1$ by replacing $v$ by $u$, and which has no more terminals than
$L_1$. We show that

(\*) *Let $L \in \{L_1, L_2\}$, and let $M$ be a linkage of $G/e$ whose pattern is obtained from the
pattern of $L$ by replacing $v$ by $u$. Then $M = K/e$ for some linkage $K$ of $G$ with the same
pattern as $L$.*

This implies that $G/e$ is an intertwining of $L_1/e$ and $L_2$, for if $M_1, M_2$ are linkages of
$G/e$ with the patterns of $L_1/e, L_2$, then the corresponding $K_1, K_2$ have the patterns of
$L_1, L_2$, so $K_1 \cup K_2 = G$, and $M_1 \cup M_2 = (K_1 \cup K_2)/e = G/e$. To prove (\*), note
first that if $u$ and $v$ are both terminals of $L$, then $\{u, v\}$ is a block of its pattern,
since then $L = L_1$ and $e \in E(L_1)$. No edge of $M$ is incident in $G$ with both $u$ and $v$,
since it would be a loop of $M$. Let $a$ and $b$ be the numbers of edges of $M$ incident with $u$ in
$G/e$ that are incident in $G$ with $u$ and with $v$ respectively; so $u$ has degree $a + b \le 2$
in $M$, and $u$ is a terminal of $M$ if and only if $u$ or $v$ is a terminal of $L$. There are three
ways to choose $K$:

(A) if $b = 0$, let $K = M$;

(B) if $a = 0$, let $K$ be $M$ with $u$ renamed $v$;

(C) if $u \in V(M)$ and $a, b \le 1$, let $K$ be $M$ with $v$ and $e$ added.

In each case $K$ is a linkage of $G$ with $K/e = M$ (in (C), $u$ and $v$ have degree $a + 1$ and
$b + 1$ in $K$, and a circuit of $K$ would give one of $M$), and the pattern of $K$ becomes that of
$M$ when $v$ is replaced by $u$. So the patterns of $K$ and $L$ agree after this replacement, and so
they are equal if neither $K$ nor $L$ has $v$ as a terminal, or neither has $u$ as a terminal, or
both have $\{u, v\}$ as a block, since in each of these cases the replacement is one-to-one on the
blocks involved. We choose $K$ so that one of these holds.

Suppose first that $b = 0$. If $v$ is not a terminal of $L$, we use (A), and then $v \notin V(K)$.
If $u$ and $v$ are both terminals of $L$, then $\{u, v\}$ is a block of $L$, so $\{u\}$ is a block
of $M$ and $a = 0$; we use (C), and then $\{u, v\}$ is a block of $K$. If $v$ is a terminal of $L$
and $u$ is not, then $u$ is a terminal of $M$, so $a \le 1$; we use (B) if $a = 0$ and (C) if
$a = 1$, and in both cases $u$ is not a terminal of $K$. Now suppose that $b \ge 1$. If $a = 0$, and
either $b = 2$ or $v$ is a terminal of $L$ and $u$ is not, we use (B); then $u \notin V(K)$, and $u$

$b = 1$ because otherwise $u$ would also be a terminal of $L$, and $\{u, v\}$ would be a block of
$L$ and $\{u\}$ a block of $M$, contrary to $b = 1$. This proves (\*).

## 59. Proof of (12.3), page 51 of the preprint: the bound $|N(T)| \le 2^n$

## 62. Proof of (12.4), page 53 of the preprint: the full proof

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "We only sketch the proof, since we shall not use the result."
The sketch uses a variation of (11.2) for path-decompositions that the paper does not prove, an
analogue of Claim (2) in the proof of (12.3) that it does not write out, and it says that the graph
of each component of $P \setminus B$ without $Z$ is a $(w + 1)$-chain "since no vertex of $C$ is

form, after the sketch.

**Proof.** We give the proof in full. It follows the sketch, but with the linked tree-decompositions
used in the proof of (12.3) in place of a linked path-decomposition, so that no variation of (11.2)
is needed.

Choose $w'$ so that (1.1) holds with $w$ replaced by $w'$, choose $n$ so that (2.6) holds with $k$
replaced by $w' + 1$, and let $p' = p + 12 \cdot 2^n (w' + 1)$. Let $G$ have a vital $p$-linkage
$L$. If $E(G) = \emptyset$, every vertex of $G$ is a terminal of $L$, so $|V(G)| \le p$, and $G$ is
a $p$-chain, with both sets equal to $V(G)$ and the one-vertex paths; so we assume that
$E(G) \ne \emptyset$. For a tree-decomposition $(T, W)$ of $G$, we say that $t \in V(T)$ is *good*
if $t$ has exactly two neighbours $u, u'$, and, with $S = V(W(t) \cap W(u))$ and
$S' = V(W(t) \cap W(u'))$, either (a) $S = S' = V(W(t))$, or (b) every vertex of $W(t)$ is in
$S \cup S'$, $W(t)$ has exactly one edge, with distinct ends $v, v'$, and $S = X \cup \{v\}$,
$S' = X \cup \{v'\}$ where $v, v' \notin X$. Other vertices are *bad*. By (1.1) and (11.2), $G$ has
a linked tree-decomposition $(T, W)$ with width $\le w'$ in which every vertex of $T$ has degree
$\le 3$; choose one with as few bad vertices as possible. As in the proof of (12.3), we may assume
that no edge of $G$ belongs to two of the graphs $W(t)$. Indeed, delete each edge of $G$ from all
but one of the graphs $W(t)$ that contain it. This changes no vertex set, so the new
tree-decomposition is still linked, of width $\le w'$, with the same sets $V(W(t) \cap W(u))$. A
good vertex $t$ stays good. In case (a) this holds since its sets $S, S'$ do not change. In case
(b), let the edge of $W(t)$ have ends $v, v'$. If it were in $W(u)$ for some $u \ne t$, both ends
would be in the set that $W(t)$ shares with the neighbour of $t$ on the path of $T$ to $u$, that is,
both in $S$ or both in $S'$; but $v \notin S'$ and $v' \notin S$. So the edge is in no other graph
$W(u)$, and it stays in $W(t)$. Hence the new tree-decomposition still has as few bad vertices as
possible.

(1) *Claim (1) of the proof of (12.3) holds with $N(T)$ replaced by the set of bad vertices.*

*Subproof.* Let $(P, W')$ and $(T^*, W^*)$ be as in that proof; by (12.2), $(T^*, W^*)$ is a linked
tree-decomposition of width $\le w'$ whose vertices have degree $\le 3$. Each vertex of $P$ other
than $s_1, s_2$ is good in $(T^*, W^*)$, by (12.1)(ii), (iii) and the property of $(P, W')$ given
there, since the sets it shares with its two neighbours are the sets $W'(e)$ of its two edges. A
good vertex of $T_1 \cup T_2$ stays good, since it keeps its graph and the sets it shares with its
neighbours (for $s_i$, the neighbour in $T_0$ is replaced by its neighbour in $P$, and the shared
set is $W(f_i)$ in both cases). So every bad vertex of $T^*$ is a bad vertex of $T$ in
$T_1 \cup T_2$, and by the choice of $(T, W)$, no vertex of $T_0$ is bad. This proves (1).

(2) *Every path of $T$ has at most $n$ bad vertices.*

*Subproof.* This is the argument of Claim (2) in the proof of (12.3), with the bad vertices in place
of $N(T)$ and (1) in place of Claim (1) there. Suppose not; then there is a path $Q$ of $T$ with
both ends bad and with exactly $n + 1$ bad vertices. Let them be $t_0, t_1, \dots, t_n$, in order on
$Q$. For $1 \le i \le n$, let $f_i$ be an edge of $Q$ between $t_{i-1}$ and $t_i$ such that
$|W(f_i)|$ is minimum among all such edges; let $S_i, T_i$ be the two components of
$T \setminus f_i$ where $t_0 \in V(S_i)$, and let $A_i = W(S_i)$ and $B_i = W(T_i)$. Then
$(A_i, B_i)$ is a separation of $G$ of order $|W(f_i)| \le w' + 1$, and for $1 \le i < j \le n$,
$A_i \subseteq A_j$ and $B_j \subseteq B_i$. Suppose that $1 \le i < i' \le n$, that
$|W(f_i)| = |W(f_{i'})| = k$ say, and that $|W(f_j)| > k$ for $i < j < i'$. By the choice of the
edges $f_j$, $|W(e)| \ge k$ for every edge $e$ of the path of $T$ between $f_i$ and $f_{i'}$. Since
$(T, W)$ is linked, there are $k$ mutually vertex-disjoint paths of $G$ between $W(f_i)$ and
$W(f_{i'})$, and so there is a linkage $M_{ii'}$ in $B_i \cap A_{i'}$ with $k$ components, each with
one end in $V(A_i \cap B_i)$ and the other in $V(A_{i'} \cap B_{i'})$. The bad vertex $t_i$ is
between $f_i$ and $f_{i'}$, so by (1), either $V(M_{ii'}) \ne V(B_i \cap A_{i'})$, or there is more
than one choice for $M_{ii'}$. In either case we may choose $M_{ii'}$ so that
$M_{ii'} \ne L \cap B_i \cap A_{i'}$, since $V(L \cap B_i \cap A_{i'}) = V(B_i \cap A_{i'})$. But
this contradicts (2.6), since each $(A_j, B_j)$ has order $\le w' + 1$. This proves (2).

Let $N$ be the set of bad vertices. A vertex of $T$ that is not in $N$ is good, and so it has
exactly two neighbours. Since $T$ is a finite tree, it has a vertex of degree $\le 1$, which is bad,
and so $N \ne \emptyset$.

(3) *$|N| \le 3 \cdot 2^n$.*

*Subproof.* For adjacent $u, v \in V(T)$, let the *branch* at $u$ through $v$ be the set of vertices
$t$ of $T$ such that the path of $T$ between $u$ and $t$ contains $v$. We show by induction on
$j \ge 0$ that if every path of $T$ with first vertex $u$ and second vertex $v$ has at most $j$
vertices in $N$ other than $u$, then the branch at $u$ through $v$ contains at most $2^j - 1$
members of $N$. We may assume that the branch contains a member of $N$; let $y$ be one for which the
path $R$ of $T$ between $u$ and $y$ is shortest. Then $j \ge 1$, since $R$ has first vertex $u$ and
second vertex $v$. The vertices of $R$ other than $u, y$ are not in $N$, and so have degree $2$ in
$T$. Hence every other member of $N$ in the branch is in the branch at $y$ through a neighbour of
$y$ not in $R$, and $y$ has at most two such neighbours, since $T$ has maximum degree $\le 3$. For
each such neighbour $y'$, every path with first vertex $y$ and second vertex $y'$ has at most
$j - 1$ vertices in $N$ other than $y$, since adding $R$ to it gives a path with first vertex $u$
and second vertex $v$, in which $y$ and these vertices are in $N$. By induction, each of these
branches contains at most $2^{j-1} - 1$ members of $N$, and so the branch at $u$ through $v$
contains at most $1 + 2(2^{j-1} - 1) = 2^j - 1$ of them. Now choose $r \in N$. Every vertex of $T$
other than $r$ is in the branch at $r$ through one of the at most three neighbours $v$ of $r$, and
by (2), every path with first vertex $r$ and second vertex $v$ has at most $n$ vertices in $N$. So
each of these branches contains at most $2^n - 1$ members of $N$, and
$|N| \le 1 + 3(2^n - 1) \le 3 \cdot 2^n$. This proves (3).

Let $Z = \bigcup (V(W(t)) : t \in N)$. Since $(T, W)$ has width $\le w'$, $|Z| \le |N|(w' + 1)$.

Let $C$ be a component of $T \setminus N$. Its vertices have degree $2$ in $T$, so $C$ is a path
$t_1 \dots t_m$, and $T$ has edges $t_0 t_1$ and $t_m t_{m+1}$ with $t_0, t_{m+1} \in N$. Let
$K_C = \bigcup (W(t_j) : 1 \le j \le m)$, $S = V(W(t_0) \cap W(t_1))$ and
$S' = V(W(t_m) \cap W(t_{m+1}))$. The edges $t_j t_{j+1}$ ($0 \le j \le m$) give separations of $G$
as in (12.1), and since $t_1, \dots, t_m$ are good, the path-decomposition of $G$ with graphs
$W(R), W(t_1), \dots, W(t_m), W(R')$, where $R$ and $R'$ are the components of
$T \setminus \{t_0 t_1, t_m t_{m+1}\}$ that contain $t_0$ and $t_{m+1}$, satisfies (12.1)(i)--(iii)
and the condition added to the converse of (12.1) (in case (a) the graph $W(t_j)$ may have any
number of edges, but all of them have both ends in the set it shares with its neighbours, and this
does not affect the proof of the converse). By the converse of (12.1), there is a unique set of
$|S|$ mutually vertex-disjoint paths of $K_C$ between $S$ and $S'$, and every vertex of $K_C$ is on
one of them. A vertex of $K_C$ in $Z$ is in $S \cup S'$, since the path of $T$ from $t_j$ to a
vertex of $N$ uses $t_1 t_0$ or $t_m t_{m+1}$. So $K_C \setminus Z = K_C \setminus (S \cup S')$, and
this is a $(w' + 1)$-chain, formed by the same paths without their ends; for another such set of
paths would extend to another set in $K_C$.

The graphs $K_C \setminus Z$ for the components $C$ of $T \setminus N$ cover $V(G) \setminus Z$, are
mutually vertex-disjoint (a vertex in graphs of two components is in a graph at a bad vertex between
them), and no edge of $G \setminus Z$ joins two of them (each edge is in one graph $W(t)$). There
are at most $3|N|$ of them, since each $C$ contains a neighbour of a bad vertex. Taking the union of
the sets of paths, $G \setminus Z$ is a $3|N|(w' + 1)$-chain (a path of $G \setminus Z$ stays in one
of these graphs), and adding the vertices of $Z$ to both sets of this chain, as one-vertex paths,
$G$ is a $(3|N|(w' + 1) + |Z|)$-chain. (In a set of paths between the enlarged sets, a vertex of $Z$
is the first vertex of one path and the last of another, which are then the same one-vertex path.)
Since $3|N|(w' + 1) + |Z| \le 12 \cdot 2^n (w' + 1) \le p'$, $G$ is a $p'$-chain.
