**60** (1994), 72–106.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is

## 1. Section 3, page 75: radial drawings

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After the definition of a radial drawing, the paper states that every 2-cell
painting or drawing $\Gamma$ has a radial drawing $\Gamma'$, that $\Gamma'$ is 2-cell, that every
region of $\Gamma'$ includes a unique edge of $\Gamma$ (unless $|V(\Gamma)| = 1$ and
$E(\Gamma) = \emptyset$), and that a radial drawing is unique up to automorphisms of $\Sigma$ which
fix $U(\Gamma)$ pointwise. It says "We omit the proofs of these assertions, which are elementary but
rather tedious." The copy gives the proofs of the first three assertions. The uniqueness is not
used in the formalization, and its proof is still omitted.

**Proof.** First let $\Gamma$ be a drawing. At a vertex $x$ of a drawing, the edges at $x$ (a loop
counted twice) leave $x$ in a cyclic order. We call these ends of edges the *half-edges* at $x$, and
the parts of a small neighbourhood of $x$ between two consecutive half-edges the *corners* at $x$.
Each corner is connected and misses the drawing, and so it lies in a region of the drawing. Its two
*sides* are the half-edges that bound it. If $x$ is in the closure of a region, then some corner at
$x$ lies in this region, since near $x$ the points not in the drawing are in the corners. We use
several times that a connected set that misses the boundary of a closed disc lies in its interior or
outside it.

For the existence, choose a point in each region of $\Gamma$. These points and the vertices of
$\Gamma$, with no edges, form a drawing that satisfies (i)–(iv). We show below that every drawing
that satisfies (i)–(iv) has at most $|V(\Gamma)| + 2|E(\Gamma)|$ edges. Then some drawing $\Gamma'$
has the most edges among those that satisfy (i)–(iv), and it satisfies (v). For let $\Gamma''$
satisfy (i)–(iv) and contain $\Gamma'$. By (ii) and (iii), $\Gamma''$ has the same vertices as
$\Gamma'$, and it has no more edges, and so $\Gamma'' = \Gamma'$.

For the bound, let $\Gamma'$ satisfy (i)–(iv), and let $H$ be the drawing formed by $\Gamma$ and
$\Gamma'$, which is a drawing by (i). By (i), each edge of $\Gamma'$ misses $U(\Gamma)$, and so it
lies in a region $r$ of $\Gamma$. By (iii) it joins a vertex of $\Gamma$ to a vertex of $\Gamma'$
not in $V(\Gamma)$, which is in the closure of $r$ but not in $U(\Gamma)$, and so is $v_r$ by (ii).
In particular each edge of $\Gamma'$ has just one half-edge at a vertex of $\Gamma$. Let
$x \in V(\Gamma)$, suppose that there are at least two half-edges of $H$ at $x$, and suppose that
both sides of a corner at $x$ are half-edges of $\Gamma'$. The corner lies in a region $r$ of
$\Gamma$, and its sides belong to two different edges $f, g$ of $\Gamma'$ that join $x$ to $v_r$.
Choose points of $f$ and of $g$ near $x$, and join them by an arc through the corner. With the rest
of $f$ and $g$, this arc forms an $O$-arc in $r$, which bounds a closed disc in $r$, since $r$ is an
open disc. The part of the corner cut off by the arc contains $x$, which is not in $r$, and so it
lies outside this disc. Together they form a closed disc in $r \cup \{x\}$ bounded by
$\bar{f} \cup \bar{g}$, contrary to (iv). So the half-edge that follows a half-edge of $\Gamma'$ in
the cyclic order at $x$ is a half-edge of $\Gamma$. Map each edge of $\Gamma'$ to the half-edge that
follows its half-edge at its end $x$ in $V(\Gamma)$, or to $x$ itself if there is only one half-edge
at $x$. This map is one-to-one, since the map taking each half-edge at $x$ to the next one is
one-to-one, and since at most one edge of $\Gamma'$ is at $x$ if there is only one half-edge at $x$.
Its values are among the vertices and the $2|E(\Gamma)|$ half-edges of $\Gamma$, which gives the
bound.

Now let $\Gamma'$ be a radial drawing of $\Gamma$, and let $H$ be as before. Condition (v) is used
in the following way. Let $r$ be a region of $\Gamma$, let $x$ be a vertex of $\Gamma$, and let $K$
be a line in $r \cup \{x\}$ with ends $v_r$ and $x$ that meets $U(\Gamma')$ only in its ends. Then
$\Gamma'$ with $K$ added as an edge satisfies (i)–(iii), and so by (v) it fails (iv). That is, $K$
and the closure of some edge $h$ of $\Gamma'$ with ends $x$ and $v_r$ bound a closed disc in
$r \cup \{x\}$. The interior of this disc contains no point of $U(H)$. For the interior lies in $r$,
and so it misses $U(\Gamma)$, and it contains no vertex of $\Gamma'$, since the only vertices of
$\Gamma'$ in $r \cup \{x\}$ are $v_r$ and $x$. Let $g$ be an edge of $\Gamma'$ that meets the
interior. It misses the boundary of the disc, since it misses $K$ and $h$, and so it lies in the
interior, and its ends are vertices of $\Gamma'$ in the disc, which are $x$ and $v_r$. Then $g$
splits the disc into two closed discs, and one of them is bounded by $\bar{g} \cup \bar{h}$ and lies
in $r \cup \{x\}$, contrary to (iv).

Now suppose that $\Gamma$ has an edge. Every vertex $x$ of $\Gamma$ is an end of an edge of
$\Gamma$. For otherwise a small closed disc around $x$ meets $U(\Gamma)$ only in $x$. Its boundary
bounds a closed disc in the region of $\Gamma$ that contains it, since this region is an open disc.
These two closed discs have the same boundary and are different, since only one contains $x$, and so
their union is open and closed in $\Sigma$, and it is $\Sigma$, since $\Sigma$ is connected. So
$U(\Gamma) = \{x\}$, a contradiction. Also the closure of each region $r$ of $\Gamma$ contains a
vertex of $\Gamma$. For $r \ne \Sigma$, since $U(\Gamma) \ne \emptyset$, and so the frontier of $r$
is not empty, since $\Sigma$ is connected. It lies in $U(\Gamma)$, and it contains a vertex, or a
point of an edge and then also the ends of this edge. We obtain the following facts, in order.

(a) *For each region $r$ of $\Gamma$ and each vertex $x$ of $\Gamma$ in the closure of $r$, some
edge of $\Gamma'$ joins $x$ to $v_r$.* For $r - U(H)$ is the union of the finitely many regions of
$H$ inside $r$, and it is dense in $r$, since $U(H) \cap r$ consists of $v_r$ and edges of
$\Gamma'$. So $x$ is in the closure of a region $p$ of $H$ inside $r$. Also $v_r$ is in the closure
of every region $q$ of $H$ inside $r$. For $q$ is open and non-empty, and $q \ne r$, since
$v_r \in r - q$. Since $r$ is connected, it follows that the frontier of $q$ meets $r$. A point of
this frontier in $r$ is in $U(\Gamma') \cap r$, which consists of $v_r$ and edges of $\Gamma'$ that
have $v_r$ in their closure, and if it is in such an edge then the whole edge, and so $v_r$, is in
the closure of $q$. Hence some corner at $x$ and some corner at $v_r$ lie in $p$. Since $p$ is open
and connected, there is a line $K$ as above, which runs from $v_r$ through a corner in $p$, then
inside $p$, and reaches $x$ through a corner in $p$. Then $h$ joins $x$ to $v_r$.

(b) *At each vertex $x$ of $\Gamma$, each corner has one side in $\Gamma$ and the other in
$\Gamma'$.* We saw in the proof of the bound that the two sides of a corner are not both in
$\Gamma'$, if there are at least two half-edges at $x$. If there is only one, it is a half-edge of
$\Gamma$, since $x$ is an end of an edge of $\Gamma$. So suppose that both sides of a corner at $x$
are half-edges of $\Gamma$ (the same one, if it is the only half-edge at $x$). The corner lies in a
region $p$ of $H$, inside a region $r$ of $\Gamma$, and as in (a) there is a line $K$ as above, with
all points other than its ends in $p$, that reaches $x$ inside this corner. Near $x$, the closed
disc bounded by $K$ and $\bar{h}$ lies on one side of $K$, between $K$ and $h$ in the cyclic order.
Since its interior misses $U(H)$, no half-edge of $H$ at $x$ lies strictly between $K$ and $h$ on
this side. So the half-edge of $h$ at $x$ is next to $K$, and it is one of the two sides of the
corner, which are half-edges of $\Gamma$, a contradiction.

(c) *For each region $r$ of $\Gamma$, different corners at $v_r$ lie in different regions of $H$.*
For suppose that two of them lie in one region $p$ of $H$. Since $p$ is open and connected, there is
an $O$-arc $J$ with $J - \{v_r\} \subseteq p$ that leaves $v_r$ through one of these corners and
returns through the other. Since $J \subseteq r$ and $r$ is an open disc, $J$ bounds a closed disc
in $r$. No edge of $\Gamma'$ meets the interior of this disc. For such an edge misses $J$, so it
would lie in the disc with both its ends, while each edge of $\Gamma'$ has an end in $V(\Gamma)$,
outside $r$. But near $v_r$ the disc lies on one side of $J$, and since the two corners are
different, each side of $J$ near $v_r$ contains a half-edge of $\Gamma'$ at $v_r$, a contradiction.

(d) *For each region $p$ of $H$, one or two edges of $\Gamma'$ are in the closure of $p$, and if
two, each of them has different regions of $H$ on its two sides.* Let $r$ be the region of $\Gamma$
that includes $p$. As in (a), $v_r$ is in the closure of $p$, and by (a), $v_r$ is an end of an edge
of $\Gamma'$, since the closure of $r$ contains a vertex of $\Gamma$. So some corner at $v_r$ lies
in $p$, and by (c) just one does. Each edge of $\Gamma'$ in the closure of $p$ has $v_r$ as an end,
and the points of $p$ near it close to $v_r$ lie in a corner beside it, which is then the corner in
$p$. So each such edge is a side of this corner. If there are two of them, then the two corners
beside one of them at $v_r$ are different, and so by (c) they lie in different regions of $H$.

(e) *The closure of each region $p$ of $H$ includes exactly one edge of $\Gamma$, and contains no
vertex of $\Gamma$ other than the ends of this edge.* Let a half-edge of an edge of $\Gamma$ in the
closure of $p$ be given. Some corner beside it lies in $p$, and by (b) the other side of this corner
is a half-edge of an edge of $\Gamma'$, which is in the closure of $p$. Each edge of $\Gamma'$ has
one half-edge at a vertex of $\Gamma$, and at most two half-edges are next to it there, one on each
side. If there are two edges of $\Gamma'$ in the closure of $p$, then by (d) only one side of each
of them lies in $p$. So in both cases of (d), at most two half-edges of $\Gamma$ are next to these
edges of $\Gamma'$ with the corner between them in $p$. An edge of $\Gamma$ in the closure of $p$
gives two of them, its two half-edges, and so there is at most one such edge. There is at least one,
since if $f$ is an edge of $\Gamma'$ in the closure of $p$, then at its end in $V(\Gamma)$ a corner
beside $f$ lies in $p$, and by (b) the other side of this corner is a half-edge of $\Gamma$.
Finally, if a vertex of $\Gamma$ is in the closure of $p$, then some corner at it lies in $p$, and
by (b) one side of this corner is a half-edge of $\Gamma$, whose edge is the edge of $\Gamma$ in the
closure of $p$.

(f) *Each region $s$ of $\Gamma'$ includes exactly one edge $e$ of $\Gamma$, and $s$ is the union of
$e$ and the regions of $H$ whose closure includes $e$.* This is the third assertion. Since
$V(\Gamma) \subseteq U(\Gamma')$, the set $s$ is the union of the edges of $\Gamma$ in $s$ and the
regions of $H$ in $s$. By (e), the closure of each of these regions includes exactly one edge of
$\Gamma$, and this edge lies in $s$, since it misses $U(\Gamma')$ and meets the closure of $s$. In
particular $s$ includes an edge of $\Gamma$. For each edge $f$ of $\Gamma$ in $s$, let $s_f$ be the
union of $f$ and the regions of $H$ whose closure includes $f$. It is open, since each point of $f$
has a neighbourhood that meets $U(H)$ only in $f$, and whose points off $f$ lie in regions of $H$
beside $f$. The sets $s_f$ cover $s$, and they are disjoint by (e). Since $s$ is connected, there is
only one of them.

(g) *$\Gamma'$ is 2-cell, whether or not $\Gamma$ has an edge.* First, for each region $r$ of
$\Gamma$, $v_r$ is an end of an edge of $\Gamma'$. If $\Gamma$ has an edge, this follows from (a),
since the closure of $r$ contains a vertex of $\Gamma$. Otherwise $V(\Gamma) \ne \emptyset$, since
$\Gamma$ is 2-cell and so not null. If no edge of $\Gamma'$ ends at $v_r$, then $U(\Gamma')$ meets
$r$ only in $v_r$, and a line $K$ as above, from $v_r$ to a vertex of $\Gamma$ on the frontier of
$r$ with its other points in $r$, gives an edge $h$ at $v_r$, a contradiction. Next, each region $p$
of $H$ is an open disc. For $p$ lies in a region $r$ of $\Gamma$, which is an open disc. An $O$-arc
$F$ in $p$ bounds a closed disc $\Delta$ in $r$. Each point of $U(\Gamma')$ in $r$ lies in the
closure of an edge of $\Gamma'$ with an end outside $r$. This closure is connected, misses $F$, and
has a point outside $\Delta$, and so it misses $\Delta$. Hence $\Delta$ misses $U(H)$, and so
$\Delta \subseteq p$. Thus $p$ is a connected open subset of the open disc $r$ in which every
$O$-arc bounds a closed disc, and so $p$ is an open disc, since a connected open subset of the plane
with this property is an open disc. If $\Gamma$ has no edge, then $H = \Gamma'$, and we are done.

Otherwise, each edge $e$ of $\Gamma$ has two different regions of $H$ beside it. For suppose that
the two corners beside $e$ at an end $x$ of $e$ lie in one region $p$ of $H$. By (b), their other
sides are half-edges of edges $f_1, f_2$ of $\Gamma'$, which are in the closure of $p$. Let $y$ be
the other end of $e$ ($y = x$ if $e$ is a loop). As in (e), the half-edge of $e$ at $y$ is next to
the half-edge of an edge $f$ of $\Gamma'$ in the closure of $p$, with the corner between them in
$p$. Suppose first that $f_1 \ne f_2$. Then by (d) these are the only edges of $\Gamma'$ in the
closure of $p$, and each has different regions of $H$ on its two sides. So $f$ is $f_1$ or $f_2$,
and since an edge of $\Gamma'$ has just one half-edge at a vertex of $\Gamma$, $y = x$, and $e$ is a
loop. But then the half-edge of $f$ at $x$ is next to both half-edges of $e$ at $x$, with the
corners between them in $p$, and so $p$ is on both sides of $f$, contrary to (d). Now suppose that
$f_1 = f_2$. Then $x$ has just two half-edges, one of $e$ and one of $f_1$, and $p$ is on both sides
of $f_1$. By (d), $f_1$ is the only edge of $\Gamma'$ in the closure of $p$, and so $f = f_1$, and
as before $y = x$. Then $e$ is a loop at $x$, and $x$ has three half-edges, a contradiction. By (f),
it follows that each region of $\Gamma'$ is the union of an edge $e$ of $\Gamma$ and the two regions
of $H$ beside $e$, which are disjoint open discs whose closures include $e$, and so it is an open
disc, since gluing two disjoint open discs along an edge in the frontier of both gives an open disc.
So $\Gamma'$ is 2-cell.

Now let $\Gamma$ be a painting. If the closure $\bar{e}$ of some edge $e$ of $\Gamma$ contains no
vertex, then $e$ is the only edge and $\Gamma$ has no vertex. For the sets $bd(\bar{f})$
$(f \in E(\Gamma))$, with $V(\Gamma)$ and one more point on each $bd(\bar{f})$ as vertices, form a
drawing whose regions are the regions of $\Gamma$ and the sets $\bar{f} - bd(\bar{f})$
$(f \in E(\Gamma))$, which are open discs. The point set of this drawing is connected. For the
frontier of an open disc in a surface is connected, and the frontier of each region is not empty,
since $\Sigma$ is connected. So if the point set were the union of two disjoint non-empty closed
sets $C_1, C_2$, the frontier of each region would lie in $C_1$ or in $C_2$, and adding to each
$C_i$ the closures of the regions whose frontier lies in $C_i$ would split $\Sigma$ into two
disjoint non-empty closed sets, which is impossible. But $bd(\bar{e})$ is closed and disjoint from
the rest of this point set, which is also closed, and so the rest is empty. In this case $\Sigma$ is
a sphere, since it is the union of the closed disc $\bar{e}$ and the region $\Sigma - \bar{e}$ of
$\Gamma$, which is an open disc. By (i) and (iii), $\Gamma'$ has no edge, since
$V(\Gamma) = \emptyset$, and so it has one vertex. So the only region of $\Gamma'$ is $\Sigma$ minus
a point, which is an open disc, and it includes $e$.

Otherwise let $D$ be the drawing formed by the sets $bd(\bar{e})$ $(e \in E(\Gamma))$, with vertex
set $V(\Gamma)$. Its regions are the regions of $\Gamma$ and the sets $\bar{e} - bd(\bar{e})$
$(e \in E(\Gamma))$, since these sets are connected, open and disjoint, and their union is
$\Sigma - U(D)$. So $D$ is 2-cell. Let $\Gamma'$ satisfy (i)–(iv) for $\Gamma$. For each edge $e$ of
$\Gamma$, add to $\Gamma'$ a new vertex in $\bar{e} - bd(\bar{e})$, joined by one edge inside
$\bar{e}$ to each vertex of $\Gamma$ in $\bar{e}$. This gives a drawing $G$ that satisfies (i)–(iv)
for $D$. For (i)–(iii) hold by the construction, and (iv) holds for the new edges since no two of
them have the same ends, and for the other edges since the regions of $\Gamma$ are regions of $D$.
Since $G$ has at least as many edges as $\Gamma'$, the bound for $D$ bounds the number of edges of
$\Gamma'$, and the existence follows as before.

Now let $\Gamma'$ be a radial drawing of $\Gamma$. Then $G$ is a radial drawing of $D$. For let
$G''$ satisfy (i)–(iv) for $D$ and contain $G$. The edges of $G''$ in the regions of $\Gamma$, with
the vertices of $\Gamma'$, form a drawing that satisfies (i)–(iv) for $\Gamma$ and contains
$\Gamma'$, and so by (v) they are the edges of $\Gamma'$. An edge of $G''$ in
$\bar{e} - bd(\bar{e})$ joins the new vertex in it to a vertex $x$ of $\Gamma$ in $\bar{e}$. If two
such edges had the same ends, they would form an $O$-arc in $\bar{e}$ meeting $bd(\bar{e})$ only in
$x$, which bounds a closed disc in $(\bar{e} - bd(\bar{e})) \cup \{x\}$, contrary to (iv). So
$G'' = G$. By the case of drawings, each region of $G$ includes a unique edge of $D$, and each
region of the drawing formed by $D$ and $G$ is an open disc. Let $s$ be a region of $\Gamma'$. Each
edge of $\Gamma$ that meets $s$ lies in $s$, since it misses $U(\Gamma')$ by (i), and each region of
$G$ that meets $s$ lies in $s$, since $U(\Gamma') \subseteq U(G)$. Since
$V(\Gamma) \subseteq U(\Gamma')$, the set $s$ is the union of the edges of $\Gamma$ in $s$ and the
regions of $G$ in $s$. For each edge $e$ of $\Gamma$ in $s$, the union of $e$ and the regions of $G$
that include an edge of $D$ in $e$ is open, as in (f). These sets cover $s$, and they are disjoint,
since each region of $G$ includes just one edge of $D$. Since $s$ is connected, there is only one of
them. So $s$ includes a unique edge $e$ of $\Gamma$. Moreover, each edge $a$ of $D$ in $e$ has two
regions of the drawing formed by $D$ and $G$ beside it, by (g) for $D$ and $G$. Each of these
regions lies inside $\bar{e}$ or misses $\bar{e}$, since it is connected and misses $bd(\bar{e})$.
One is inside and one is outside, since near a point of $a$ one side of $a$ is inside $\bar{e}$ and
the other is outside. The regions outside, for different edges $a$, are different, by (e) for $D$
and $G$. So $s$ is formed by the open disc $\bar{e} - bd(\bar{e})$ and, for each edge $a$ of $D$ in
$e$, the edge $a$ and the region beside it outside $\bar{e}$. Adding these one at a time keeps an
open disc, since each time we glue an open disc, disjoint from the set built so far, along the edge
$a$. So $\Gamma'$ is 2-cell.

## 2. Statement of (3.1), page 76: a local hypothesis on $X$ and $X \cap Y$

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (3.1) states that if $X, Y \subseteq \Sigma$ are closed, $X$ is
simply-connected, and $X \cap Y$ is non-empty and arc-wise connected, then every continuous
$\phi \colon S^1 \to X \cup Y$ is homotopic in $X \cup Y$ to a continuous $\phi' \colon S^1 \to Y$.
This is false for general closed sets. Work in a disc of $\Sigma$, with plane coordinates. Let $K$
be the comb $[0,1] \times \{0\} \cup \{0\} \times [0,1] \cup \bigcup_n \{1/n\} \times [0,1]$, let
$X = [0,1]^2$, let $B_m$ be the semicircle above the line $y = 1$ with ends $(1/(2m), 1)$ and
$(1/(2m+1), 1)$, and let $Y = K \cup \bigcup_m B_m$. Then $X$ and $Y$ are closed, $X$ is
simply-connected, and $X \cap Y = K$ is arc-wise connected. Let $\phi$ run along the top of the
square from $(1,1)$ to $(0,1)$, going over each $B_m$ in place of the segment under it, and then
back to $(1,1)$ inside $X$. It is continuous, because the $B_m$ shrink to the point $(0,1)$.

- Collapsing everything except the interior of $B_m$ to a point maps $X \cup Y$ onto a circle, and
  maps $\phi$ to a curve of degree $\pm 1$. So every curve homotopic to $\phi$ meets every $B_m$.
- In $Y$, two different semicircles $B_m$ are joined only through the teeth and the base of the
  comb, so a curve in $Y$ that passes through two of them goes down to the line $y = 0$ between
  them. A curve in $Y$ that meets every $B_m$ would do this infinitely often, which is impossible
  for a continuous map of $S^1$.

So $\phi$ is not homotopic into $Y$. The printed proof fails at the same point: when the intervals
$T_i$ are infinitely many, the curves $\phi'_i$ chosen for them one at a time need not fit together
into a continuous $\phi'$.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that every point $y \in X \cap Y$ has an open neighbourhood $A$
such that $X \cap A$ can be deformed in $X$ into $X \cap Y$ with the points of $X \cap Y \cap A$
kept fixed. With it, (3.1) holds, by another proof. The statement also holds when $X$ and $Y$ are
closed and nice with respect to a drawing in a closed surface. The paper applies (3.1) to sets built
from a drawing and closed discs bounded by its circuits, in the proofs of (5.4), (6.4), (7.8),
(7.11) and (8.5), and these sets are of this kind.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM11.theorem_3_1` in `GraphMinor/GM11/Results.lean`, with the added hypothesis
`GraphMinor.GM11.IsLocalDeformationRetract` in `GraphMinor/GM11/Section3.lean`, and
`GraphMinor.GM11.nice_31` in `GraphMinor/GM11/NicePush.lean` for nice sets.

## 3. Statement of (6.5), pages 91–92: order 2 and a loop

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (6.5) states that a respectful pretangle $\mathscr{T}$ in a 2-cell painting or
drawing $\Gamma$ is a tangle if and only if the slope $\mathrm{ins}$ in a radial drawing $\Gamma'$
derived from $\mathscr{T}$ is even. The "if" part fails for order 2. Let $\Gamma$ be the drawing in
the sphere with one vertex $v$ and one loop $e$, which divides the sphere into two discs. Its radial
drawing $\Gamma'$ joins $v$ to the vertex of $\Gamma'$ in each of the two discs. So $\Gamma'$ has no
circuit, and its only region, which contains $e$, has perimeter of length 4. For $\theta = 2$ there
is no circuit or region of length $< 2\theta$, so the slope of order 2 in $\Gamma'$ is even, since
there is nothing to check. The pretangle of order 2 derived from it is respectful by (6.1), and it
contains $(\Gamma \setminus e, K_e)$, which has order 1. But $\Gamma \setminus e$ is the vertex $v$
alone, so $V(\Gamma \setminus e) = V(\Gamma)$, against the third tangle axiom. The proof of the "if"

## 4. Statement of (6.6), pages 92–93: order 2 and a loop

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (6.6) gives a bijection between respectful tangles of order $\theta$ in a
2-cell drawing $\Gamma$ and in its dual $\Gamma^*$, and deduces that $\Gamma$ has a tangle of order
$\theta$ if and only if $\Gamma^*$ does. The argument before (6.6) applies (6.5) in both $\Gamma$
and $\Gamma^*$, so it fails in the case excluded by the correction to (6.5) above. Take $\Gamma$ as
there, with $\theta = 2$. Its dual $\Gamma^*$ has two vertices joined by one edge, and the radial
drawing $\Gamma'$ is common to both. The pretangle of order 2 derived from the even slope of
$\Gamma'$ is a tangle in $\Gamma^*$, but the corresponding pretangle in $\Gamma$ is not a tangle. In
fact $\Gamma$ has no tangle of order 2: the separation $(\Gamma \setminus e, K_e)$ has order 1, and
both its sides have vertex set $\{v\} = V(\Gamma)$, so neither side can be the first member of a
pair in a tangle, by the third tangle axiom. So the bijection fails, and so does the last sentence
of (6.6).

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that if $\theta = 2$, then neither $\Gamma$ nor $\Gamma^*$ has a
loop. With it, (6.5) applies in both drawings, and (6.6) holds.
