# Graph Minors II: corrections

N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. II. Algorithmic aspects of tree-width*, J. Algorithms
**7** (1986), 309–322.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM02/GM02.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM02/GM02.pdf) in the
repository [GraphMinorPapers](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers).

The corrections are in the order of the pages.

## 1. Statement of (2.3), page 311: $v'$, not $v$

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** (2.3) is printed "If $v, v \notin X_i$ and are not separated in $G$ by $X_i$,
then $T_i(v) = T_i(v')$." The conclusion is about two vertices $v$ and $v'$, but the hypothesis
names $v$ twice, so $v'$ is not bound.

**Correction.** The hypothesis reads "$v, v' \notin X_i$", as in (2.2) just before.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_3` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean`.

## 2. Proof of (2.5), page 313: $\cap$, not $\cup$

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** The proof has the display
$|(W - Y) \cap Q| = |W' \cup Q| = \frac12|Q - (Y \cup A)|$. As printed it is false whenever
$Q \ne \emptyset$, since $|W' \cup Q| \ge |Q| > \frac12|Q - (Y \cup A)|$.

**Correction.** The display reads $|(W - Y) \cap Q| = |W' \cap Q| = \frac12|Q - (Y \cup A)|$. The
line before chooses $Y$ with $|(W - Y) \cap Q| = |W' \cap Q|$, and since $(W - Y, W')$ is a
partition of $V(G) - (Y \cup A)$, the sets $(W - Y) \cap Q$ and $W' \cap Q$ partition
$Q - (Y \cup A)$. So each has $\frac12|Q - (Y \cup A)|$ elements. The rest of the proof is
unchanged.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_5_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM02/BalancedSeparator.lean`.

## 3. Proof of (2.7), page 314: tree-width of minors

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper calls (2.7), "If $G$ has tree-width $< w$, so does every minor of
$G$", an "elementary observation" and gives no proof.

**Proof.** We give the details. Let $(X_i : i \in I)$, $T$ be a tree-decomposition of $G$ with
$|X_i| \le w$ for all $i \in I$, and let $H$ be a minor of $G$. Then there are disjoint non-empty
sets $Y(v) \subseteq V(G)$ $(v \in V(H))$, each inducing a connected subgraph of $G$, and for each
edge $e$ of $H$ an edge $f(e)$ of $G$, such that if $e$ has ends $u, v$, then $f(e)$ has one end in
$Y(u)$ and the other in $Y(v)$. (Take for $Y(v)$ the set of vertices of $G$ that are contracted into
$v$, and for $f(e)$ the edge of $G$ that becomes $e$.) For $i \in I$ let $X'_i$ be the set of all
$v \in V(H)$ with $Y(v) \cap X_i \ne \emptyset$. Choosing for each $v \in X'_i$ a vertex in
$Y(v) \cap X_i$ gives distinct vertices for distinct $v$, since the sets $Y(v)$ are disjoint, and so
$|X'_i| \le |X_i| \le w$. We claim that $(X'_i : i \in I)$, $T$ is a tree-decomposition of $H$, and
this proves (2.7). For (W1), each $Y(v)$ is non-empty, and every vertex of $G$ is in some $X_i$. For
(W2), let $e$ be an edge of $H$ with ends $u, v$. Then $f(e)$ has ends $a \in Y(u)$ and
$b \in Y(v)$, and $a, b \in X_i$ for some $i$, by (W2) for $G$. Then $u, v \in X'_i$. For (W3), let
$j$ lie on the path of $T$ from $i$ to $k$, and let $v \in X'_i \cap X'_k$. Suppose that
$Y(v) \cap X_j = \emptyset$. We claim that for all $a, b \in Y(v)$ and all $i', k' \in I$ with
$a \in X_{i'}$ and $b \in X_{k'}$, there is a walk of $T$ from $i'$ to $k'$ that does not pass
through $j$. If $a = b$, then $j$ is not on the path of $T$ from $i'$ to $k'$, since otherwise
$a \in X_{i'} \cap X_{k'} \subseteq X_j$ by (W3) for $G$, and we take this path. Since $Y(v)$
induces a connected subgraph of $G$, it is enough now to show that if the claim holds for $a, b$,
and $c \in Y(v)$ is adjacent to $b$, then it holds for $a, c$. Let $a \in X_{i'}$ and
$c \in X_{k'}$. By (W2) for $G$, some $X_t$ contains $b$ and $c$. There is a walk from $i'$ to $t$
not through $j$, since the claim holds for $a, b$, and a walk from $t$ to $k'$ not through $j$, by
the case $a = b$ applied to $c$. Together they give a walk from $i'$ to $k'$ not through $j$. This
proves the claim. Now choose $a \in Y(v) \cap X_i$ and $b \in Y(v) \cap X_k$. By the claim there is
a walk of $T$ from $i$ to $k$ not through $j$. But every walk of the tree $T$ from $i$ to $k$ passes
through every vertex of the path of $T$ from $i$ to $k$, and in particular through $j$. This is a
contradiction, and so $Y(v) \cap X_j \ne \emptyset$, that is, $v \in X'_j$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_7` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_7_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM02/MinorTreeWidth.lean`. The sets $X'_i$ are
built in `GraphMinor.GM02.TreeWidthLE.of_isIsoMinor` and
`GraphMinor.GM02.TreeWidthLE.of_minorModel` in `GraphMinor/GM02/MinorWidth.lean`, and the proof of
(W3) is `GraphMinor.GM02.IsTreeDecomposition.inter_nonempty_of_connected` in the same file.

## 4. Proof of (2.8), page 315: the number of crossing schemes

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says of the bound $\max(2, (|X| + k)^{|X|})$ in (2.8): "The proof is
routine, and is left to the reader."

**Proof.** We give the details. As in (iv), where they are the edges of $F$, we regard the members
of a termination as unordered pairs. Let $n = |X|$, and let $(\mathscr{A}, \mathscr{A}', Z, Z')$ be
a crossing scheme. By (i), $S(\mathscr{A}) \subseteq X \cup Y$ and
$S(\mathscr{A}') \subseteq X \cup Y'$. So every vertex in $S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$ is
in $X$, and it is not in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, by (ii). Every vertex $v$ of $F$ is in
$S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ or in $S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$. For if $v$ is in just one of
$S(\mathscr{A}), S(\mathscr{A}')$, then $v \in S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, by (i) if $v \notin X$ and by (ii)
if $v \in X$. Also every vertex of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ is in exactly one of
$S(\mathscr{A}), S(\mathscr{A}')$, by (i) and (ii). Let $1 \le i \le k$. By (iv), the only vertices
of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ in $F_i$ are $s_i$ and $t_i$, since the other vertices of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$
are in other components of $F$. So every internal vertex of $F_i$ is in
$S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$, and therefore in $X - S(\mathscr{A}_0)$. Let $L_i$ be the
sequence of internal vertices of $F_i$, in order from $s_i$ to $t_i$. These are distinct vertices of
$X - S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, and no vertex is in two of $L_1, \dots, L_k$. Let $M$ be the set of vertices
of $X$ that are in $Z$, or in $S(\mathscr{A}_0) \cap S(\mathscr{A})$. No vertex of $M$ is in any
$L_i$, since the vertices of the sequences $L_i$ are in $S(\mathscr{A})$ and not in
$S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, while $Z$ is disjoint from $S(\mathscr{A})$ by (iii).

We claim that $L_1, \dots, L_k$ and $M$ determine the crossing scheme. First, $F_i$ is the path
$s_i, L_i, t_i$ for $1 \le i \le k$, and every edge of $F$ is in some $F_i$. So $F$ is determined.
Second, the vertices in the sequences $L_i$ are exactly the vertices of
$S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$. For each vertex of $S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$ is a
vertex of $F$ not in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, and so it is an internal vertex of some $F_i$. Third,
$S(\mathscr{A})$ consists of these vertices together with the vertices of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ in
$S(\mathscr{A})$, since every vertex of $F$ is in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ or in
$S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$. A vertex of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ not in $X$ is in
$S(\mathscr{A})$ exactly when it is in $Y$, by (i), and one in $X$ is in $S(\mathscr{A})$ exactly
when it is in $M$. So $S(\mathscr{A})$ is determined. Fourth, $S(\mathscr{A}')$ consists of the
vertices in the sequences $L_i$ together with the vertices of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ not in
$S(\mathscr{A})$, since each vertex of $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$ is in exactly one of
$S(\mathscr{A}), S(\mathscr{A}')$. Fifth, $Z = M - S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, and then $Z'$ is determined by
(iii). Finally, $\mathscr{A}$ is determined by $F$ and $S(\mathscr{A})$. For let $\mathscr{B}$ be
another set of disjoint pairs that are edges of $F$, with union $S(\mathscr{A})$. Each vertex of
$S(\mathscr{A})$ is in one member of $\mathscr{A}$ and one member of $\mathscr{B}$, and so every
vertex has valency 0 or 2 in the graph with edge set
$(\mathscr{A} - \mathscr{B}) \cup (\mathscr{B} - \mathscr{A})$. If this graph had an edge it would
have a circuit, which is impossible since it is a subgraph of the forest $F$. So
$\mathscr{B} = \mathscr{A}$. In the same way $\mathscr{A}'$ is determined by $F$ and
$S(\mathscr{A}')$. This proves the claim.

Call an *arrangement* on a set $W$ any $k$ sequences $L_1, \dots, L_k$ of distinct vertices of $W$,
no vertex in two of them, together with a set $M$ of vertices of $W$ in none of them. Let $w = |W|$.
We show by induction on $w$ that there are at most $\prod_{1 \le j \le w} (k+j+1)$ arrangements on
$W$. For $w = 0$ there is one. Let $w > 0$ and $x \in W$. Removing $x$ from an arrangement on $W$
gives an arrangement on $W - \{x\}$, and the arrangement on $W$ is determined by this and one of the
following: $x \in M$; $x$ is in no sequence and not in $M$; $x$ is first in $L_i$, for some $i$; $x$
comes just after $y$ in some sequence, for some $y \in W - \{x\}$. These are
$2 + k + w - 1 = k + w + 1$ possibilities, as required. Let $P_w = \prod_{1 \le j \le w} (k+j+1)$.
Then $P_w \le (k+w)^w$ for $w \ge 3$, by induction on $w$. For $w = 3$ this holds since
$(k+2)(k+4) \le (k+3)^2$. If it holds for some $w \ge 3$, then since $(k+w+2)(k+w) \le (k+w+1)^2$,
$$\begin{aligned}P_{w+1} = (k+w+2) P_w &\le (k+w+2)(k+w) \cdot (k+w)^{w-1}\\ &\le (k+w+1)^2 (k+w+1)^{w-1} = (k+w+1)^{w+1}.\end{aligned}$$
By the claim, the number of crossing schemes is at most the number of arrangements on $X$, which is
at most $P_n \le (n+k)^n$ if $n \ge 3$. This proves (2.8) when $n \ge 3$.

Now let $n \le 2$, and suppose first that some $a \in X$ is in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$. Then $a$ is in no
sequence $L_i$, and so the arrangement given by a crossing scheme is determined by the arrangement
on $X - \{a\}$ obtained by removing $a$, and by whether $a \in M$. There are at most $P_{n-1}$
arrangements on $X - \{a\}$, which is $1$ if $n = 1$ and $k+2$ if $n = 2$. So there are at most $2$
crossing schemes if $n = 1$, and at most $2(k+2) \le (k+2)^2$ if $n = 2$.

Next suppose that no vertex of $X$ is in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$. If $n = 0$, there is only one
arrangement on $X$, and so at most one crossing scheme. Let $n \ge 1$. For $1 \le i \le k$, every
member of $\mathscr{A}$ that meets $F_i$ is an edge of $F_i$, and so $|S(\mathscr{A}) \cap V(F_i)|$
is even. This set consists of the vertices of $L_i$ and those of $s_i, t_i$ that are in
$S(\mathscr{A})$. Since $s_i, t_i \notin X$, by (i) these are the ones in $Y$. So
$|L_i| + |\{s_i, t_i\} \cap Y|$ is even, and the parity of $|L_i|$ is the same for all crossing
schemes. Suppose that $|L_i|$ is odd for some $i$. Then $|L_i| = 1$, since $n \le 2$, and there are
at most $n$ choices for its vertex $x$. At most one other vertex $y$ of $X$ remains, and for
$j \ne i$, $L_j$ is the sequence $y$ if $|L_j|$ is odd, and is empty otherwise. So the sequences are
determined by $x$, and $M \subseteq \{y\}$ gives at most two choices for $M$. This gives at most
$2n$ crossing schemes, and $2n \le 2$ if $n = 1$, while $2n = 4 \le (k+2)^2$ if $n = 2$. Suppose
finally that every $|L_i|$ is even. If $n = 1$, all $L_i$ are empty and there are two choices for
$M$. If $n = 2$, either all $L_i$ are empty and there are four choices for $M$, or one $L_i$
consists of both vertices of $X$ and $M = \emptyset$, which gives $2k$ choices. So there are at most
$4 + 2k \le (k+2)^2$ crossing schemes. This proves (2.8).

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_8` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_8_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM02/CrossingSchemeCount.lean`. The sequences and

$S(\mathscr{A})$ by `GraphMinor.GM02.Termination.eq_of_support_in_paths` in

`GraphMinor.GM02.finite_arrangements_and_card_le` in `GraphMinor/GM02/ArrangementCount.lean`, and
the bound $P_w \le (k+w)^w$ is `GraphMinor.GM02.insertion_product_le_pow` in

## 5. Statement of (2.9), page 315: the induced terminated graphs

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (2.9) states that $(G, \mathscr{A}_0)$ is feasible if and only if there is a
crossing scheme $(\mathscr{A}, \mathscr{A}', Z, Z')$ for $\mathscr{A}_0$ with respect to $(H, H')$
such that both induced terminated graphs $(H \setminus Z, \mathscr{A})$ and
$(H' \setminus Z', \mathscr{A}')$ are feasible. The "if" direction is false, because a path of an
induced graph may pass through a vertex of $X$ that is a terminal of $\mathscr{A}_0$ used only by
the other side. Let $G$ be the star with centre $0$ and leaves $1, 2, 3$, and let
$\mathscr{A}_0 = \{(2, 3), (0, 1)\}$. Let $H$ and $H'$ both have all four vertices, with the edges
$02, 03$ in $H$ and the edge $01$ in $H'$. Then $X = V(G)$, and $\mathscr{A} = \{(2, 3)\}$,
$\mathscr{A}' = \{(0, 1)\}$, $Z = Z' = \emptyset$ is a crossing scheme: conditions (i) and (iii)
hold since $V(H) - V(H')$ and $V(H') - V(H)$ are empty and every vertex is a terminal, each vertex
is in exactly two of $S(\mathscr{A}_0), S(\mathscr{A}), S(\mathscr{A}')$, and the graph $F$ of (iv)
consists of the two one-edge paths $23$ and $01$. Both induced graphs are feasible, by the path
$2, 0, 3$ in $H$ and the edge $01$ in $H'$. But $(G, \mathscr{A}_0)$ is not feasible, since every
path from $2$ to $3$ passes through the terminal $0$ of the other pair.

**Correction.** Change the induced graphs. Let
$$\bar Z = Z \cup ((S(\mathscr{A}_0) \cap X) - S(\mathscr{A})), \qquad \bar Z' = Z' \cup ((S(\mathscr{A}_0) \cap X) - S(\mathscr{A}')),$$
and let the induced terminated graphs be $(H \setminus \bar Z, \mathscr{A})$ and
$(H' \setminus \bar Z', \mathscr{A}')$. So each side also loses the terminals of $\mathscr{A}_0$ in
$X$ that its new termination does not use. These vertices are not in $S(\mathscr{A})$, or not in
$S(\mathscr{A}')$, so $\mathscr{A}$ and $\mathscr{A}'$ are still terminations of the smaller graphs,
and the bound (2.10) on the index is unchanged. With this definition (2.9) holds.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_2_9` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean` proves the corrected
equivalence. `GraphMinor.GM02.FeasibilityCounterexample.counterexample_2_9` in
`GraphMinor/GM02/FeasibilityCounterexample.lean` proves that the example above refutes the printed
statement.

## 6. Proof of (2.9), page 315: feasibility through a crossing scheme

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says of (2.9): "This is clear once the various definitions have been
digested, and it is left to the reader." The proof below is for the corrected definition of the
induced terminated graphs, given in the correction just above.

**Proof.** We give the details, with $\bar Z, \bar Z'$ as above. Suppose first that
$(G, \mathscr{A}_0)$ is feasible, and let $P_1, \dots, P_k$ be as in the definition of feasibility.
Since $E(H) \cap E(H') = \emptyset$, each edge of $P_i$ is in exactly one of $H, H'$. Divide $P_i$
into maximal subpaths whose edges are all in $H$ or all in $H'$, and call them the *pieces* of
$P_i$, of the first or second kind. Each piece has at least one edge, since $s_i \ne t_i$. Two
consecutive pieces of $P_i$ are of different kinds, and they share one vertex, which is in $X$,
since it is incident with an edge of $H$ and an edge of $H'$. Let $\mathscr{A}$ be the set of pairs
of ends of the pieces of the first kind, and $\mathscr{A}'$ the same for the second kind. Two pieces
of the same kind are disjoint, and so $\mathscr{A}$ and $\mathscr{A}'$ are terminations. An internal
vertex of a piece is not an end of any piece, and not in $S(\mathscr{A}_0)$. Let $Z'$ be the set of
vertices of $X$ that are internal vertices of pieces of the first kind, and let $Z$ be the set of
vertices of $X - (S(\mathscr{A}_0) \cup S(\mathscr{A}) \cup S(\mathscr{A}'))$ not in $Z'$. We check
(i)–(iv). For (i), an end of a piece of the first kind is a vertex of $H$, and it is either in $X$
or an end of some $P_i$. Conversely, if $s_i \in Y$, then the edge of $P_i$ at $s_i$ is not in $H'$,
since $s_i \notin V(H')$, and so $s_i \in S(\mathscr{A})$. The same holds for $t_i$, and similarly
for $Y'$. For (ii), let $x \in X$. If $x$ is on no $P_i$, or is an internal vertex of a piece, it is
in none of the three supports. If $x$ is $s_i$ or $t_i$, it is an end of exactly one piece.
Otherwise $x$ is the common end of two consecutive pieces, of different kinds. In each case $x$ is
in two or none of $S(\mathscr{A}_0), S(\mathscr{A}), S(\mathscr{A}')$. Condition (iii) holds by the
definition of $Z$ and $Z'$. For (iv), the ends of the pieces of $P_i$, in order along $P_i$, form a
path $F_i$ of $F$ from $s_i$ to $t_i$, and the paths $F_1, \dots, F_k$ are disjoint and contain all
vertices and edges of $F$. Finally, each piece of the first kind is a path of $H$ that does not meet
$\bar Z$. For its ends are in $S(\mathscr{A})$, and its internal vertices are not in
$S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, and are in $Y$ or in $Z'$. Since these pieces are disjoint,
$(H \setminus \bar Z, \mathscr{A})$ is feasible, and similarly
$(H' \setminus \bar Z', \mathscr{A}')$ is feasible.

Conversely, let $(\mathscr{A}, \mathscr{A}', Z, Z')$ be a crossing scheme such that both induced
terminated graphs are feasible. Then for each pair $p \in \mathscr{A}$ there is a path $Q_p$ of
$H \setminus \bar Z$ joining the two vertices of $p$, and for each $p \in \mathscr{A}'$ a path $Q_p$
of $H' \setminus \bar Z'$, such that paths for different pairs of the same termination are disjoint.
(As in the proof of (2.8), no pair is in both $\mathscr{A}$ and $\mathscr{A}'$.) We claim that if
$p \in \mathscr{A}$ and $q \in \mathscr{A}'$, then $Q_p$ and $Q_q$ meet only in common vertices of
$p$ and $q$. For let $v$ be a vertex of both. Then $v \in X$, and $v \notin \bar Z \cup \bar Z'$. If
$v \in S(\mathscr{A}_0)$, then $v \in S(\mathscr{A}) \cap S(\mathscr{A}')$ by the definition of
$\bar Z, \bar Z'$, contrary to (ii). So $v \notin S(\mathscr{A}_0)$. Since $v \notin Z \cup Z'$, it
follows from (iii) that $v$ is in $S(\mathscr{A})$ or $S(\mathscr{A}')$, and then from (ii) that it
is in both. Hence $v$ is a vertex of some $p' \in \mathscr{A}$, and so $v \in Q_{p'}$, and $p' = p$
since the paths $Q_r$ $(r \in \mathscr{A})$ are disjoint. Similarly $v$ is a vertex of $q$, as
claimed. Now for $1 \le i \le k$, let $P_i$ be the union of the paths $Q_p$ for the edges $p$ of
$F_i$. Two consecutive edges of $F_i$ are in different terminations, since the pairs of a
termination are disjoint, and so their paths meet only in their common vertex. Two edges of $F$ that
are not consecutive edges of the same $F_i$ have no common vertex, and so their paths are disjoint.
Hence $P_i$ is a path of $G$ joining $s_i$ and $t_i$, and $P_1, \dots, P_k$ are pairwise disjoint.
Thus $(G, \mathscr{A}_0)$ is feasible.

## 7. Statement of (4.5), page 319: loops and parallel edges of $H$

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (4.5) states that $G$ topologically contains $H$ if and only if the terminated
graph $(G', \mathscr{A})$ built before (4.4) is feasible. In $G'$, two vertices are adjacent when
their images under $g$ are adjacent in $G$, so $G'$ does not record which edge of $G$ joins them,
and two paths of $G'$ may use the same edge of $G$. Two examples, in which $G$ has no loops:

- Let $V(G) = V(H) = \{u, v\}$, let $G$ have one edge $uv$, and let $H$ have two edges with ends
  $u, v$. Then $s_1t_1$ and $s_2t_2$ are disjoint edges of $G'$, so $(G', \mathscr{A})$ is feasible.
  But $G$ does not topologically contain $H$, since $G$ has only one edge.
- Let $V(G) = \{u, x\}$, let $G$ have one edge $ux$, and let $H$ be the vertex $u$ with one loop.
  The path $s_1, x, t_1$ of $G'$ shows that $(G', \mathscr{A})$ is feasible. But $G$ has no cycle,
  so it does not topologically contain $H$.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that $H$ has no loops and no two edges with the same ends. The
direction from containment to feasibility holds for every $H$. The new hypothesis is needed only for
the converse, where it makes the edges of $G$ used by different paths distinct. For the use of (4.5)
in (4.6), where $H$ may have loops and parallel edges, see the next correction.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_4_5` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean` (the corrected statement),
and `GraphMinor.GM02.splitFeasible_of_topologicallyContains` in
`GraphMinor/GM02/Section4FeasibleForward.lean` (the forward direction for every $H$). The two
examples are `GraphMinor.GM02.counterexample_4_5_parallel` and

## 8. Proof of (4.6), page 319: the construction of $G'$

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** The proof of (4.6) builds $(G', \mathscr{A})$ as for (4.4) and (4.5) and uses
(4.5) to decide whether $G$ topologically contains $H$. Here $H$ is isomorphic to a subgraph of
$H_0$, so it may have loops and parallel edges, and then (4.5) fails, by the correction to (4.5)
above.

**Correction.** Change the construction so that it records the edges of $G$. Put one new vertex on
each edge of $G$, and join each new terminal $s_i$ or $t_i$ only to the new vertex of one chosen
edge of $G$ at $u_i$ or $v_i$, the two ends of a loop of $H$ choosing different edges. Since $k$ is
fixed, there are polynomially many choices, and $G$ topologically contains $H$ if and only if the
terminated graph is feasible for some choice. If $G$ has tree-width at most $w$, each of these
graphs has tree-width at most $\max(w, 2) + 2k$. So (3.2) is applied, for each choice, with $w$
replaced by $\max(w, 2) + 2k$ instead of $w + 2k$, and alternative (iii) changes in the same way.
These two facts take the place of (4.5) and (4.4) in the rest of the proof, and (4.6) holds as

## 9. Proof of (4.8), page 320: splitting vertices

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper proves (4.8) in one sentence: "This is elementary. (We 'split' every
vertex of $H$ with valency $\ge 4$ into two adjacent vertices of valency $\ge 3$, repeatedly, in all
possible ways.)" It does not show that this gives finitely many graphs, or that a graph with a minor
isomorphic to $H$ topologically contains one of them.

**Proof.** We give the details. Valencies count a loop twice. Let $L$ be a graph with an edge $f$
whose ends $x, y$ are distinct and both have valency $\ge 3$, and let $K$ be obtained from $L$ by
contracting $f$. The new vertex of $K$ has valency equal to the sum of the valencies of $x$ and $y$
minus 2, which is at least 4, and we say that $L$ is obtained from $K$ by *splitting* it. Call a
graph a *split* of $H$ if it can be obtained from a graph isomorphic to $H$ by a sequence of such
steps. Each step adds one vertex and one edge. It raises the sum of the valencies by 2, and the sum
over all vertices $v$ of $\min(d(v), 3)$ by 3, where $d(v)$ is the valency of $v$. Since the second
sum is at most the first, a split of $H$ arises after at most $2|E(H)|$ steps, and so it has at most
$|V(H)| + 2|E(H)|$ vertices and at most $3|E(H)|$ edges. Hence there are only finitely many splits
of $H$ up to isomorphism. Let $H_1, \dots, H_r$ contain one of each.

If $G$ topologically contains a graph isomorphic to some $H_i$, then $G$ has a minor isomorphic to
$H_i$, as observed above, and $H_i$ has a minor isomorphic to $H$, obtained by contracting edges. So
$G$ has a minor isomorphic to $H$.

Conversely, suppose that $G$ has a minor isomorphic to $H$. Then $G$ has a subgraph $J$ with
vertex-disjoint subtrees $T_v$ $(v \in V(H))$ containing all vertices of $J$, such that the edges of
$J$ in no $T_v$ correspond one-to-one to the edges of $H$, an edge of $H$ with ends $u, v$
corresponding to an edge of $J$ with one end in $V(T_u)$ and the other in $V(T_v)$. (Take a subgraph
of $G$ contractible to a graph isomorphic to $H$, and keep a spanning tree of each set of vertices
that is contracted to one vertex, together with the edges that are not contracted.) Call the edges
of $J$ in no $T_v$ the *outer* edges, and their ends *marks*. We change $J$ in three steps, keeping
these properties, and keeping that $G$ topologically contains $J$.

First, replace each $T_v$ that contains a mark by the smallest subtree of $T_v$ containing all marks
in $T_v$, and each other $T_v$ by one of its vertices, and delete all other vertices of $J$. Now
every leaf of a tree $T_v$ with at least two vertices is a mark, and so every vertex of such a tree
has valency $\ge 2$ in $J$.

Second, while some vertex $x$ of a tree $T_v$ with at least two vertices has valency 2, suppress
$x$. This is possible, since $x$ is incident with an edge of $T_v$, and so with no loop. If both
edges at $x$ are in $T_v$, the new edge joins two vertices of $T_v$, and we add it to $T_v - x$,
which is then a tree. Otherwise $x$ is a leaf of $T_v$, the edges at $x$ are an edge of $T_v$ with
other end $z$ and an outer edge $e$, and the new edge joins $z$ to the other end of $e$. We let it
correspond to the same edge of $H$ as $e$, and replace $T_v$ by $T_v - x$. In both cases the
valencies of the other vertices do not change. Each suppression removes a vertex, and so this stops.
Then every vertex of a tree $T_v$ with at least two vertices has valency $\ge 3$.

Third, we show by induction on the number of edges in the trees $T_v$ that $J$ is a split of $H$. If
there are none, every $T_v$ has one vertex, and $J$ is isomorphic to $H$. Otherwise let $f$ be an
edge of some $T_v$, with ends $x, y$. Both have valency $\ge 3$. Contracting $f$ gives a graph with
trees as above, with fewer edges in its trees, in which again every vertex of a tree with at least
two vertices has valency $\ge 3$, since the new vertex has valency $\ge 4$. By induction it is a
split of $H$, and so $J$ is a split of $H$, since it is obtained from that graph by splitting the
new vertex. Hence $J$ is isomorphic to some $H_i$, and $G$ topologically contains $J$, as required.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_4_8` in `GraphMinor/GM02/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM02.theorem_4_8_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM02/TopologicalPatterns.lean`. The bound on the
size of a split is `GraphMinor.GM02.IsSplitOf.ncard_le`, and that a split of $H$ has a minor
isomorphic to $H$ is `GraphMinor.GM02.IsSplitOf.isIsoMinor`, both in
`GraphMinor/GM02/VertexSplit.lean`. The three steps of the converse are in
`GraphMinor.GM02.exists_split_topological_pattern` in `GraphMinor/GM02/SplitExtraction.lean`.

## 10. Proof of (5.4), page 321: minor-closed classes of bounded tree-width

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "The argument for (5.3) can be extended to yield the following",
states (5.4), and says "The proof is left to the reader."

**Proof.** We give the proof. We use the following result of [11] (Graph Minors IV in the paper's
list of references), which is (1.5) there and which implies (5.1): for any number $k$, in any
infinite sequence $G_1, G_2, \dots$ of graphs each with tree-width $\le k$, there exist $i < j$ such
that $G_i$ is isomorphic to a minor of $G_j$. Let $\mathscr{T}$ be the set of all graphs with
tree-width $\le w + 1$ that are not in $\mathscr{F}$.

(1) *There is a finite set $\mathscr{C} \subseteq \mathscr{T}$ such that every member of
$\mathscr{T}$ has a minor isomorphic to a member of $\mathscr{C}$.*

Suppose not. Then we may choose $H_1, H_2, \dots \in \mathscr{T}$ in turn such that for each
$j \ge 1$, $H_j$ has no minor isomorphic to any of $H_1, \dots, H_{j-1}$, since the set
$\{H_1, \dots, H_{j-1}\}$ does not satisfy (1). By the result above with $k = w + 1$, there exist
$i < j$ such that $H_i$ is isomorphic to a minor of $H_j$, a contradiction. This proves (1).

Let $\mathscr{C} = \{H_1, \dots, H_r\}$ be as in (1). We claim that a graph $G$ is in $\mathscr{F}$
if and only if it has no minor isomorphic to one of $H_1, \dots, H_r$. If $G \in \mathscr{F}$ and
$G$ has a minor isomorphic to $H_i$, then $H_i \in \mathscr{F}$ by the hypothesis on $\mathscr{F}$,
which is impossible since $H_i \in \mathscr{T}$. Now let $G \notin \mathscr{F}$. Delete vertices of
$G$ one at a time, as long as the graph remains outside $\mathscr{F}$. This gives a subgraph $M$ of
$G$ with $M \notin \mathscr{F}$, such that $M \setminus v \in \mathscr{F}$ for every $v \in V(M)$.
Then $M$ has tree-width $\le w + 1$. For this is true if $M$ has no vertices. Otherwise let
$v \in V(M)$. Then $M \setminus v$ has a tree-decomposition of width $\le w$, since it is in
$\mathscr{F}$, and adding $v$ to every set of this tree-decomposition gives a tree-decomposition of
$M$ of width $\le w + 1$. Hence $M \in \mathscr{T}$, and by (1), $M$ has a minor isomorphic to some
$H_i$. Since $M$ is a subgraph of $G$, so has $G$. This proves the claim.

The algorithm is now as in (5.3). We apply (4.9) to $G$ with $H_1, \dots, H_r$ in turn. If one of
the applications yields that $G$ has tree-width $> w$, then $G \notin \mathscr{F}$, since all
members of $\mathscr{F}$ have tree-width $\le w$. Otherwise we decide for $1 \le i \le r$ whether or
not $G$ has a minor isomorphic to $H_i$, and hence whether or not $G \in \mathscr{F}$. As for (5.3),
this proves that the algorithm exists, but does not construct it.
