## 1. Proof of (1.2), page 40: the derivation from (1.1)

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "It is easy to prove the equivalence of (1.2) with (1.1) by
making use of two observations: (a) if $H$ is a minor of $G$, then its path-width is no greater than
the path-width of $G$, and (b) there are trees with arbitrarily large path-width (e.g., the tree
$\mathbf{Y}_\lambda$ defined later has path-width $\lceil\frac12(\lambda+1)\rceil$ ...)". It proves
neither observation and does not write out the derivation.

**Proof.** We give the details. For (a), let $H$ be isomorphic to a minor of $G$. Then $H$ is
isomorphic to a graph obtained from a subgraph $G'$ of $G$ by contracting a set $C$ of edges. For
each $v\in V(H)$ let $Y(v)$ be the vertex set of the component of the graph with vertex set $V(G')$
and edge set $C$ that is contracted to $v$, and for each edge $e$ of $H$ let $f(e)$ be the
corresponding edge of $G'$. Then the sets $Y(v)$ $(v\in V(H))$ are disjoint, each $G|Y(v)$ is
connected, and for each edge $e$ of $H$ with ends $v,v'$, the edge $f(e)$ has one end in $Y(v)$ and
the other in $Y(v')$. (These are the conditions (M1)–(M3) of Section 3.) We use the following.

(1) *Let $X_1,\dots,X_r$ be a path-decomposition of $G$, let $K$ be a connected subgraph of $G$, and
let $1\le i\le j\le l\le r$. If $K$ has a vertex in $X_i$ and a vertex in $X_l$, then $K$ has a
vertex in $X_j$.*

Suppose not, and let $x\in V(K)\cap X_i$ and $y\in V(K)\cap X_l$. We claim that every vertex of $K$
is in some $X_t$ with $t\le j$. This holds for $x$, since $i\le j$. Let $z,w$ be adjacent vertices
of $K$, where $z\in X_t$ for some $t\le j$. By (W1) some $X_s$ contains both $z$ and $w$. If $s>j$,
then $z\in X_t\cap X_s\subseteq X_j$ by (W2), which is impossible since $K$ has no vertex in $X_j$.
So $s\le j$, and $w\in X_s$. Since $K$ is connected, every vertex of $K$ is reached from $x$ by such
steps, and the claim follows. In particular $y\in X_t$ for some $t\le j$, and so
$y\in X_t\cap X_l\subseteq X_j$ by (W2), which is impossible. This proves (1).

Now let $X_1,\dots,X_r$ be a path-decomposition of $G$ with $|X_i|\le k+1$ $(1\le i\le r)$. For
$1\le i\le r$ let $X'_i$ be the set of all $v\in V(H)$ with $Y(v)\cap X_i\ne\varnothing$. We claim
that $X'_1,\dots,X'_r$ is a path-decomposition of $H$ with $|X'_i|\le k+1$ $(1\le i\le r)$. For
(W1), let $e$ be an edge of $H$ with ends $v,v'$. Then $f(e)$ has an end $a\in Y(v)$ and an end
$b\in Y(v')$, and by (W1) for $G$ some $X_i$ contains $a$ and $b$. So $v,v'\in X'_i$. For (W2), let
$1\le i\le j\le l\le r$ and $v\in X'_i\cap X'_l$. Then $G|Y(v)$ is a connected subgraph of $G$ with
a vertex in $X_i$ and a vertex in $X_l$, and so it has a vertex in $X_j$, by (1). Thus $v\in X'_j$.
For the sizes, choose for each $v\in X'_i$ a vertex in $Y(v)\cap X_i$. Since the sets $Y(v)$ are
disjoint, distinct members of $X'_i$ give distinct vertices of $X_i$, and so
$|X'_i|\le|X_i|\le k+1$. This proves (a).

For (b) we prove that $\mathbf{Y}_{2k+1}$ has path-width $>k$ for every integer $k\ge 0$. (This is
the lower bound above for odd $\lambda$, which is all that we need.) For $k=0$ it is true, since
$\mathbf{Y}_1$ has an edge, and by (W1) some set of any path-decomposition contains both of its
ends. Let $k\ge 0$, suppose that $\mathbf{Y}_{2k+1}$ has path-width $>k$, and let
$\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Y}_{2k+3}$. Let $c$ be the vertex of valency 3 of the copy of $K_{1,3}$ from
which $\mathbf{Y}$ is built, and let $K_1, K_2, K_3$ be the components of
$\mathbf{Y}\backslash\{c\}$. Each $K_j$ is isomorphic to the tree $\mathbf{H}_{2k+2}$ of Section 3,
and so it has a minor isomorphic to $\mathbf{Y}_{2k+1}$, by (3.3). Hence $K_j$ has path-width $>k$,
by (a). Now suppose that $X_1,\dots,X_r$ is a path-decomposition of $\mathbf{Y}$ with $|X_i|\le k+2$
$(1\le i\le r)$. For $j=1,2,3$ the sets $X_i\cap V(K_j)$ $(1\le i\le r)$ form a path-decomposition
of $K_j$, and so $|X_{i_j}\cap V(K_j)|\ge k+2$ for some $i_j$. Then $X_{i_j}\subseteq V(K_j)$ and
$X_{i_j}\ne\varnothing$. Since $K_1,K_2,K_3$ are disjoint, $i_1, i_2, i_3$ are distinct, and we may
assume that $i_1<i_2<i_3$. The graph $\mathbf{Y}\backslash V(K_2)$ is connected, since $c$ is
adjacent to a vertex of $K_1$ and to a vertex of $K_3$. It contains $K_1$ and $K_3$, and so it has a
vertex in $X_{i_1}$ and a vertex in $X_{i_3}$. By (1) it has a vertex in $X_{i_2}$. But
$X_{i_2}\subseteq V(K_2)$, which is impossible. Thus $\mathbf{Y}_{2k+3}$ has path-width $>k+1$, and
(b) follows by induction.

Now assume (1.1), and let $\mathscr{F}$ be a set of graphs. If (i) holds, let $H=\mathbf{Y}_{2w+1}$,
which is a tree. If some $G\in\mathscr{F}$ had a minor isomorphic to $H$, then $H$ would have
path-width $\le w$ by (a), contrary to (b). So (ii) holds. If (ii) holds, (1.1) applied to $H$ gives
(i).

## 2. Proofs of (1.4)–(1.6), page 40: three facts about path-width

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper states (1.4), (1.5) and (1.6) and says "(the proofs are easy)". It
gives no proofs.

**Proof.** We give the proofs. First, if $G$ has path-width $\le k$, then it has a
path-decomposition $X_1,\dots,X_r$ with $|X_i|\le k+1$ $(1\le i\le r)$ in which every vertex is in
some $X_i$. For by (W1) a vertex in no $X_i$ is incident with no edge, and we may add the set
$\{v\}$ at the end of the sequence for each such vertex $v$. Then (W1) and (W2) still hold.

For (1.4), take a path-decomposition of each component of $G$ with sets of size $\le k+1$, and write
these sequences one after another. The result is a path-decomposition of $G$. For every edge of $G$
is an edge of some component, and each vertex is in sets of only one of the sequences, since the
sets of each sequence are subsets of the vertex set of its component.

For (1.5), take a path-decomposition $X_1,\dots,X_r$ of $G\backslash X$ with $|X_i|\le k+1$
$(1\le i\le r)$ in which every vertex of $G\backslash X$ is in some $X_i$. We claim that
$X_1\cup X,\dots,X_r\cup X,X$ is a path-decomposition of $G$. Its sets have size $\le k+1+|X|$, and
so this will prove (1.5). For (W1), every set contains $X$, and so an edge with both ends in $X$ has
both ends in the last set. An edge with just one end in $X$ has its other end in some $X_i$, and so
both ends in $X_i\cup X$. An edge with no end in $X$ is an edge of $G\backslash X$, and so both its
ends are in some $X_i$. For (W2), a vertex in $X$ is in every set, and a vertex not in $X$ is in
$X_i\cup X$ exactly when it is in $X_i$, and is not in the last set.

For (1.6), let $X_1,\dots,X_r$ be as in (1.6). For $1\le i\le r$ let
$B_i=X_i\cap\bigcup(X_j\colon j\ne i)$ and $G_i=G\backslash\bigcup(X_j\colon j\ne i)$. A vertex in
$X_i\cap X_j$ with $j<i$ is in $X_{i-1}$, and one with $j>i$ is in $X_{i+1}$, by (W2). So
$B_i\subseteq(X_{i-1}\cap X_i)\cup(X_i\cap X_{i+1})$ (where $X_0=X_{r+1}=\varnothing$), and
$|B_i|\le 2k$. By hypothesis and the first paragraph, $G_i$ has a path-decomposition
$Z_{i1},\dots,Z_{im_i}$ with $|Z_{ij}|\le k'+1$ $(1\le j\le m_i)$ in which every vertex of $G_i$ is
in some set. We call the sequence
$$(Z_{i1}\cap X_i)\cup B_i,\;\dots,\;(Z_{im_i}\cap X_i)\cup B_i,\;B_i$$
the *refinement* of $X_i$. Its sets are subsets of $X_i$ of size $\le k'+1+2k$, and each of them
contains $B_i$.

(2) *For $1\le i\le r$, the refinement of $X_i$ is a path-decomposition of $G|X_i$, and every vertex
of $X_i$ is in some set of it.*

A vertex of $X_i-B_i$ is in no $X_j$ with $j\ne i$, and so it is a vertex of $G_i$, and it is in
some $Z_{ij}$. The vertices of $B_i$ are in the last set. For (W1), let $e$ be an edge of $G|X_i$
with ends $u,v$. If $u,v\notin B_i$, then $u,v\notin\bigcup(X_j\colon j\ne i)$, so $e$ is an edge of
$G_i$, and some $Z_{ij}$ contains $u$ and $v$, by (W1) for $G_i$. Then $(Z_{ij}\cap X_i)\cup B_i$
contains both. If $u\in B_i$, then $v$ is in some set of the refinement, as we just saw, and $u$ is
in every set. The case $v\in B_i$ is the same. For (W2), a vertex of $B_i$ is in every set, and a
vertex $v\in X_i-B_i$ is in $(Z_{ij}\cap X_i)\cup B_i$ exactly when $v\in Z_{ij}$, and is not in the
last set. So (W2) follows from (W2) for $Z_{i1},\dots,Z_{im_i}$. This proves (2).

Now write the refinements of $X_1,\dots,X_r$ one after another. We claim that the result is a
path-decomposition of $G$. Since its sets have size $\le k'+1+2k$, this will prove (1.6). For (W1),
let $e$ be an edge with ends $u,v$. By (W1), $u,v\in X_i$ for some $i$. So $e$ is an edge of
$G|X_i$, and some set of the refinement of $X_i$ contains $u$ and $v$, by (2). For (W2), let $v$ be
in a set of the refinement of $X_i$ and in a later set of the refinement of $X_l$, where $i\le l$,
and consider a set between them, in the refinement of $X_j$ with $i\le j\le l$. If $i=l$, then
$j=i$, and $v$ is in the set by (2). Suppose that $i<l$. The sets of the refinements of $X_i$ and
$X_l$ are subsets of $X_i$ and $X_l$, and so $v\in X_i\cap X_l$, and $v\in X_j$ by (W2). Since
$i<l$, one of $i,l$ is not $j$, and so $v$ is in $X_j$ and in some other set $X_{j'}$. Hence
$v\in B_j$. So $v$ is in every set of the refinement of $X_j$.

`GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_1_6` in `GraphMinor/GM01/Results.lean`, proved by

`GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_1_5_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM01/PathWidth.lean` and
`GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_1_6_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM01/PathWidthGluing.lean`. The first
paragraph is `GraphMinor.GM01.HasPathWidthLE.exists_covering` in `GraphMinor/GM01/PathWidth.lean`.
For (1.6), the bound $|B_i|\le 2k$ is `GraphMinor.GM01.PathDecomposition.boundary_ncard_le` in
`GraphMinor/GM01/PathBagBoundaries.lean`, claim (2) is
`GraphMinor.GM01.exists_path_bag_refinement` in `GraphMinor/GM01/PathBagRefinement.lean`, and the

## 3. Statement of (1.7), page 41: the range of $i$ and $i'$

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** The hypothesis of (1.7) is printed "for $1 < i, i' \le T$". Read this way, it
says nothing about $Y_1$, and the conclusion fails. Let $G$ be the path $\rho\, a\, b$, let $T = 2$,
$Y_1 = \{b\}$ and $Y_2 = \{a\}$. The printed hypothesis only asks that $Y_2$ separates $a$ and
$\rho$, which holds since $a \in Y_2$. The set $W_0$ of vertices not separated from $\rho$ by $Y_1$
is $\{\rho, a\}$, and the set $W_2$ of vertices separated from $\rho$ by $Y_2$ is $\{a, b\}$. So
$W_0$ and $W_2$ are not disjoint.

**Correction.** The hypothesis reads "for $1 \le i, i' \le T$". The proof needs the case $i = 1$: in
the uniqueness step on page 42, the set $Y_{i+1}$ with $i = 0$ is $Y_1$. The only use of (1.7) is in
Section 4, and there (4.5) gives the hypothesis for all $1 \le i, i' \le T$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_1_7` in `GraphMinor/GM01/Results.lean`.

## 5. Statement of (2.4), page 44: an empty family $B_1, \dots, B_n$

**Kind.** Degenerate case.

**What goes wrong.** (2.4) concludes that $n < \theta^{2\theta-2}\binom{m}{\frac12\theta^2}$. If
$n = 0$ and $m < \frac12\theta^2$, every hypothesis holds, but the binomial coefficient is $0$ and
the conclusion reads $0 < 0$. For example, take the graph with no vertices and $m = n = 0$.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis $m \ge \frac12\theta^2$. If $n \ge 1$, this holds anyway, because
$B_1$ meets at least $\frac12\theta^2$ of the paths. So the new hypothesis only removes the case
$n = 0$. The only use of (2.4) is in the proof of (2.6), and there $m \ge \frac12\theta^2$ by the
correction to (2.6) below.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_2_4` in `GraphMinor/GM01/Results.lean`.

## 6. Statement of (2.6), page 46: $m > 0$ is not enough

**Kind.** Degenerate case.

$n < 2m\theta^{2\theta-2}\binom{m}{\frac12\theta^2}$. Take $\theta = 6$, let $G$ have one vertex $v$

of (2.8), where $m = \frac12 s\theta^2$ with $s \ge 1$ by the correction to (2.8) below. So every
use satisfies the new hypothesis.

## 10. Proof of (3.2), page 51: the induction

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "The proof by induction is left to the reader. We hope that a
'proof by diagram' is convincing", and Figure 3 shows the case $\lambda = 6$.

**Proof.** We give the induction. For $n\ge 0$ let $s_n=2^{n+1}-1$ and $m_n=2^n$. We call
$v_{m_nm_n}$ the *middle* of the $s_n$-grid, and $\{v_{im_n}\colon 1\le i\le s_n\}$ its *middle
column*. The *root* of $\mathbf{H}_{2n}$ is its vertex of even valency (its only vertex if $n=0$).
We prove by induction on $n$ that there are sets $Y(v)$ $(v\in V(\mathbf{H}_{2n}))$ of vertices of
the $s_n$-grid satisfying (M1)–(M3) below (with $G_1=\mathbf{H}_{2n}$ and $G_2$ the grid), such that
$Y(v)$ is the set containing only the middle when $v$ is the root, and no other $Y(v)$ meets the
middle column. Such sets give a minor of the grid isomorphic to $\mathbf{H}_{2n}$, and this proves
(3.2). For $n=0$ the grid has one vertex, and we take $Y$ of the root to be that vertex.

Now let $n\ge 0$, put $s=s_n$ and $m=m_n$, and assume the result for $n$. Then $s_{n+1}=2s+1$, and
the middle of the $(2s+1)$-grid is $v_{s+1,s+1}$. Deleting row $s+1$ and column $s+1$ from the
$(2s+1)$-grid leaves four disjoint $s$-grids, its *quarters*, with middles $v_{mm}$, $v_{s+1+m,m}$,
$v_{m,s+1+m}$ and $v_{s+1+m,s+1+m}$. The tree $\mathbf{H}_{2n+2}$ consists of its root $\rho$, two
neighbours $a, b$ of $\rho$, and four copies of $\mathbf{H}_{2n}$, where $a$ is adjacent to the
roots of two of the copies and $b$ to the roots of the other two. Put each copy in one quarter, by
the sets given by the inductive hypothesis, with the copies at $a$ in the two quarters with
$j\le s$. Let
$$\begin{aligned}A&=\{v_{im}\colon m+1\le i\le s+m\}\cup\{v_{s+1,j}\colon m\le j\le s\},\\ B&=\{v_{i,s+1+m}\colon m+1\le i\le s+m\}\cup\{v_{s+1,j}\colon s+2\le j\le s+1+m\},\end{aligned}$$
and let $Y(\rho)=\{v_{s+1,s+1}\}$, $Y(a)=A$, $Y(b)=B$. The sets $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, and each
induces a connected subgraph, being the union of a vertical and a horizontal path which share a
vertex in row $s+1$. They do not meet column $s+1$. They meet the quarters only in vertices of the
middle columns of the quarters other than their middles, and these vertices are in no set of the
four copies, by the inductive hypothesis. The set $A$ contains neighbours of $v_{s+1,s+1}$, of
$v_{mm}$ and of $v_{s+1+m,m}$, namely $v_{s+1,s}$, $v_{m+1,m}$ and $v_{s+m,m}$. Similarly $B$
contains neighbours of $v_{s+1,s+1}$ and of the middles of the two other quarters. Hence the sets
satisfy (M1)–(M3) for $\mathbf{H}_{2n+2}$. Moreover $Y(\rho)$ is the middle of the $(2s+1)$-grid,
and no other set meets column $s+1$, which is its middle column. This completes the induction.

`GraphMinor.GM01.theorem_3_2_proof` and `GraphMinor.GM01.binaryTree_isIsoMinor_grid` in
`GraphMinor/GM01/GridBinaryMinor.lean`.

## 11. Proof of (3.3), page 51: $\mathbf{Y}_\lambda$ in $\mathbf{H}_{\lambda+1}$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "The proof is clear." The proof is given because (3.3) is also
used for observation (b) in the proof of (1.2) above.

**Proof.** To see this, let $\rho$ be the root of $\mathbf{H}_{\lambda+1}$ (its vertex of valency
2). Deleting $\rho$ and its two neighbours from $\mathbf{H}_{\lambda+1}$ leaves four components,
each isomorphic to $\mathbf{H}_{\lambda-1}$, and each containing one vertex at distance 2 from
$\rho$, which is its root. Contract the two edges incident with $\rho$, and delete one of the four
components. In the resulting tree the new vertex is adjacent to the roots of the three remaining
components, and so the tree is isomorphic to $\mathbf{Y}_\lambda$, which consists of a vertex
adjacent to the roots of three copies of $\mathbf{H}_{\lambda-1}$.

## 12. Proof of (3.6), page 53: the four inequalities

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "This is routine, and is left to the reader."

**Proof.** By definition, $f_i(x)=f_{i-1}(\beta^x)$ for $i\ge 1$ and $x\ge 0$.

(1) *For each integer $i\ge 0$, $f_i$ is strictly increasing, and $f_i(x+d)\ge f_i(x)+d$ for all
integers $x,d\ge 0$.*

The map $f_0$ is the identity. For $i>0$, $f_i$ is the map $x\mapsto\beta^x$ followed by $f_{i-1}$.
The first is strictly increasing since $\beta=2^{\theta^6}>1$, and so $f_i$ is strictly increasing,
by induction on $i$. A strictly increasing map from the non-negative integers to themselves
increases by at least $1$ from $x$ to $x+1$, and so by at least $d$ from $x$ to $x+d$. This proves
(1).

(2) $\beta\ge 16$, *and* $\beta\ge\theta^2+2+4\theta^{2\theta}$.

Since $\theta\ge 6$, we have $\beta=2^{\theta^6}\ge 16$ and
$\theta^6=\theta^2\theta^4\ge 3\theta^2\ge 2\theta^2+3$. Since $\theta\le 2^\theta$, we have
$\theta^{2\theta}\le 2^{2\theta^2}$. Since also $\theta^2\le\theta^{2\theta}$, it follows that
$\theta^2+2+4\theta^{2\theta}\le 8\theta^{2\theta}\le 2^{2\theta^2+3}\le 2^{\theta^6}=\beta$. This
proves (2).

Now (i) holds since $p(0,\delta)=\delta$. For (ii), let $y=2+4\theta^{2\theta}+2\delta$. By (2),
$\beta+2\delta\ge y+\theta^2$, and so by (1),
$q(\gamma,\delta)=f_{\gamma-1}(\beta+2\delta)\ge f_{\gamma-1}(y)+\theta^2=p(\gamma-1,y)+\theta^2$.
For (iii), $\beta^\delta\ge\beta^2\ge\beta+8$ by (2), and so
$p(\gamma,\delta)=f_{\gamma-1}(\beta^\delta)\ge f_{\gamma-1}(\beta+8)=q(\gamma,4)$. For (iv), let
$A=\alpha^{\delta\cdot 2^\gamma}$. Since $\gamma\le\lambda$ and $\theta\ge 2^{(\lambda+3)/2}$, we
have $\theta^2\ge 2^{\lambda+3}\ge 2^{\gamma+1}$. Since also $\delta\ge 2$, it follows that
$\delta\cdot 2^\gamma\theta^4\le\frac12\delta\theta^6\le(\delta-1)\theta^6$. Hence
$A\le 2^{(\delta-1)\theta^6}=\beta^{\delta-1}$. Also $\beta+12\le 2\beta^{\delta-1}$, and so by (2),
$$\beta^\delta\ge 16\beta^{\delta-1}\ge\max(\beta^{\delta-1}+A,\;7A+\beta+12).$$
By (1) it follows that
$p(\gamma,\delta)=f_{\gamma-1}(\beta^\delta)\ge f_{\gamma-1}(\beta^{\delta-1})+A=p(\gamma,\delta-1)+A$,
and that $p(\gamma,\delta)\ge f_{\gamma-1}(\beta+6A+12)+A=q(\gamma,3A+6)+A$. So $p(\gamma,\delta)$
is at least $A$ plus the larger of $p(\gamma,\delta-1)$ and $q(\gamma,3A+6)$, and this proves (iv).

## 13. Statement of (4.4)(i), page 56: $j \ge i$, not $j \ge 1$

**What goes wrong.** Here $\nu = \theta^{2\theta}$, $n - 1 = \nu T$ with $T \ge 2$, and
