## 1. Proof of (2.6), page 156: tangles of order 1 and 2 in a graph

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "Since we do not need the result, we merely sketch the proof",
and says three times that a step "is easy to see": that every tangle of order 1 comes from a vertex,
that every tangle of order 2 comes from a non-loop edge, and that two edges give the same tangle if
and only if they are in the same block.

**Proof.** We give the details. Besides (2.2)–(2.4) we use (2.7), (2.9) and the extreme separations
defined before (2.8), whose proofs do not use (2.6). Let $K$ be the null graph. For every tangle
$\mathscr{T}$ in $G$, the pair $(K, G)$ is in $\mathscr{T}$, since $(K, G)$ and $(G, K)$ are
separations of order 0 and $(G, K) \notin \mathscr{T}$ by the third axiom.

For $v \in V(G)$ let $\mathscr{T}_v$ be the set of all separations $(A, B)$ of $G$ of order 0 with
$v \in V(B)$. This is the tangle of order 1 of (2.5). Indeed, if $(A, B)$ has order 0 then $v$ is in
exactly one of $V(A)$, $V(B)$, which gives the first axiom, and if $(A_i, B_i) \in \mathscr{T}_v$
$(1 \le i \le 3)$ then $v$ is in none of $V(A_1)$, $V(A_2)$, $V(A_3)$, which gives the second and
third.

(1) *Every tangle $\mathscr{T}$ in $G$ of order $1$ equals $\mathscr{T}_v$ for some $v \in V(G)$.*

Choose $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$ with $|V(A)| + |E(A)| - |V(B)| - |E(B)|$ maximum (this is possible
since $(K, G) \in \mathscr{T}$). Then $(A, B)$ is extreme, since for $(A', B') \in \mathscr{T}$ with
$A \subseteq A'$ and $B' \subseteq B$ the maximality gives $A' = A$ and $B' = B$. By the third axiom
there exists $v \in V(G) - V(A)$, and $v \in V(B)$ since $A \cup B = G$. Let
$(A', B') \in \mathscr{T}$. A vertex in both $A \cup A'$ and $B \cap B'$ is in $A \cap B$ or in
$A' \cap B'$, and so $(A \cup A', B \cap B')$ has order 0. Hence it is in $\mathscr{T}$ by (2.2),
and $B \cap B' = B$ since $(A, B)$ is extreme. Thus $v \in V(B')$, and so
$\mathscr{T} \subseteq \mathscr{T}_v$. Conversely, let $(A', B') \in \mathscr{T}_v$. If
$(B', A') \in \mathscr{T}$ then $v \in V(A')$ by what we just proved, and so $v \in V(A' \cap B')$,
which is impossible since $(A', B')$ has order 0. Hence $(A', B') \in \mathscr{T}$ by the first
axiom. This proves (1).

(2) *For $u, v \in V(G)$, $\mathscr{T}_u = \mathscr{T}_v$ if and only if $u$ and $v$ are in the same
component of $G$.*

Let $(A, B)$ be a separation of order 0. An edge of $G$ with an end in $V(B)$ is not in $E(A)$,
since all ends of an edge of $A$ are in $V(A)$ and no vertex is in both. So it is in $E(B)$, and all
its ends are in $V(B)$. Hence if $u$ and $v$ are joined by a path of $G$, then $u \in V(B)$ if and
only if $v \in V(B)$, and so $\mathscr{T}_u = \mathscr{T}_v$. Now let $u$ and $v$ be in different
components, let $A$ be the component that contains $u$, and let $B$ be the subgraph formed by all
the other vertices and edges of $G$. Then $(A, B)$ is a separation of order 0 since no edge of $B$
has an end in $V(A)$, and $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}_v - \mathscr{T}_u$. This proves (2).

By (1) and (2), mapping each component $C$ of $G$ to $\mathscr{T}_v$, where $v \in V(C)$, gives a
1-1 correspondence between the components of $G$ and the tangles of order 1. (By (2) the image does
not depend on the choice of $v$ and distinct components have distinct images, and by (1) every
tangle of order 1 is an image.)

For a non-loop edge $e$ let $\mathscr{T}_e$ be the set of all separations $(A, B)$ of $G$ of order
$\le 1$ with $e \in E(B)$. Since $e$ has size 2, $\mathscr{T}_e$ is a tangle of order 2 by (2.4).

(3) *Every tangle $\mathscr{T}$ in $G$ of order $2$ equals $\mathscr{T}_e$ for some non-loop edge
$e$.*

For $X \subseteq E(G)$, let $L(X)$ be the subgraph with edge set $X$ whose vertices are the ends of
the edges in $X$ and the vertices incident with no edge, and let $R(X)$ be the subgraph with edge
set $E(G) - X$ whose vertices are the ends of the edges not in $X$. Then $(L(X), R(X))$ is a
separation of $G$. If $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$ then $(L(E(A)), R(E(A))) \in \mathscr{T}$. For a
vertex in both $L(E(A))$ and $R(E(A))$ is an end of an edge of $A$ and of an edge of $B$, and so it
is in $V(A \cap B)$. Hence $(L(E(A)), R(E(A)))$ has order $\le 1$, and it is in $\mathscr{T}$ by
(2.9)(iii). In particular $(L(\emptyset), R(\emptyset)) \in \mathscr{T}$, since
$(K, G) \in \mathscr{T}$.

We claim that some edge $e$ of $G$ is in $E(B)$ for all $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$. Suppose not. Then
every edge is in $E(A)$ for some $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$, and so it is in some $X \subseteq E(G)$
with $(L(X), R(X)) \in \mathscr{T}$. Choose a set $\mathscr{S}$ of subsets $X$ of $E(G)$ with union
$E(G)$, such that $(L(X), R(X)) \in \mathscr{T}$ for all $X \in \mathscr{S}$, with $|\mathscr{S}|$
minimum. If $|\mathscr{S}| \le 3$, then the members of $\mathscr{S}$ and copies of $\emptyset$ give
three members of $\mathscr{T}$ the union of whose first terms contains every edge of $G$, contrary
to (2.3). So $|\mathscr{S}| \ge 4$. Choose $X_1 \in \mathscr{S}$, and put
$(A_1, B_1) = (L(X_1), R(X_1))$. Let $X' \in \mathscr{S} - \{X_1\}$, and put
$(A', B') = (L(X'), R(X'))$. Then $(A_1 \cup A', B_1 \cap B')$ has order $\ge 2$. For otherwise it
is in $\mathscr{T}$ by (2.2), and so $(L(X_1 \cup X'), R(X_1 \cup X')) \in \mathscr{T}$ by the
previous paragraph, since $E(A_1 \cup A') = X_1 \cup X'$. But then replacing $X_1$ and $X'$ by
$X_1 \cup X'$ in $\mathscr{S}$ gives a smaller set, contrary to the choice of $\mathscr{S}$. Now
every vertex of $(A_1 \cup A') \cap (B_1 \cap B')$ is either in $V(A_1 \cap B_1) \cap V(B')$ or in
$V(A' \cap B') - V(A_1)$. Each of these two sets has at most one member, since both separations have
order $\le 1$, and together they have at least two. So each has exactly one. Hence
$V(A_1 \cap B_1) = \{u\}$ for some $u \in V(B')$, and the only vertex of $A' \cap B'$ is not in
$V(A_1)$, so it is not $u$. Since $u \in V(B')$, it follows that $u \notin V(A')$. Here $u$ does not
depend on $X'$, since $V(A_1 \cap B_1) = \{u\}$. Now $u \in V(B_1)$, and so $u$ is an end of some
edge $f \notin X_1$. Since the union of $\mathscr{S}$ is $E(G)$, $f \in X'$ for some
$X' \in \mathscr{S} - \{X_1\}$, and then $u \in V(L(X'))$, a contradiction. This proves our claim.

Choose $e$ as in the claim. If $e$ is a loop, then $e$ has size 1, and so
$(K_e, G \setminus e) \in \mathscr{T}$ by (2.7), where $K_e$ is formed by $e$ and its end. This is
impossible since $e \notin E(G \setminus e)$. So $e$ is not a loop, and
$\mathscr{T} \subseteq \mathscr{T}_e$ by the claim. Conversely, let $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}_e$. Then
$e \notin E(A)$, and so $(B, A) \notin \mathscr{T}$ by the claim. Hence $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$ by
the first axiom. This proves (3).

(4) *If two distinct edges $e_1, e_2$ of $G$ are in a circuit $C$, then
$\mathscr{T}_{e_1} = \mathscr{T}_{e_2}$.*

Since $C$ has two edges, it is not a loop, and so every edge of $C$ has two ends and every vertex of
$C$ is incident with exactly two edges of $C$. Let $(A, B)$ be a separation of order $\le 1$. For
$v \in V(C)$ let $d(v)$ be the number of edges of $C$ in $E(A)$ incident with $v$. The sum of $d(v)$
over all $v \in V(C)$ is twice the number of edges of $C$ in $E(A)$, and so it is even. If
$v \in V(A) - V(B)$ then $d(v) = 2$, since an edge of $B$ at $v$ would put $v$ in $V(B)$, and if
$v \notin V(A)$ then $d(v) = 0$. Since at most one vertex of $C$ is in $V(A \cap B)$, it follows
that $d(v)$ is even for that vertex too. So at each vertex of $C$, either both edges of $C$ are in
$E(A)$ or neither is. Since $C$ is connected, either every edge of $C$ is in $E(A)$ or none is.
Since every edge of $G$ is in exactly one of $E(A)$, $E(B)$, it follows that $e_1 \in E(B)$ if and
only if $e_2 \in E(B)$. This proves (4).

(5) *If $e_1, e_2$ are distinct non-loop edges with $\mathscr{T}_{e_1} = \mathscr{T}_{e_2}$, then
some circuit of $G$ contains both.*

Let $Y_1$, $Y_2$ be the sets of ends of $e_1$, $e_2$, and let $G' = G \setminus \{e_1, e_2\}$.
Suppose that $G'$ has a separation $(G_1, G_2)$ of order $\le 1$ with $Y_1 \subseteq V(G_1)$ and
$Y_2 \subseteq V(G_2)$. Adding $e_1$ to $G_1$ and $e_2$ to $G_2$ gives a separation $(A, B)$ of $G$
of order $\le 1$ with $e_1 \in E(A)$ and $e_2 \in E(B)$. Then
$(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}_{e_2} = \mathscr{T}_{e_1}$, and so $e_1 \in E(B)$, a contradiction. Hence by
Menger's theorem there are two disjoint paths of $G'$ from $Y_1$ to $Y_2$. Since they are disjoint
and $|Y_1| = |Y_2| = 2$, one of them joins one end of $e_1$ to one end of $e_2$, the other joins the
other two ends, and neither has an internal vertex in $Y_1 \cup Y_2$. So the two paths together with
$e_1$ and $e_2$ form a circuit of $G$. This proves (5).

For a non-loop edge $e$, let $H_e$ be the subgraph of $G$ whose edges are the non-loop edges $f$
with $\mathscr{T}_f = \mathscr{T}_e$, and whose vertices are the ends of these edges.

(6) *For every non-loop edge $e$, $H_e$ is a block of $G$, and every block of $G$ that contains $e$
equals $H_e$.*

Let $f_1, f_2$ be distinct edges of $H_e$. Then $\mathscr{T}_{f_1} = \mathscr{T}_{f_2}$, and so some
circuit $C$ of $G$ contains $f_1$ and $f_2$, by (5). Every edge of $C$ is a non-loop edge with the
same tangle as $f_1$, by (4), and so $C \subseteq H_e$. Hence any two distinct edges of $H_e$ are in
a circuit of $H_e$. Also $H_e$ is connected, since any two of its vertices are ends of edges
$f_1, f_2$ of $H_e$, and either $f_1 = f_2$ or $f_1, f_2$ are in a circuit of $H_e$. Now let $H$ be
a connected subgraph of $G$ that contains $e$, any two distinct edges of which are in a circuit of
$H$. We show that $H \subseteq H_e$. Each edge $f \ne e$ of $H$ is in a circuit of $H$ together with
$e$. This circuit is not a loop, and so $f$ is a non-loop edge with $\mathscr{T}_f = \mathscr{T}_e$,
by (4). So $E(H) \subseteq E(H_e)$. Each vertex of $H$ is incident with an edge of $H$, since $H$ is
connected and contains the edge $e$. So $V(H) \subseteq V(H_e)$, and $H \subseteq H_e$. Applying
this to a subgraph $H$ that contains $H_e$ shows that $H_e$ is maximal, and so $H_e$ is a block.
Applying it to a block $H$ that contains $e$ gives $H \subseteq H_e$, and so $H = H_e$ by the
maximality of $H$. This proves (6).

For non-loop edges $e, e'$, $H_e = H_{e'}$ if and only if $\mathscr{T}_e = \mathscr{T}_{e'}$, by the
definition of $H_e$ and since $e \in E(H_e)$. Map each block $H$ with a non-loop edge to
$\mathscr{T}_e$, where $e$ is a non-loop edge of $H$. By (6), $H = H_e$ for every such $e$, and so
the image does not depend on the choice of $e$, and distinct blocks have distinct images. By (3) and
(6), every tangle of order 2 is $\mathscr{T}_e$ for some non-loop edge $e$, and so it is the image
of the block $H_e$. This gives the 1-1 correspondence for order 2.

## 2. Proof of (4.2)(ii), page 165: graphs with loops

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** (4.2)(ii) states that $G$ has branch-width $\le 1$ if and only if every
component of $G$ has at most one vertex of valency $\ge 2$. The proof of "only if" says that it
follows from (4.1) and the fact that a 2-edge circuit and a 3-edge path both have branch-width 2.
This misses graphs with loops. Let $G$ have two vertices joined by one edge, with a loop at each.
Both vertices have valency $\ge 2$, but $G$ has no 2-edge circuit and no 3-edge path as a minor,
since each of these needs two edges that are not loops. The statement still holds for this graph,
which has branch-width 2.

**Correction.** Prove "only if" directly. Let a component of $G$ have two vertices of valency
$\ge 2$, and let $e$ be the first edge of a path between them. Both ends of $e$ have valency
$\ge 2$, so each of them is incident with an edge other than $e$. So in every branch-decomposition
$(T, \tau)$ of $G$, the edge of $T$ incident with the leaf $\tau^{-1}(e)$ has order 2, and $G$ has
branch-width $\ge 2$. This argument covers loops, and (4.2) holds as printed.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM10.branchWidth_le_one_iff_components_le_one_deg_ge_two` in
`GraphMinor/GM10/Section4Minors.lean`, and `GraphMinor.GM10.theorem_4_2` in
`GraphMinor/GM10/Results.lean`. The example is `GraphMinor.GM10.loopBridgeLoop` in
`GraphMinor/GM10/Section4LoopExample.lean`, where
`GraphMinor.GM10.loopBridgeLoop_has_neither_named_minor` proves that it has neither minor.

## 4. Proof of (9.1), page 177: laminar sets of separations and tree-decompositions

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "The proof is easy and is left to the reader."

**Proof.** For an edge $e$ of $T$ with ends $u, v$, let $G^e_u$ be the union of the subhypergraphs
$\tau(t)$ over the vertices $t$ of the component of $T \setminus e$ that contains $u$. Thus
$(G^e_u, G^e_v)$ and $(G^e_v, G^e_u)$ are the separations made by $e$. (They are separations, since
$G^e_u \cup G^e_v = G$ by (i) of the definition of tree-decomposition, and no edge of $G$ is in
both, by (ii).) If we replace one of two separations by its reverse, the four conditions in the
definition of crossing are permuted. Hence two separations cross if and only if they cross after one
or both of them are reversed.

For the first statement, let $e_1, e_2 \in E(T)$. If $e_1 = e_2$, the separations made by $e_1$ are
$(A, B)$ and $(B, A)$ for some $A, B$, and two separations each equal to $(A, B)$ or $(B, A)$ do not
cross: if they are equal the first condition holds, and otherwise the second holds. So we may assume
that $e_1 \ne e_2$. Let $e_i$ have ends $u_i, v_i$ $(i = 1, 2)$. Since $e_2$ is an edge of
$T \setminus e_1$, its ends are in the same component of $T \setminus e_1$, and by exchanging the
names $u_1, v_1$ we may assume that this is the component that contains $v_1$. Similarly, by
exchanging the names $u_2, v_2$, which keeps this property, we may assume that $u_1$ and $v_1$ are
in the component of $T \setminus e_2$ that contains $u_2$. The component of $T \setminus e_1$ that
contains $u_1$ does not contain $e_2$, since both ends of $e_2$ are in the other component, and so
it is a connected subgraph of $T \setminus e_2$. It contains $u_1$, and so it is contained in the
component of $T \setminus e_2$ that contains $u_2$. Hence $G^{e_1}_{u_1} \subseteq G^{e_2}_{u_2}$.
Similarly the component of $T \setminus e_2$ that contains $v_2$ does not contain $e_1$, since both
ends of $e_1$ are in the component that contains $u_2$. So it is a connected subgraph of
$T \setminus e_1$, and it contains $v_2$, which is in the component of $T \setminus e_1$ that
contains $v_1$. Hence $G^{e_2}_{v_2} \subseteq G^{e_1}_{v_1}$. So $(G^{e_1}_{u_1}, G^{e_1}_{v_1})$
and $(G^{e_2}_{u_2}, G^{e_2}_{v_2})$ satisfy the first condition and do not cross. The other
separations made by $e_1$ and $e_2$ are their reverses, and so no two of the separations made by
edges of $T$ cross.

For the converse, let $\mathscr{S}$ be the set of the separations $(A_i, B_i)$ and $(B_i, A_i)$
$(1 \le i \le k)$. It is laminar, by the remark on reversal. We prove by induction on
$|\mathscr{S}|$ that if $\mathscr{S}$ is a laminar set of separations of $G$ that contains the
reverse of each of its members, then there is a tree-decomposition $(T, \tau)$ of $G$ such that the
separations made by edges of $T$ are exactly the members of $\mathscr{S}$, and each member of
$\mathscr{S}$ is made by a unique edge of $T$. This proves (i) and (ii), since the two separations
made by an edge of $T$ are the reverses of each other. If $\mathscr{S} = \emptyset$, let $T$ have
one vertex $t$ and put $\tau(t) = G$. So we may assume that $\mathscr{S} \ne \emptyset$. For a
separation $(A, B)$ of $G$ let
$$s(A, B) = |V(A)| + |E(A)| + |V(G) - V(B)| + |E(G) - E(B)|,$$
and choose $(A_0, B_0) \in \mathscr{S}$ with $s(A_0, B_0)$ minimum. Let $\mathscr{S}'$ be obtained
from $\mathscr{S}$ by removing $(A_0, B_0)$ and $(B_0, A_0)$. Then $\mathscr{S}'$ is laminar and
contains the reverse of each of its members, and so from our inductive hypothesis there is a
tree-decomposition $(T', \tau')$ of $G$ for $\mathscr{S}'$ as above. For an edge $e$ of $T'$ with
ends $u, v$ we define $G'^e_u$ for $(T', \tau')$ as we defined $G^e_u$.

(1) *For each $(C, D) \in \mathscr{S}'$, either $A_0 \subseteq C$ and $D \subseteq B_0$, or
$A_0 \subseteq D$ and $C \subseteq B_0$.*

For $(A_0, B_0)$ and $(C, D)$ do not cross, and so one of the four conditions holds. The first two
are the two outcomes of (1). Suppose that $B_0 \subseteq C$ and $D \subseteq A_0$. Then
$(D, C) \in \mathscr{S}$, and $s(D, C) \le s(A_0, B_0)$ with equality only if $D = A_0$ and
$C = B_0$, since $D \subseteq A_0$ and $B_0 \subseteq C$. By the choice of $(A_0, B_0)$ equality
holds, and so $(C, D) = (B_0, A_0) \notin \mathscr{S}'$, a contradiction. Similarly, if
$B_0 \subseteq D$ and $C \subseteq A_0$, then $(C, D) = (A_0, B_0)$, again a contradiction. This
proves (1).

Call an ordered pair $(u, w)$ of adjacent vertices of $T'$ *good* if $A_0 \subseteq G'^e_u$ and
$G'^e_w \subseteq B_0$, where $e$ is the edge with ends $u, w$. Since
$(G'^e_u, G'^e_w) \in \mathscr{S}'$, (1) implies that for every edge $e$ of $T'$ with ends $u, w$,
at least one of $(u, w)$, $(w, u)$ is good.

(2) *There is a vertex $u_0$ of $T'$ such that $(u_0, w)$ is good for every neighbour $w$ of $u_0$.*

For suppose not. Then each vertex $v$ of $T'$ has a neighbour $u$ such that $(v, u)$ is not good,
and then $(u, v)$ is good. Starting from any vertex and repeating, we obtain an infinite walk
$v_0 v_1 v_2 \dots$ of $T'$ such that for each $i$, $(v_i, v_{i+1})$ is not good and
$(v_{i+1}, v_i)$ is good. Then $v_{i+2} \ne v_i$, since $(v_{i+1}, v_{i+2})$ is not good and
$(v_{i+1}, v_i)$ is good. A walk in a tree that never goes back along the edge it has just used is a
path, and so $T'$ has arbitrarily long paths, which is impossible. This proves (2).

(3) *$\tau'(t) \subseteq B_0$ for every vertex $t \ne u_0$ of $T'$, and $A_0 \subseteq \tau'(u_0)$.*

Let $t \ne u_0$, and let $w$ be the neighbour of $u_0$ on the path of $T'$ from $u_0$ to $t$, and
$e$ the edge with ends $u_0, w$. Then $t$ is in the component of $T' \setminus e$ that contains $w$,
and so $\tau'(t) \subseteq G'^e_w \subseteq B_0$ by (2). For the second statement, let
$x \in V(A_0)$. Then $x \in V(\tau'(t))$ for some $t$. If $t \ne u_0$, define $w$ and $e$ as before.
Since $A_0 \subseteq G'^e_{u_0}$ by (2), $x \in V(\tau'(t_1))$ for some vertex $t_1$ of the
component of $T' \setminus e$ that contains $u_0$. Then $u_0$ is on the path of $T'$ between $t_1$
and $t$, and so $x \in V(\tau'(u_0))$. Now let $f \in E(A_0)$. Then $f \in E(\tau'(t))$ for some
$t$, and $t = u_0$, since otherwise $f \in E(B_0)$ by the first statement, which is impossible since
$E(A_0 \cap B_0) = \emptyset$. This proves (3).

Now let $T$ be obtained from $T'$ by adding a new vertex $t_0$ and an edge $e_0$ joining $t_0$ and
$u_0$. Let $\tau(t_0) = A_0$, $\tau(u_0) = \tau'(u_0) \cap B_0$, and $\tau(t) = \tau'(t)$ for every
other vertex $t$. Since $\tau'(u_0) \subseteq A_0 \cup B_0$ and $A_0 \subseteq \tau'(u_0)$ by (3),
$$\tau(t_0) \cup \tau(u_0) = A_0 \cup (\tau'(u_0) \cap B_0) = \tau'(u_0).$$
We check that $(T, \tau)$ is a tree-decomposition of $G$. First, $T$ is a tree, and (i) holds by (i)
for $(T', \tau')$ and the equation above. For (ii), $\tau(t_0) = A_0$ has no edge in common with
$\tau(u_0)$ or with any $\tau(t) = \tau'(t)$ $(t \ne t_0, u_0)$, since these are subhypergraphs of
$B_0$ by (3) and $E(A_0 \cap B_0) = \emptyset$. The other pairs satisfy (ii) since
$\tau(t) \subseteq \tau'(t)$ for every vertex $t$ of $T'$. For (iii), let $t'$ be on the path of $T$
between $t$ and $t''$, where $t' \ne t, t''$ (otherwise (iii) is trivial). Then $t' \ne t_0$, since
$t_0$ has valency 1. If $t_0 \notin \{t, t''\}$, then $\tau(t) \cap \tau(t'') \subseteq \tau'(t')$
by (iii) for $(T', \tau')$. If $t_0 \in \{t, t''\}$, say $t = t_0$, then $t'$ is on the path of $T'$
between $u_0$ and $t''$. Since $A_0 \subseteq \tau'(u_0)$, it follows that
$\tau(t_0) \cap \tau(t'') \subseteq \tau'(u_0) \cap \tau'(t'') \subseteq \tau'(t')$. So in each case
$\tau(t) \cap \tau(t'') \subseteq \tau'(t')$. This proves (iii) when $t' \ne u_0$. When $t' = u_0$,
neither $t$ nor $t''$ is $u_0$, and one of them is not $t_0$. That one is a vertex $t_1 \ne u_0$ of
$T'$, and $\tau(t_1) \subseteq B_0$ by (3). Hence
$\tau(t) \cap \tau(t'') \subseteq \tau'(u_0) \cap B_0 = \tau(u_0)$. So $(T, \tau)$ is a
tree-decomposition of $G$.

The separations made by $e_0$ are $(A_0, B_0)$ and $(B_0, A_0)$. For the component of
$T \setminus e_0$ that contains $t_0$ has only the vertex $t_0$, and $\tau(t_0) = A_0$. The union of
$\tau(t)$ over the other vertices is a subhypergraph of $B_0$ by (3). It also contains every vertex
and edge of $B_0$, since each of them is in some $\tau'(t)$, and if $t = u_0$ it is in
$\tau'(u_0) \cap B_0 = \tau(u_0)$. So this union is $B_0$. Now let $e$ be an edge of $T'$. Then
$t_0$ and $u_0$ are in the same component of $T \setminus e$, and
$\tau(t_0) \cup \tau(u_0) = \tau'(u_0)$. So $e$ makes the same separations under $(T, \tau)$ as
under $(T', \tau')$. Hence the separations made by edges of $T$ are the members of
$\mathscr{S}' \cup \{(A_0, B_0), (B_0, A_0)\} = \mathscr{S}$. Each member of $\mathscr{S}'$ is made
by a unique edge of $T'$, and not by $e_0$. The separations $(A_0, B_0)$ and $(B_0, A_0)$ are made
by $e_0$, and by no edge of $T'$, since they are not in $\mathscr{S}'$. This completes the
induction, and so proves (9.1).

## 5. Proof of (10.4), page 184: at most $|V(G)|$ maximal tangles

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "We merely sketch the proof since we do not need the result".
The sketch says that if $V(A) = V(C)$ "then it follows easily that $A = C$, $B = D$, a
contradiction", and that from the inclusions $V(A) \subset V(C)$ and $V(D) \subset V(B)$ "one can
show that $|E(T)| \le |V(G)| - 1$".

**Proof.** We give the details. There are only finitely many tangles in $G$, since each of them is a
set of separations of $G$. Two distinct maximal tangles are distinguishable, since if one of them
were a subset of the other they would be equal, by maximality. A tie-breaker exists by (9.2), and so
if $n \ge 1$, the standard tree-decomposition $(T, \tau)$ exists by (10.3), where $t_i$ corresponds
to $\mathscr{T}_i$. Let $\theta_i$ be the order of $\mathscr{T}_i$. For an edge $e$ of $T$ with ends
$t_i, t_j$, let $G^e_i$ be the union of $\tau(t)$ over the vertices $t$ of the component of
$T \setminus e$ that contains $t_i$, as in the proof of (9.1). By (10.3)(ii), applied to $t_i$ and
$t_j$, whose path in $T$ has only the edge $e$, one of the two separations made by $e$ is the
$(\mathscr{T}_j, \mathscr{T}_i)$-distinction. It is in $\mathscr{T}_j$ and its reverse is in
$\mathscr{T}_i$. It is not $(G^e_j, G^e_i)$, since $(G^e_i, G^e_j) \notin \mathscr{T}_i$ by
(10.3)(i). Hence
$$(G^e_i, G^e_j) \in \mathscr{T}_j \quad \text{and} \quad (G^e_j, G^e_i) \in \mathscr{T}_i.$$
We first prove the inclusions $V(A) \subset V(C)$ and $V(D) \subset V(B)$ of the sketch. As in the
proof of (9.1), we may name the ends of $e$ as $t_i, t_j$ and those of $f$ as $t_k, t_l$ so that the
ends of $f$ are in the component of $T \setminus e$ that contains $t_j$, and the ends of $e$ are in
the component of $T \setminus f$ that contains $t_k$. Then $(A, B) = (G^e_i, G^e_j)$ and
$(C, D) = (G^f_k, G^f_l)$ satisfy $A \subseteq C$ and $D \subseteq B$, as shown there. Suppose that
$V(A) = V(C)$. Then $V(C \cap D) \subseteq V(A \cap B)$ since $V(D) \subseteq V(B)$, and so $(C, D)$
has order less than $\theta_j$, because $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}_j$. Since $V(C) \subseteq V(A)$, it
follows that $(C, D) \in \mathscr{T}_j$ by (2.9)(ii). But $t_j$ is in the component of
$T \setminus f$ that contains $t_k$, and so $(C, D) \notin \mathscr{T}_j$ by (10.3)(i), a
contradiction. Thus $V(A) \subset V(C)$. The same argument, applied to $(D, C) \in \mathscr{T}_k$
and $(B, A)$, shows that $V(D) \subset V(B)$.

For the bound on $n$ we show the following.

(1) *If $t_i, t_j$ are adjacent in $T$, then $V(\tau(t_i)) \not\subseteq V(\tau(t_j))$.*

Let $e$ be the edge joining $t_i$ and $t_j$, and put $(A, B) = (G^e_i, G^e_j)$. Then
$(B, A) \in \mathscr{T}_i$, and so $(A, B) \notin \mathscr{T}_i$ by (2.1), and $(A, B)$ has order
less than $\theta_i$. Suppose that $V(\tau(t_i)) \subseteq V(\tau(t_j))$. Since
$\tau(t_i) \subseteq A$ and $\tau(t_j) \subseteq B$, it follows that
$V(\tau(t_i)) \subseteq V(A \cap B)$. Let $t_{k_1}, \dots, t_{k_m}$ be the neighbours of $t_i$ other
than $t_j$, let $e_h$ be the edge joining $t_i$ and $t_{k_h}$, and put
$(A_h, B_h) = (G^{e_h}_{k_h}, G^{e_h}_i)$ $(1 \le h \le m)$. Then $(A_h, B_h) \in \mathscr{T}_i$, as
shown above. Also $V(A_h \cap B_h) \subseteq V(\tau(t_i))$, by (iii) of the definition of
tree-decomposition, since $t_i$ is on the path of $T$ between any vertex of the component of
$T \setminus e_h$ that contains $t_{k_h}$ and any vertex of the other component. If $m = 0$, then
$t_i$ is the only vertex of its component of $T \setminus e$, so $A = \tau(t_i)$ and
$V(A) \subseteq V(B)$. Then $V(B) = V(G)$, contrary to the third axiom, since
$(B, A) \in \mathscr{T}_i$. So $m \ge 1$. For $1 \le h \le m$, every vertex of
$(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_h) \cap (B_1 \cap \dots \cap B_h)$ is in $A_g \cap B_g$ for some $g \le h$,
and so it is in $V(\tau(t_i)) \subseteq V(A \cap B)$. Hence this separation has order less than
$\theta_i$, and by (2.2) and induction on $h$ it is in $\mathscr{T}_i$. Put
$C = A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_m$ and $D = B_1 \cap \dots \cap B_m$, so that $(C, D) \in \mathscr{T}_i$.
We claim that $V(D) \subseteq V(B)$. Let $x \in V(D)$, and suppose that $x \notin V(B)$. Then
$x \in V(A)$, and so $x \in V(\tau(t))$ for some vertex $t$ of the component of $T \setminus e$ that
contains $t_i$. Here $t \ne t_i$ since $V(\tau(t_i)) \subseteq V(B)$, and so the path of $T$ from
$t_i$ to $t$ has first edge $e_h$ for some $h$. Since $x \in V(B_h)$, there is a vertex $t'$ of the
component of $T \setminus e_h$ that contains $t_i$ with $x \in V(\tau(t'))$. Then $t_i$ is on the
path of $T$ between $t'$ and $t$, and so $x \in V(\tau(t_i)) \subseteq V(B)$, a contradiction. Thus
$V(D) \subseteq V(B)$, and so $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}_i$ by (2.9)(i), a contradiction. This proves
(1).

If $n = 0$ there is nothing to prove, and if $n = 1$ then $n \le |V(G)|$ since $\mathscr{T}_1$ has
order at least 1 and at most $|V(G)|$. So we may assume that $n \ge 2$. For each vertex $t \ne t_1$
of $T$, let $p(t)$ be the neighbour of $t$ on the path of $T$ from $t$ to $t_1$, let $e_t$ be the
edge joining $t$ and $p(t)$, and choose $x(t) \in V(\tau(t)) - V(\tau(p(t)))$, which is possible by
(1). Since $n \ge 2$, $t_1$ has a neighbour, and so $V(\tau(t_1)) \ne \emptyset$ by (1). Choose
$x(t_1) \in V(\tau(t_1))$. We claim that $x(t) \ne x(t')$ for all distinct $t, t'$. Suppose that
$x(t) = x(t') = x$. If $t \ne t_1$ and $t'$ is in the component of $T \setminus e_t$ that contains
$p(t)$, then $p(t)$ is on the path of $T$ between $t$ and $t'$, and so $x \in V(\tau(p(t)))$ by
(iii), contrary to the choice of $x(t)$. Similarly it is impossible that $t' \ne t_1$ and $t$ is in
the component of $T \setminus e_{t'}$ that contains $p(t')$. If $t = t_1$, then $t' \ne t_1$, and
$t = t_1$ is in the component of $T \setminus e_{t'}$ that contains $p(t')$, by the choice of
$p(t')$, which we have seen is impossible. So $t \ne t_1$, and similarly $t' \ne t_1$. Hence $t'$ is
in the component of $T \setminus e_t$ that contains $t$, and $t$ is in the component of
$T \setminus e_{t'}$ that contains $t'$. In particular $e_t \ne e_{t'}$, since otherwise
$t' = p(t)$. The component of $T \setminus e_t$ that contains $p(t)$ also contains $t_1$, by the
choice of $p(t)$, and it does not contain $t'$. So it does not contain $e_{t'}$, and $p(t)$ and
$t_1$ are in the same component of $T \setminus e_{t'}$. Since $t'$ and $t$ are in the same
component of $T \setminus e_{t'}$, and $t$ and $p(t)$ are joined by $e_t \ne e_{t'}$, it follows
that $t'$ and $t_1$ are in the same component of $T \setminus e_{t'}$. This is contrary to the
choice of $p(t')$, which puts $t'$ and $t_1$ in different components of $T \setminus e_{t'}$. This
proves our claim. Hence $n = |V(T)| \le |V(G)|$, and $|E(T)| = n - 1 \le |V(G)| - 1$.
