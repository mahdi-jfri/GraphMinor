N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. XIII. The disjoint paths problem*, J. Combin. Theory
Ser. B **63** (1995), 65–110.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM13/GM13.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM13/GM13.pdf) in the

## 1. Proof of (2.1), page 69: the folio relative to a subset

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "We shall need some easy observations on folio management, the
proofs of which we leave to the reader:" and states (2.1) and (2.2) without proof. For (2.1) it
remains to show how the $\delta$-folio relative to $Z'$ is read off from the $\delta$-folio relative
to $Z$.

**Proof.** *Proof of (2.1).* Let $k = |Z|$ and $k' = |Z'|$, and let $Z = \{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$, where
$Z' = \{v_1, \dots, v_{k'}\}$. Let $(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$ be a rooted digraph with detail
$\le \delta$. We say that a rooted digraph $(H^+, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ *extends* it if $H$ is a
subdigraph of $H^+$ with $E(H^+) = E(H)$, and every vertex of $H^+$ not in $V(H)$ is one of
$u_{k'+1}, \dots, u_k$. Such a rooted digraph has detail $\le \delta$, because it has the same edges
as $H$, and each of its vertices that is not a root is a vertex of $H$ different from
$u_1, \dots, u_{k'}$. We claim that $(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$ is a minor of
$(G, v_1, \dots, v_{k'})$ if and only if some rooted digraph that extends it is a minor of
$(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$. Then the $\delta$-folio of $(G, v_1, \dots, v_{k'})$ consists of the rooted
digraphs with detail $\le \delta$ that are extended by some member of the $\delta$-folio of
$(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$, and (2.1) follows.

To prove the claim, let $\phi$ be a model in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$ of a rooted digraph that extends
$(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$. Then the restriction of $\phi$ to $V(H) \cup E(H)$ is a model of
$(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$ in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_{k'})$. Conversely, let $\phi$ be a model of
$(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$ in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_{k'})$. For $k' < i \le k$, if $v_i \in V(\phi(u))$
for some $u \in V(H)$ let $u_i = u$, and otherwise let $u_i$ be a new vertex and let $\phi(u_i)$ be
the subgraph of $G$ with vertex set $\{v_i\}$ and no edges. Let $H^+$ be obtained from $H$ by adding
the new vertices. Then $(H^+, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ extends $(H, u_1, \dots, u_{k'})$, and $\phi$ is a
model of it in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$, because the vertices $v_i$ with $u_i$ new are distinct and
belong to no $\phi(u)$ with $u \in V(H)$. This proves (2.1).

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_1` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_1_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Section2.lean`. The claim is
`GraphMinor.GM13.inDeltaFolio_iff_exists_isRootedExtension`, with the extensions
`GraphMinor.GM13.IsRootedExtension`, in the same file.

## 2. Proof of (2.2), page 69: folios across a separation

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The same sentence leaves the proof of (2.2) to the reader. The proof needs a
description of the $\delta$-folio of $G$ in terms of those of $A$ and $B$, which the paper does not
give.

**Proof.** *Proof of (2.2).* Let $Z = \{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ with $k = |Z|$. Let $I_A$ be the set of
all $i$ ($1 \le i \le k$) with $v_i \in V(A)$, and define $I_B$ similarly. Thus every $i$ belongs to
$I_A$ or to $I_B$, and $i \in I_A \cap I_B$ if and only if $v_i \in V(A \cap B)$. Let
$(H, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ be a rooted digraph with detail $\le \delta$. A *split* of it consists of
rooted digraphs $(H_A, a_i : i \in I_A)$ and $(H_B, b_i : i \in I_B)$ with roots indexed by $I_A$
and $I_B$, and maps $\pi_A \colon V(H_A) \to V(H)$ and $\pi_B \colon V(H_B) \to V(H)$, such that

(a) $E(H)$ is the disjoint union of $E(H_A)$ and $E(H_B)$, and each $e \in E(H_A)$ with head $a$ and
tail $a'$ in $H_A$ has head $\pi_A(a)$ and tail $\pi_A(a')$ in $H$, and similarly for $H_B$

(b) $\pi_A(a_i) = u_i$ for $i \in I_A$, and $\pi_B(b_i) = u_i$ for $i \in I_B$

(c) for each $u \in V(H)$ the set $\pi_A^{-1}(u) \cup \pi_B^{-1}(u)$ is non-empty, and the graph
$K_u$ with this vertex set, in which $a_i$ and $b_i$ are adjacent for each $i \in I_A \cap I_B$ with
$u_i = u$, is connected.

We claim that $(H, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ is a minor of $(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$ if and only if it has a
split in which $(H_A, a_i : i \in I_A)$ and $(H_B, b_i : i \in I_B)$ have detail $\le \delta$ and
are minors of $(A, v_i : i \in I_A)$ and $(B, v_i : i \in I_B)$ respectively. This condition depends
only on the $\delta$-folios of $A$ relative to $Z \cap V(A)$ and of $B$ relative to $Z \cap V(B)$,
and so (2.2) follows from the claim.

Suppose first that there is such a split, and let $\phi_A, \phi_B$ be models of the two rooted
digraphs in $(A, v_i : i \in I_A)$ and $(B, v_i : i \in I_B)$. For $u \in V(H)$ let $\phi(u)$ be the
union of the graphs $\phi_A(a)$ ($a \in \pi_A^{-1}(u)$) and $\phi_B(b)$ ($b \in \pi_B^{-1}(u)$), and
for $e \in E(H)$ let $\phi(e) = \phi_A(e)$ if $e \in E(H_A)$ and $\phi(e) = \phi_B(e)$ if
$e \in E(H_B)$. By (c), $\phi(u)$ is non-null. It is connected, since its parts are connected, $K_u$
is connected, and $v_i \in V(\phi_A(a_i)) \cap V(\phi_B(b_i))$ for $i \in I_A \cap I_B$. Let
$u, w \in V(H)$ be distinct, and suppose that some $x$ belongs to $V(\phi(u)) \cap V(\phi(w))$.
Since the graphs $\phi_A(a)$ ($a \in V(H_A)$) are mutually disjoint, and so are the graphs
$\phi_B(b)$ ($b \in V(H_B)$), we may assume that $x \in V(\phi_A(a)) \cap V(\phi_B(b))$ where
$\pi_A(a) = u$ and $\pi_B(b) = w$. Then $x \in V(A \cap B) \subseteq Z$, and so $x = v_i$ for some
$i \in I_A \cap I_B$. Since $v_i \in V(\phi_A(a_i))$, it follows that $a = a_i$, and similarly
$b = b_i$. So $u = u_i = w$ by (b), a contradiction. Thus the graphs $\phi(u)$ are mutually
vertex-disjoint. The edges $\phi(e)$ are distinct, and none of them is an edge of any $\phi(u)$,
because $\phi_A$ and $\phi_B$ are models and $E(A \cap B) = \emptyset$. By (a), each $\phi(e)$ has
its head and tail in the right graphs $\phi(u)$, and $v_i \in V(\phi(u_i))$ for $1 \le i \le k$ by
(b). Hence $\phi$ is a model of $(H, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$.

Conversely, let $\phi$ be a model of $(H, u_1, \dots, u_k)$ in $(G, v_1, \dots, v_k)$. For
$u \in V(H)$, we call the components of the subgraph $(V(\phi(u)) \cap V(A), E(\phi(u)) \cap E(A))$
the *$A$-pieces* of $u$, and define the $B$-pieces of $u$ similarly. Let $H_A$ be the digraph whose
vertices are the $A$-pieces of all vertices of $H$, and whose edges are the edges $e \in E(H)$ with
$\phi(e) \in E(A)$, where such an $e$ has as head the $A$-piece containing the head of $\phi(e)$ and
as tail the $A$-piece containing the tail of $\phi(e)$. (If $u$ is the head of $e$ in $H$, the head
of $\phi(e)$ is in $V(\phi(u)) \cap V(A)$, and so it is in an $A$-piece of $u$.) For $i \in I_A$ let
$a_i$ be the $A$-piece of $u_i$ that contains $v_i$, and let $\pi_A$ map each $A$-piece of $u$ to
$u$. Define $H_B$, $b_i$ and $\pi_B$ similarly. Mapping each piece to itself and each edge $e$ to
$\phi(e)$ gives models of $(H_A, a_i : i \in I_A)$ in $(A, v_i : i \in I_A)$ and of
$(H_B, b_i : i \in I_B)$ in $(B, v_i : i \in I_B)$, and (a) and (b) hold by construction. For (c),
let $u \in V(H)$. Then $\phi(u)$ is non-null, and so $u$ has a piece. If an $A$-piece and a
$B$-piece of $u$ have a common vertex $x$, then $x \in V(A \cap B) \subseteq Z$, and so $x = v_i$
for some $i \in I_A \cap I_B$. Then $u = u_i$, since $v_i \in V(\phi(u_i))$, and the two pieces are
$a_i$ and $b_i$, which are adjacent in $K_u$. Since every edge of $\phi(u)$ is an edge of one piece,
no two $A$-pieces and no two $B$-pieces of $u$ have a common vertex, and $\phi(u)$ is connected, it
follows that $K_u$ is connected. It remains to show that $(H_A, a_i : i \in I_A)$ has detail
$\le \delta$ (and similarly for $H_B$). First, $|E(H_A)| \le |E(H)| \le \delta$. Now let $P$ be an
$A$-piece of some $u \in V(H)$ that is not a root of $H_A$. Then $V(P) \cap Z = \emptyset$, for if
$v_i \in V(P)$ then $i \in I_A$, $u = u_i$ and $P = a_i$. Let $f$ be an edge of $\phi(u)$ with an
end $x \in V(P)$. If $f \in E(B)$ then $x \in V(A \cap B) \subseteq Z$, which is impossible. So
$f \in E(A)$, and $f \in E(P)$. Since $\phi(u)$ is connected, it follows that $P = \phi(u)$.
Moreover $u$ is not a root of $H$, for if $u = u_i$ then $v_i \in V(\phi(u)) = V(P)$. Hence the
vertices of $H_A$ that are not roots are graphs $\phi(u)$ for distinct vertices $u$ of $H$ that are
not roots, and there are at most $\delta$ of them. This proves the claim, and hence (2.2).

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_2` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_2_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Section2.lean`. The splits are
`GraphMinor.GM13.IsSeparatedMinorSplit`, and the claim is
`GraphMinor.GM13.inDeltaFolio_iff_exists_isSeparatedMinorSplit`, with its two directions
`GraphMinor.GM13.exists_isSeparatedMinorSplit_of_inDeltaFolio` and
`GraphMinor.GM13.isRootedDigraphMinor_of_isSeparatedMinorSplit`, in the same file.

## 3. Proof of (2.3), page 70: irrelevance passes to $G$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "From (2.1) and (2.2), the following follows easily." and gives
no proof of (2.3). The proof applies (2.2) to a set larger than $Z$, and then (2.1).

**Proof.** To see this, let $v$ be irrelevant to the $\delta$-folio of $B$ relative to
$V(A \cap B)$. Then $v \notin V(A \cap B)$, and so $v \notin V(A)$, and $v \notin Z$ since
$Z \subseteq V(A)$. Let $Z' = Z \cup V(A \cap B)$. Now $(A, B \setminus v)$ is a separation of
$G \setminus v$, and $V(A \cap (B \setminus v)) = V(A \cap B) \subseteq Z'$. Since
$Z \subseteq V(A)$ and $v \notin Z'$, we have
$Z' \cap V(B) = Z' \cap V(B \setminus v) = V(A \cap B)$, and $Z' \cap V(A) = Z'$. By hypothesis the
$\delta$-folios of $B$ and $B \setminus v$ relative to $V(A \cap B)$ are equal. Hence by (2.2),
applied to the separations $(A, B)$ of $G$ and $(A, B \setminus v)$ of $G \setminus v$, the
$\delta$-folios of $G$ and $G \setminus v$ relative to $Z'$ are equal. By (2.1), their
$\delta$-folios relative to $Z$ are equal, and since $v \notin Z$, $v$ is irrelevant to the
$\delta$-folio of $G$ relative to $Z$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_3` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Results.lean`, proved by
`GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_2_3_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Section2.lean`.

## 4. Algorithm (3.3), step (3), page 73: the conditions (i)–(iii)

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After step (3) the paper says "It is easy to see that $G_{i+1}$, $T_{i+1}$,
$\tau_{i+1}$ satisfy (i), (ii), and (iii)." The bound on the orders of the two new edges of
$T_{i+1}$ in (iii) needs the choice of $g$ made in step (3).

**Proof.** To see this, (i) holds since $G_{i+1} = G_i$, and (ii) holds since $v$ has valency $3$ in
$T'$ and $v_1, v_2$ are leaves. For (iii), $\tau_{i+1}$ is a surjection, since
$\tau_{i+1}(g) = v_2$, and $\tau_{i+1}(e) = v_1$ for some $e \ne g$ because at least two edges $e$
of $G_i$ have $\tau_i(e) = v$. Each edge of $T'$ other than $vv_1$ and $vv_2$ divides $E(G_i)$ in
the same way as in $T_i$ (for $f$, the side containing $v$ still contains just the edges of $R$),
and so it has the same order. The edge $vv_2$ has order at most $2 \le 3\omega$, since only the ends
of $g$ can be counted. The edges $e$ with $\tau_{i+1}(e) = v_1$ are the edges of $R$ other than $g$,
and so each vertex counted in the order of $vv_1$ is incident with an edge of $R$ and with $g$ or an
edge of $R'$, that is, it is in $Z$ or it is an end of $g$. If $Z = \emptyset$, the order of $vv_1$
is therefore at most $2$. If $0 < |Z| < 3\omega$, then $g$ has an end in $Z$, so at most one end of
$g$ is outside $Z$, and the order of $vv_1$ is at most $|Z| + 1 \le 3\omega$.

## 5. Algorithm (3.3), step (6), page 74: deleting an edge

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** In step (6) the paper says "we leave as an exercise the fact that deleting an
edge reduces branch-width by at most one, except when branch-width $\le 2$ which does not occur here
since $\omega \ge 2$".

**Proof.** We give the details, in the form needed here. Suppose that $G_{i+1}$ has branch-width
$\le \omega - 1$. We shall obtain a contradiction with the fact that $G_i$ has branch-width
$> \omega$.

Suppose first that $\omega \ge 3$. Since $G_i$ has branch-width $\ge \omega + 1$ and every edge of
$G_i$ has at most $2 \le \omega + 1$ ends, by [19, Theorem (4.3)] (Graph Minors X, (4.3)) there is a
tangle in $G_i$ of order $\ge \omega + 1$. Its members of order $\le \omega$ form a tangle
$\mathscr{T}$ in $G_i$ of order $\omega + 1$. Let $u$ be an end of $g$. By [19, Theorem (8.5)]
applied to $\mathscr{T}$ with $W = \{u\}$, there is a tangle of order $\omega$ in $G_i \setminus u$.
Since $g$ is incident with $u$, the graph $G_i \setminus u$ is a subgraph of
$G_i \setminus g = G_{i+1}$, and so by [19, Theorem (6.1)] there is a tangle of order $\omega$ in
$G_{i+1}$. (This tangle consists of the separations $(A, B)$ of $G_{i+1}$ of order $< \omega$ such
that $(A \cap (G_i \setminus u), B \cap (G_i \setminus u))$ is in the tangle of $G_i \setminus u$.)
On the other hand, $G_{i+1}$ has an edge, since $G_i$ has branch-width $> 0$ and so has at least two
edges. Every edge of $G_{i+1}$ has at most $2 \le \omega - 1$ ends, and $G_{i+1}$ has branch-width
$\le \omega - 1$, and so by [19, Theorem (4.3)] there is no tangle in $G_{i+1}$ of order
$\ge \omega$. This is a contradiction.

Now suppose that $\omega = 2$, so that $G_{i+1}$ has branch-width $\le 1$. If $G_{i+1}$ has at most
one edge, then $G_i$ has at most two edges, and so it has branch-width $\le 2$, because a
branch-decomposition of $G_i$, if there is one, has a tree with only one edge, and this edge has
order at most $2$ since all the vertices it counts are ends of one edge of $G_i$. Thus we may assume
that $G_{i+1}$ has a branch-decomposition $(T, \tau)$ of width $\le 1$. Choose $e \in E(G_{i+1})$,
sharing an end with $g$ if some edge of $G_{i+1}$ does so. Let $T^+$ be the tree obtained from $T$
by adding two new vertices adjacent to the leaf $\tau(e)$, and let $\tau^+$ map $e$ and $g$ to these
two new vertices and agree with $\tau$ on the other edges of $G_i$. Then $(T^+, \tau^+)$ is a
branch-decomposition of $G_i$, since $\tau(e)$ now has valency $3$. The two new edges of $T^+$ have
order at most $2$, since each of them separates one edge of $G_i$ from the others. Every other edge
$f$ of $T^+$ is an edge of $T$, and it divides $E(G_i)$ as it divides $E(G_{i+1})$ in $T$, with $g$
added to the side that contains $e$. A vertex counted in the order of $f$ in $T^+$ but not in $T$ is
therefore an end of $g$ that is incident with no edge of $G_{i+1}$ on the side of $e$. If $e$ shares
an end with $g$, this end is not such a vertex, and so the order of $f$ grows by at most one. If no
edge of $G_{i+1}$ shares an end with $g$, then no end of $g$ is incident with an edge of $G_{i+1}$,
and the order of $f$ does not grow. Hence $(T^+, \tau^+)$ has width $\le 2$, and so $G_i$ has
branch-width $\le \omega$, a contradiction.

## 6. Proof of (4.3), step (1), page 77: too few parallel edges are kept

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** Here $H_1, \dots, H_k$ are the excluded minors of the class $\mathcal{F}$, and
$\delta = \max(\varepsilon(H_1), \dots, \varepsilon(H_k))$. Step (1) chooses a maximal subgraph
$G_1$ of $G$ "with at most $\delta$ loops at each vertex and with no $\delta + 1$ edges mutually
parallel", and says that $G_1 \in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $G \in \mathcal{F}$, "as is easily
seen". This is false, because a minor with $\delta$ edges may need $\delta + 1$ parallel edges of
$G$: one is contracted, and the other $\delta$ become loops. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the class of
graphs with no minor isomorphic to $H_1$, one vertex with two loops. This class is minor-closed,
$H_1$ is planar, and $\delta = 2$. Let $G$ be two vertices joined by three parallel edges.
Contracting one edge of $G$ gives $H_1$, so $G \notin \mathcal{F}$. But $G_1$ is two vertices joined
by two parallel edges. Every minor of $G_1$ has at most one loop, so $G_1 \in \mathcal{F}$.

**Correction.** Let $G_1$ have no $\delta + 2$ edges mutually parallel, that is, it keeps up to
$\delta + 1$. Then $G_1 \in \mathcal{F}$ if and only if $G \in \mathcal{F}$, for the following
reason. A model in $G$ of a graph with at most $\delta$ edges uses at most $\delta$ loops at each
vertex. Between two distinct vertices of $G$, it uses at most $\delta$ edges that represent edges of
the graph, and at most one more edge inside a branch set. So its edges can be chosen in $G_1$. The
same cap of $|E(H)| + 1$ parallel edges appears in Step (1) of (4.4) on the same page. The graph
$G_1$ still has $O(v^2)$ edges, so the running time is unchanged, and (4.3) holds as printed.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.Compute.isIsoMinor_capPair` and `GraphMinor.Compute.membership_capPair43` in
`GraphMinor/Compute/GM13PairCapMinor.lean`, and `GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_4_3` in
`GraphMinor/GM13/Results.lean`.

## 7. Claim (1) in the proof of (5.3), page 80: the subgraphs $H_1, \dots, H_\kappa$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The subproof of (1) ends "Then it is easy to see that $H_1, \dots, H_\kappa$
satisfy the theorem, a contradiction." It does not check that the $H_i$ are connected and disjoint,
or conditions (a) and (b).

**Proof.** To see this, we may assume that each $P_i$ has no vertex in $Z \cup Z'$ except its ends
(replacing it by a subpath if necessary). Since $|Z| = |Z'| = \xi$, each vertex of $Z$ and each
vertex of $Z'$ is an end of exactly one of $P_1, \dots, P_\xi$. Since $Z \subseteq V(A)$, we have
$Z \cap V(B) \subseteq Z'$. For $1 \le i \le \xi$, $H_i$ is connected, since $P_i$ and $H'_i$ are
connected and both contain the end $z_i$ of $P_i$ in $Z'$. The graphs $H_1, \dots, H_\kappa$ are
mutually vertex-disjoint, since the $P_i$ are disjoint subgraphs of $A$, the $H'_j$ are disjoint
subgraphs of $B$, and a vertex of $P_i$ in $V(B)$ is in $V(A \cap B) = Z'$ and so equals $z_i$,
which belongs to no $H'_j$ with $j \ne i$. For (a), if $1 \le i \le \xi$ then
$V(H'_i) \cap Z \subseteq V(H'_i) \cap Z' = \{z_i\} \subseteq V(P_i)$, and so
$V(H_i) \cap Z = V(P_i) \cap Z$, which has exactly one member. If $\xi + 1 \le i \le \kappa$, then
$V(H_i) \cap Z \subseteq V(H'_i) \cap Z' = \emptyset$. For (b), every edge of $B$ is an edge of $G$,
so if there is no edge of $G$ between $H_i$ and $H_j$ then there is none of $B$ between $H'_i$ and
$H'_j$, and so $i, j \le \mu$.

## 8. Section 7, page 89: a wall from a model

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "since every vertex of an elementary wall $H$ of height $h$ has
valency $\le 3$, if a graph has a minor isomorphic to $H$ then it has a wall of height $h$ as a
subgraph (and algorithmically, it is easy to see how to construct the wall in time $O(\varepsilon)$,
given a model of $H$)". It gives neither the construction nor its running time.

**Proof.** To see both statements, let $\phi$ be a model of $H$ in the graph. Let $u \in V(H)$, and
let $t_1, \dots, t_k$ be the ends in $V(\phi(u))$ of the edges $\phi(e)$, one for each edge $e$ of
$H$ incident with $u$. Thus $k \le 3$, and $t_1, \dots, t_k$ need not be distinct. Take a
breadth-first search tree of $\phi(u)$ from $t_1$, and let $Q_2, Q_3$ be the paths of this tree from
$t_2, t_3$ to $t_1$ (if $k = 2$, let $t_3 = t_1$, so that $Q_3$ is the one-vertex path $t_1$). Let
$b_u$ be the first vertex of $Q_3$ that belongs to $Q_2$. Since both paths run to the root of the
tree, $Q_3$ follows $Q_2$ from $b_u$ on, and so $\phi(u)$ contains three paths from $b_u$ to
$t_1, t_2, t_3$, mutually disjoint except for $b_u$. For each edge $e = uw$ of $H$, the path from
$b_u$ to the end of $\phi(e)$ in $\phi(u)$, the edge $\phi(e)$, and the path from the end of
$\phi(e)$ in $\phi(w)$ to $b_w$ form a path $P_e$ between $b_u$ and $b_w$. Since the graphs
$\phi(u)$ are mutually disjoint, the paths $P_e$ ($e \in E(H)$) are mutually disjoint except for
their ends, and so their union is a subdivision of $H$, that is, a wall of height $h$ whose corners
are the vertices $b_u$ for the corners $u$ of $H$. Each search takes time linear in
$|V(\phi(u))| + |E(\phi(u))|$, and the graphs $\phi(u)$ are disjoint, so the wall is found in time
$O(\varepsilon)$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.Compute.modelToWall` and `GraphMinor.Compute.isValid_modelToWall` in
`GraphMinor/Compute/Algorithm72Wall.lean`. The formalization proves a running time of $O(v^2)$ for
its program, which is enough for (7.2), whose running time is $O(v^2 + \varepsilon)$. The bound
$O(\varepsilon)$ above follows from the linear time of breadth-first search.

**Proof.** We prove (7.3). Let $k = r + 1$, so that $h \ge 2k + 2$. We need a subgraph $H'$ of $H$
which is a wall of height $h - 2k$, such that $d' \ge d - 4k$ and $d(u, v) \ge k$ for every vertex
$u$ of $H'$. The distances $d$ and $d'$ depend only on the drawings of $H$ and $H'$ as subsets of
the plane. A homeomorphism of the plane maps subsets homeomorphic to $[0, 1]$ onto such subsets, and
so applying it to the drawing of $H$ changes neither the inequality $d' \ge d - 4k$ nor the numbers
$d(u, v)$. Hence we may assume that $H$ is drawn exactly as in Fig. 1, in the coordinates below.

We proceed as follows. In (1) and (2) we express $d$ through the regions of $H$. In (6) we give a
test which shows $d' \ge d - 2c$ for a subwall $H'$, by comparing the regions of $H'$ with those of
$H$. Then we build three kinds of subwalls and check the test for each: a copy of a smaller wall
moved inside $H$, used when $v$ is near a side of $H$, a wall with a small hole, used when $r = 0$,
and a wall with a large hole around $v$, used when $r \ge 1$ and $v$ is far from the sides.

The elementary wall of height $h$ has as vertices the integer points $(x, y)$ with
$0 \le x \le 2h + 1$ and $0 \le y \le h$, other than $(0, 0)$ and $(2h + 1, h)$. Its *horizontal*
edges join $(x, y)$ and $(x + 1, y)$, and its *vertical* edges join $(x, y)$ and $(x, y + 1)$ when
$x + y$ is odd. Each edge is drawn as the straight segment between its ends, and this is the drawing
of Fig. 1. We say that $(x, y)$ is in *row* $y$ and *column* $x$, and that the horizontal edge from
$(x, y)$ to $(x + 1, y)$ and the vertical edge from $(x, y)$ to $(x, y + 1)$ are *at* $(x, y)$. The
vertices and the edges of the elementary wall are its *elements*, where an edge, as an element, is
the open segment without its ends. The *midpoint* of an element is the vertex itself or the middle
of the segment. The wall $H$ is drawn as the elementary wall, with the vertices of the subdivision
placed on the segments, and so every point of the drawing of $H$ lies in exactly one element. Each
wall $H' \subseteq H$ that we construct has its branch vertices at vertices of the elementary wall,
and its drawing is a union of elements. We say that $H'$ *keeps* the elements in its drawing and
*deletes* the others.

For integers $x, y$ the *square* $(x, y)$ is the open unit square $(x, x + 1) \times (y, y + 1)$,
and the *strip* $y$ is the set of points whose second coordinate lies strictly between $y$ and
$y + 1$. A *position* is a pair $(X, Y)$ of integers with $X + Y$ even. The position of the square
$(x, y)$ is $(x, y)$ if $x + y$ is even, and $(x + 1, y)$ if $x + y$ is odd. A position $(X, Y)$ is
a *brick of height* $h$ if $0 \le Y \le h - 1$ and $1 \le X \le 2h$. In this case there is no
vertical edge at $(X, Y)$, since $X + Y$ is even, and so the open rectangle
$(X - 1, X + 1) \times (Y, Y + 1)$ is a region of $H$. These regions are the bricks of Fig. 1, and
we call this region the brick $(X, Y)$ as well. It consists of the two squares with position
$(X, Y)$, namely $(X - 1, Y)$ and $(X, Y)$, and the open segment between them. Hence the regions of
$H$ are the bricks of height $h$ and the infinite region, and each square lies in the brick of its
position if this position is a brick of height $h$, and in the infinite region otherwise. The
perimeter of $H$ consists of the points of the drawing with first coordinate at most $1$ or at least
$2h$, or with second coordinate $0$ or $h$.

A region is *at* a point $s$ if $s$ is in its closure. The regions at a point of an element are the
regions containing the squares whose closures contain the midpoint of the element. We call these
squares the squares *at* the element, and these regions the regions at the element. If the midpoint
is $(p, q)$, the squares at the element are the squares $(u, w)$ with
$\lceil p \rceil - 1 \le u \le \lfloor p \rfloor$ and
$\lceil q \rceil - 1 \le w \le \lfloor q \rfloor$. So their positions $(X, Y)$ satisfy
$|X - p| \le 1$ and $q - 1 \le Y \le q$. Moreover, some square at the element has a position with
$X \le p - \frac{1}{2}$, some has $X \ge p + \frac{1}{2}$, some has $Y \le q - \frac{1}{2}$ and some
has $Y \ge q - \frac{1}{2}$. For the last two take $w = \lceil q \rceil - 1$ and
$w = \lfloor q \rfloor$. For the first two, at a vertex $(x, y)$ one of the squares $(x - 1, y - 1)$
and $(x - 1, y)$ has position $x - 1$, and one of $(x, y - 1)$ and $(x, y)$ has position $x + 1$. At
the horizontal edge at $(x, y)$ the squares $(x, y - 1)$ and $(x, y)$ have positions $x$ and $x + 1$
in some order, and at the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ the squares $(x - 1, y)$ and $(x, y)$ have
positions $x - 1$ and $x + 1$. For every element at $(x, y)$, the square $(x, y)$ is at it.

For a pair $w = (w_1, w_2)$ of integers let $\|w\| = \max(|w_2|, \frac{1}{2}(|w_1| + |w_2|))$. Each
of the two terms of this maximum is unchanged when $w$ is replaced by $-w$, and its value at
$w + w'$ is at most the sum of its values at $w$ and at $w'$. So $\|{-w}\| = \|w\|$ and
$\|w + w'\| \le \|w\| + \|w'\|$. If $w$ is the difference of two positions then $w_1 + w_2$ is even,
and so $\|w\|$ is an integer. For a position $F = (X, Y)$ let

$$
\sigma_h(F) = \min\left(Y + 1,\ h - Y,\ \lfloor (X + 1)/2 \rfloor,\ \lfloor (2h + 2 - X)/2 \rfloor\right).
$$

We call the four numbers in this minimum the *bottom*, *top*, *left* and *right terms* of $F$, for
height $h$. By (2) below, $\|F - G\|$ is the number of steps between touching bricks from $F$ to
$G$, and $\sigma_h(F)$ is the number of steps from $F$ to the infinite region. If $F$ is a brick of
height $h$, each term is at least $1$, and $\sigma_h(F) = 1$ exactly when $Y \in \{0, h - 1\}$ or
$X \in \{1, 2, 2h - 1, 2h\}$, that is, exactly when the closure of $F$ meets the perimeter. If $F$
is not a brick of height $h$, then one of its terms is at most $0$, and so $\sigma_h(F) \le 0$. We
use two simple facts. First, for integers $A \ge B$ we have
$\lfloor A/2 \rfloor - \lfloor B/2 \rfloor \le \lceil (A - B)/2 \rceil$, since
$\lfloor A/2 \rfloor \le A/2$ and $\lfloor B/2 \rfloor \ge (B - 1)/2$. Second, to show that
$\sigma_h(F) \le \sigma_{h'}(F') + c$ for positions $F, F'$ and heights $h, h'$, it is enough to
show that each term of $F'$ for height $h'$, plus $c$, is at least some term of $F$ for height $h$.

Let $J$ be a graph drawn in the plane. Two regions of $J$ *touch* if their closures have a common
point. For regions $R, R'$ let $D_J(R, R')$ be the least $n \ge 0$ such that there are regions
$R = R_0, R_1, \dots, R_n = R'$ with $R_{i-1}$ touching $R_i$ for $1 \le i \le n$. Thus $D_J$
satisfies the triangle inequality. We define $d_J$ from the drawing of $J$ in the same way as $d$ is
defined from the drawing of $H$. The *depth* of a region $R$ is $D_J(R, R_\infty)$, where $R_\infty$
is the infinite region. For a point $s$ let $[s] = 1$ if $s$ is a point of the drawing, and
$[s] = 0$ otherwise.

(1) *For distinct points $s, t$ we have $d_J(s, t) = [s] + [t] + \min D_J(R, R')$, where the minimum
is over all regions $R$ at $s$ and $R'$ at $t$.*

Let $F$ be as in the definition of $d_J$, with the fewest points in the drawing. Since there is such
a set with finitely many points in the drawing, $F$ has finitely many. Between two consecutive ones,
$F$ runs inside one region, and the first and last of these regions are at $s$ and at $t$. At each
point of the drawing other than $s$ and $t$, $F$ passes from one region into a region that touches
it (or into the same region). So the regions met by $F$, in order, give a sequence of touching
regions from a region at $s$ to a region at $t$, with at most as many steps as $F$ has points in the
drawing other than $s$ and $t$. This proves "$\ge$". Conversely, choose a region $R$ at $s$, a
region $R'$ at $t$, and regions $R = R_0, \dots, R_n = R'$ as above, with $n$ as small as possible,
and for $1 \le i \le n$ choose a point $x_i$ of the drawing in the closures of $R_{i-1}$ and $R_i$.
The regions $R_i$ are distinct, and the points $s, x_1, \dots, x_n, t$ are distinct, since otherwise
the sequence could be shortened (if $x_i = x_j$ with $i < j$, then $R_{i-1}$ touches $R_j$, and if
$x_1 = s$ then $R_1$ is at $s$). Join each two consecutive points of $s, x_1, \dots, x_n, t$ by an
arc inside the corresponding region $R_i$, apart from its ends. The union of these arcs is a set $F$
as in the definition, and it meets the drawing in $[s] + [t] + n$ points. This proves "$\le$", and
so proves (1).

(2) *Two distinct bricks $F, G$ of $H$ touch if and only if $\|F - G\| = 1$, and a brick $F$ touches
the infinite region if and only if $\sigma_h(F) = 1$. The depth of a brick $F$ is $\sigma_h(F)$, and
$D_H(F, G) = \min(\|F - G\|, \sigma_h(F) + \sigma_h(G))$ for all bricks $F, G$.*

Let $F = (X, Y)$ and $G = (X', Y')$. Their closures are the rectangles
$[X - 1, X + 1] \times [Y, Y + 1]$ and $[X' - 1, X' + 1] \times [Y', Y' + 1]$, which meet if and
only if $|X - X'| \le 2$ and $|Y - Y'| \le 1$. Since $X - X'$ and $Y - Y'$ have the same parity and
$F \ne G$, this holds exactly when $(|X - X'|, |Y - Y'|)$ is $(2, 0)$ or $(1, 1)$, that is, when
$\|F - G\| = 1$. The closure of the infinite region is the infinite region together with the
perimeter, and so $F$ touches the infinite region exactly when the closure of $F$ meets the
perimeter, that is, when $\sigma_h(F) = 1$.

Let $F = R_0, R_1, \dots, R_n = R_\infty$ be touching regions with $n$ equal to the depth of $F$.
Then $R_1, \dots, R_{n-1}$ are bricks. Between touching bricks each term changes by at most $1$,
since the second coordinate changes by at most $1$ and the first by at most $2$, and so $\sigma_h$
changes by at most $1$. Since $R_{n-1}$ touches the infinite region, $\sigma_h(R_{n-1}) = 1$, and so
$\sigma_h(F) \le n$. Conversely, a brick $(X, Y)$ with $Y \ge 1$ touches the brick $(X - 1, Y - 1)$
or the brick $(X + 1, Y - 1)$, one of which is a brick of height $h$ since $1 \le X \le 2h$. So $Y$
such steps lead from $F$ to a brick in row $0$, which touches the infinite region, and the depth of
$F$ is at most $Y + 1$. In the same way, going up, the depth is at most $h - Y$. Going left or right
by steps that change the first coordinate by $2$, until it is in $\{1, 2\}$ or in $\{2h - 1, 2h\}$,
shows that the depth is at most $\lfloor (X + 1)/2 \rfloor$ and at most
$\lfloor (2h + 2 - X)/2 \rfloor$. Hence the depth of $F$ is $\sigma_h(F)$.

Now let $F = R_0, \dots, R_n = G$ be touching regions with $n = D_H(F, G)$. If one of them is the
infinite region, then $n \ge \sigma_h(F) + \sigma_h(G)$, by the depths. Otherwise they are all
bricks, and $n \ge \|F - G\|$ by the triangle inequality, since $\|R_i - R_{i-1}\| = 1$. Conversely,
$D_H(F, G) \le \sigma_h(F) + \sigma_h(G)$, by passing through the infinite region. Moreover
$D_H(F, G) \le \|F - G\|$. For let $w = G - F$. If $|w_1| \ge |w_2|$, take $|w_2|$ steps that add
$(\pm 1, \pm 1)$ and then $\frac{1}{2}(|w_1| - |w_2|)$ steps that add $(\pm 2, 0)$, with all signs
towards $G$. If $|w_1| < |w_2|$, take $|w_2|$ steps that add $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$, all with the second
sign towards $G$. The first $|w_1|$ of them have the first sign towards $G$, and the remaining ones
(an even number) alternately move the first coordinate away from $X'$ and back, to the side of $X'$
on which it stays between $1$ and $2h$. In both cases all the positions passed are bricks, since
their coordinates stay in the ranges of bricks, and the number of steps is $\|F - G\|$. This proves
(2).

We shall use (2) in the following form. If $R$ and $R'$ are the regions of $H$ that contain squares
with positions $F$ and $G$, then $R$ has depth $\max(\sigma_h(F), 0)$, and

$$
D_H(R, R') \ge \min(\|F - G\|, \text{depth}(R) + \text{depth}(R')).
$$

If $R$ and $R'$ are bricks this is (2). If one of them is the infinite region, then $D_H(R, R')$ is
the sum of the depths, and the infinite region has depth $0$ while $\sigma_h(F) \le 0$ if $F$ is not
a brick.

We use the following form of a theorem of Whitney (H. Whitney, Congruent graphs and the connectivity
of graphs, *Amer. J. Math.* **54** (1932), 150–168): if two drawings in a sphere are
subdivisions of the same simple 3-connected graph, then some homeomorphism of the sphere maps the
first onto the second, each branch vertex to the corresponding branch vertex, and each subdivided
edge onto the corresponding subdivided edge. For a wall $W$ of height $h'$, the *side vertices* of
$W$ are its branch vertices that correspond to the vertices $(x, y)$ of the elementary wall with
$y \in \{0, h'\}$ or $x \in \{0, 2h' + 1\}$.

(3) *Let $W$ be a wall of height $h' \ge 2$ drawn in the plane, and suppose that for each side
vertex $w$ there is a half-line starting at $w$ that meets the drawing of $W$ only in $w$, such that
these half-lines are mutually disjoint. Then some homeomorphism of the plane maps the drawing of the
elementary wall of height $h'$ as in Fig. 1 onto the drawing of $W$, each vertex to the
corresponding branch vertex, and each edge onto the corresponding path.*

Let $W^+$ be the graph obtained from the elementary wall of height $h'$ by adding a vertex $z$
adjacent to all side vertices. Add a point $\infty$ to the plane, to obtain a sphere. With $z$ drawn
at $\infty$ and its edges drawn along the given half-lines, the drawing of $W$ becomes a drawing in
the sphere of a subdivision of $W^+$. The drawing of Fig. 1 gives another one, with half-lines going
down from the vertices in row $0$, up from the vertices in row $h'$, left from the vertices $(0, y)$
and right from the vertices $(2h' + 1, y)$ with $0 < y < h'$. These half-lines meet the drawing only
in their starts, and they are mutually disjoint. We show below that $W^+$ is 3-connected. It is
simple, and so by Whitney's theorem some homeomorphism of the sphere maps the second drawing onto
the first, with each branch vertex and each subdivided edge going to the corresponding one. It maps
$\infty$ to $\infty$, since $\infty$ is the point of $z$ in both drawings, and so it restricts to
the required homeomorphism of the plane.

It remains to show that $W^+ - X$ is connected for every set $X$ of at most two vertices of $W^+$.
If $z \in X$, then $W^+ - X$ is the elementary wall minus at most one vertex, which is connected
since the elementary wall is 2-connected. (It is the union of the circuits around its bricks, and
these can be ordered so that each shares an edge with an earlier one, row by row from the bottom and
from left to right in each row. Adding a circuit that shares at least two vertices with a
2-connected graph gives a 2-connected graph.) If $z \notin X$, it is enough to show that every
vertex $w \notin X$ of the elementary wall is joined by a path of $W^+ - X$ to a side vertex, since
the side vertices are adjacent to $z$. If $w$ is a side vertex this holds trivially. Otherwise
$w = (x, y)$ with $0 < x < 2h' + 1$ and $0 < y < h'$, and three paths join $w$ to side vertices and
meet only in $w$: the path along row $y$ to $(0, y)$, the path along row $y$ to $(2h' + 1, y)$, and
a staircase. If $x + y$ is odd the staircase is
$(x, y), (x, y + 1), (x + 1, y + 1), (x + 1, y + 2), \dots$, and if $x + y$ is even it is
$(x, y), (x, y - 1), (x - 1, y - 1), (x - 1, y - 2), \dots$, in both cases stopped at the first side
vertex. These are paths of the wall, since each vertical step starts at a vertex with odd coordinate
sum (going up) or ends at one (going down), and before the first side vertex all the vertices passed
are inside the wall. The staircase leaves row $y$ at its first step. At least one of the three paths
avoids $X$, since $|X| \le 2$ and $w \notin X$. This proves (3).

If (3) applies to a wall $H' \subseteq H$ of height $h'$, we name the regions of $H'$ by the regions
of the elementary wall of height $h'$ drawn as in Fig. 1, using the homeomorphism. Thus the regions
of $H'$ are named by the bricks of height $h'$ and by the infinite region. The region named by the
brick $B'$ is the inside of the circuit $C(B')$ of $H'$ that corresponds to the circuit around $B'$,
and the infinite region of $H'$ is named by the infinite region. A homeomorphism preserves touching,
and so (2) for height $h'$ gives $D_{H'}$ and the depths in $H'$. Moreover every point of the
drawing of $H'$ is at two different regions of $H'$, since this is true for the drawing of Fig. 1.
Each region $F$ of $H$ is connected and disjoint from the drawing of $H'$, and so it lies in a
region $\pi(F)$ of $H'$. In particular $\pi(R_\infty)$ is the infinite region of $H'$, since
$R_\infty$ is unbounded.

All our walls $H' \subseteq H$ of height $h'$ have the following form. There are integers
$R(0) < R(1) < \dots < R(h')$ such that the branch vertex of $H'$ corresponding to $(x, y)$ is a
vertex of $H$ in row $R(y)$, whose column increases with $x$. Each horizontal edge of $H'$ is the
path of $H$ along a row between its ends. The vertical edges of $H'$ are mutually disjoint paths of
$H$. The vertical edge of $H'$ at $(x, y)$ meets rows $R(y)$ and $R(y + 1)$ of $H$ only in its ends,
and it contains exactly one vertical edge of $H$ in each strip $t$ with $R(y) \le t < R(y + 1)$, and
no other vertical edges of $H$. We write $c_t(x)$ for the column of this vertical edge of $H$ in
strip $t$. Since the rows $R(y)$ are distinct, the columns of the branch vertices increase along
each row, and each vertical edge of $H'$ lies between the heights of the two rows it joins and meets
these rows only in its ends, these paths of $H$ meet only in common ends, and no branch vertex is an
inner vertex of one of them. So $H'$ is a wall of height $h'$. Moreover (3) applies to $H'$, with
half-lines going down from its branch vertices in row $0$, up from those in row $h'$, left from the
first and right from the last branch vertex of every other row. For the drawing of $H'$ lies between
the horizontal lines at heights $R(0)$ and $R(h')$, and its points at height $R(y)$ are the points
of the path of $H$ along row $R(y)$ between the first and the last branch vertex of row $y$.

Finally, in each of our walls there are an integer $c_0$, and for every strip $t$ integers $y_t$ and
$a_t \le a'_t$ with $a_t \equiv a'_t \equiv t - y_t \pmod 2$, such that the following holds. If
$t < R(0)$ then $y_t < 0$, and if $t \ge R(h')$ then $y_t \ge h'$. If $R(0) \le t < R(h')$, then
$R(y_t) \le t < R(y_t + 1)$, and $c_t(x) = x + a_t$ if $x \le c_0 - 1$ and $c_t(x) = x + a'_t$ if
$x \ge c_0$, for every $x$ such that $H'$ has a vertical edge at $(x, y_t)$.

(4) *Let $H'$ be a wall of this form, and let $F = (X, Y)$ be a brick of $H$. Let $z = X - a_Y$ if
$X - a_Y \le c_0 - 1$, let $z = X - a'_Y$ if $X - a'_Y \ge c_0$, and let $z = c_0 - 1$ otherwise.
Let $P(F)$ be the position of the square $(z, y_Y)$. Then $\pi(F)$ is the region of $H'$ named by
$P(F)$ if $P(F)$ is a brick of height $h'$, and the infinite region of $H'$ otherwise. Moreover
$F - P(F) = (\Delta X, Y - y_Y)$, where $\Delta X = a_Y$ in the first case, $\Delta X = a'_Y$ in the
second case, and $\Delta X \in \{X - c_0, X - c_0 + 1\}$ in the third case. In all cases
$a_Y \le \Delta X \le a'_Y$.*

The first two cases cannot both hold, since $a_Y \le a'_Y$. Let $s$ be the square $(X, Y)$, so that
$s \subseteq F$ and $\pi(F)$ is the region of $H'$ that contains $s$. Let $y = y_Y$, and let $x$ be
the integer with $x + y$ odd and $x + 1 \in \{z, z + 1\}$. Thus $P(F) = (x + 1, y)$. We first prove
the last two statements. Since $X + Y$ and $x + 1 + y$ are even,
$\Delta X = X - x - 1 \equiv Y - y \equiv a_Y \equiv a'_Y \pmod 2$. In the first case $X - z = a_Y$
and $\Delta X \in \{a_Y, a_Y - 1\}$, so $\Delta X = a_Y$ by parity. In the same way
$\Delta X = a'_Y$ in the second case. In the third case $\Delta X \in \{X - c_0, X - c_0 + 1\}$
since $z = c_0 - 1$, and $X - a_Y \ge c_0$ and $X - a'_Y \le c_0 - 1$, and so
$a_Y \le \Delta X \le a'_Y$.

If $Y < R(0)$ or $Y \ge R(h')$, then the horizontal line through the centre of $s$ is disjoint from
the drawing of $H'$. This line and $s$ form an unbounded connected set disjoint from the drawing of
$H'$, and so $s$ lies in the infinite region of $H'$. Moreover $y < 0$ or $y \ge h'$, and so $P(F)$
is not a brick of height $h'$.

Now let $R(0) \le Y < R(h')$. Then $R(y) \le Y < R(y + 1)$, and so $0 \le y \le h' - 1$. Let $\ell$
be the half-line going left from the centre $(X + \frac{1}{2}, Y + \frac{1}{2})$ of $s$. For a brick
$B' = (x' + 1, y')$ of height $h'$, the circuit $C(B')$ consists of the vertical edges of $H'$ at
$(x', y')$ and $(x' + 2, y')$ and of parts of rows $R(y')$ and $R(y' + 1)$ of $H$. The half-line
$\ell$ meets $C(B')$ only in vertical edges of $H$ in strip $Y$, and it crosses each of them whose
column is at most $X$. If $y' \ne y$, then $C(B')$ contains no vertical edge of $H$ in strip $Y$. If
$y' = y$, then the vertical edges of $H$ in strip $Y$ in $C(B')$ are in columns
$c_Y(x') < c_Y(x' + 2)$. A point not on a closed polygon is inside it if and only if a half-line
from the point that meets the polygon only in crossings of its segments crosses it an odd number of
times. Hence $s$ lies in the region named by $(x' + 1, y)$ if $c_Y(x') \le X < c_Y(x' + 2)$, and if
there is no such $x'$, then $s$ lies in the infinite region of $H'$.

For every integer $w$ let $g(w) = w + a_Y$ if $w \le c_0 - 1$, and $g(w) = w + a'_Y$ if $w \ge c_0$.
Then $g$ is strictly increasing, since $a_Y \le a'_Y$, and $c_Y(w) = g(w)$ whenever $H'$ has a
vertical edge at $(w, y)$, that is, when $0 \le w \le 2h' + 1$ and $w + y$ is odd. We claim that
$g(x) \le X < g(x + 2)$. In the first case $x \le z \le c_0 - 1$, so
$g(x) = x + a_Y \le z + a_Y = X$, and $g(x + 2) \ge x + 2 + a_Y > z + a_Y = X$. In the second case
$x + 2 > z \ge c_0$, so $g(x + 2) = x + 2 + a'_Y > z + a'_Y = X$, and
$g(x) \le x + a'_Y \le z + a'_Y = X$. In the third case $c_0 - 2 \le x \le c_0 - 1$, so
$g(x) = x + a_Y < c_0 + a_Y \le X$ and $g(x + 2) = x + 2 + a'_Y \ge c_0 + a'_Y > X$. This proves the
claim.

If $x \ge 0$ and $x + 2 \le 2h' + 1$, then $c_Y(x) = g(x)$ and $c_Y(x + 2) = g(x + 2)$, since
$x + y$ is odd. So $s$ lies in the region named by $(x + 1, y) = P(F)$, which is a brick of height
$h'$. If $x < 0$, then every $w$ such that $H'$ has a vertical edge at $(w, y)$ satisfies
$w \ge x + 2$, since $w \ge 0$ and $w \equiv x \pmod 2$. So $c_Y(w) = g(w) \ge g(x + 2) > X$, and
$s$ lies in the infinite region of $H'$. In this case $x + 1 \le 0$, and $P(F)$ is not a brick of
height $h'$. If $x + 2 > 2h' + 1$, then every such $w$ satisfies $w \le x$, since
$w \le 2h' + 1 \le x + 1$ and $w \equiv x \pmod 2$. So $c_Y(w) = g(w) \le g(x) \le X$, and again $s$
lies in the infinite region. In this case $x + 1 \ge 2h' + 1$, and $P(F)$ is not a brick of height
$h'$. This proves (4).

For a brick $F$ of $H$ we call $F - P(F)$ the *move* of $F$. By (4), for each brick $F$ the depth of
$\pi(F)$ is at least $\sigma_{h'}(P(F))$. For if $P(F)$ is a brick of height $h'$, then $\pi(F)$ is
named by it and (2) for height $h'$ applies, and otherwise $\sigma_{h'}(P(F)) \le 0$.

(5) *Let $F$ be a brick of $H$ whose move $(\Delta X, \Delta Y)$ satisfies
$0 \le \Delta Y \le h - h'$ and $0 \le \Delta X \le 2(h - h')$. Then the bottom, top, left and right
terms of $F$ for height $h$ exceed those of $P(F)$ for height $h'$ by at most $\Delta Y$,
$h - h' - \Delta Y$, $\lceil \Delta X/2 \rceil$ and $\lceil (2(h - h') - \Delta X)/2 \rceil$,
respectively.*

Let $F = (X, Y)$, so $P(F) = (X - \Delta X, Y - \Delta Y)$. The bottom terms are $Y + 1$ and
$Y - \Delta Y + 1$, and the top terms are $h - Y$ and $h' - Y + \Delta Y$, which gives the first two
bounds. The left terms are $\lfloor A/2 \rfloor$ and $\lfloor B/2 \rfloor$ with $A = X + 1$ and
$B = A - \Delta X$, and the right terms are $\lfloor A/2 \rfloor$ and $\lfloor B/2 \rfloor$ with
$A = 2h + 2 - X$ and $B = 2h' + 2 - X + \Delta X = A - (2(h - h') - \Delta X)$. In both cases
$A \ge B$, and the first simple fact gives the last two bounds. This proves (5).

We call the four bounds in (5) the *drops* of the four terms. The following is the test mentioned
above.

(6) *Let $H' \subseteq H$ be a wall of height $h'$ to which (3) applies, and let $P$ map the bricks
of $H$ to positions, such that $\pi(F)$ is the region named by $P(F)$ if $P(F)$ is a brick of height
$h'$, and the infinite region otherwise. Let $c \ge 1$ be an integer such that*

(a) *$\|F - G\| \le \|P(F) - P(G)\| + 2c - 2$ for all bricks $F, G$ of $H$,*

(b) *$\sigma_h(F) \le \sigma_{h'}(P(F)) + c$ for every brick $F$ of $H$, and*

(c) *every element deleted by $H'$ is at a region $F$ of $H$ whose depth is at most the depth of
$\pi(F)$ plus $c - 1$.*

*Then $d' \ge d - 2c$.*

As above, the depth of $\pi(F)$ is at least $\sigma_{h'}(P(F))$ for every brick $F$. So by (b) every
region $F$ of $H$ has depth at most the depth of $\pi(F)$ plus $c$. For the infinite region this
holds since both depths are $0$. Let $s \ne t$ be points (for $s = t$ both distances are $0$), write
$[s]'$ for $[s]$ taken in $H'$, and choose regions $F', G'$ of $H'$ at $s, t$ such that
$d'(s, t) = [s]' + [t]' + D_{H'}(F', G')$, by (1) for $H'$. Since $F'$ is open and the drawing of
$H$ is a finite union of segments, $F'$ contains points arbitrarily close to $s$ that are not in the
drawing of $H$. There are finitely many regions of $H$, and so one region $F$ of $H$ contains such
points arbitrarily close to $s$. Then $F$ is at $s$, and $F \subseteq F'$ since $F$ is connected and
disjoint from the drawing of $H'$. If $s$ is a point of $H$ but not of $H'$, then a small disc
around $s$ meets no point of $H'$, since the drawing of $H'$ is closed. This disc lies in $F'$, and
so all the regions of $H$ at $s$ lie in $F'$. In this case $s$ lies in an element deleted by $H'$,
and by (c) we may choose $F$ with depth at most that of $F'$ plus $c - 1$. In every case
$[s] - [s]' \le 1$, and $[s] - [s]'$ plus the depth of $F$ is at most the depth of $F'$ plus $c$. If
$s$ is a point of $H$ but not of $H'$ this is the choice just made, and otherwise $[s] = [s]'$ and
it follows from the second sentence of this proof. Choose $G$ in the same way for $t$. By (1) for
$H$, $d(s, t) \le [s] + [t] + D_H(F, G)$. By (2) for $H'$, either $D_{H'}(F', G')$ is the sum of the
depths of $F'$ and $G'$, or $F', G'$ are named by bricks $B_1, B_2$ of height $h'$ and
$D_{H'}(F', G') = \|B_1 - B_2\|$. In the first case,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(s, t) &\le [s] + [t] + \text{depth}(F) + \text{depth}(G)\\
&\le [s]' + [t]' + \text{depth}(F') + \text{depth}(G') + 2c = d'(s, t) + 2c.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the second case $F, G$ are bricks, since $\pi$ maps the infinite region of $H$ to that of $H'$.
Moreover $\pi(F) = F'$ and $\pi(G) = G'$, so $P(F) = B_1$ and $P(G) = B_2$. By (2) for $H$ and by
(a),

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(s, t) &\le [s] + [t] + \|F - G\|\\
&\le ([s]' + 1) + ([t]' + 1) + \|B_1 - B_2\| + 2c - 2 = d'(s, t) + 2c.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves (6).

By (4), the map $P$ defined there satisfies the hypothesis of (6) for every wall of our form. We
check (a), (b), (c) as follows. If all the moves of bricks of $H$ satisfy the hypothesis of (5), and
all the drops are at most $c$, then (b) holds, by the second simple fact. For (c) and an element $m$
deleted by $H'$, it is enough to find a square at $m$ whose position $F$ is not a brick of height
$h$, or satisfies $\sigma_h(F) \le c - 1$, or satisfies $\sigma_h(F) \le \sigma_{h'}(P(F)) + c - 1$.
This is because the region of $H$ containing the square lies at $m$. It is the infinite region if
$F$ is not a brick of height $h$, and otherwise it is the brick $F$, of depth $\sigma_h(F)$, while
$\pi(F)$ has depth at least $\max(\sigma_{h'}(P(F)), 0)$. For the last inequality it is enough that
each term of $P(F)$, plus $c - 1$, is at least some term of $F$.

For (ii) we use (1) as follows. Let $m_v$ be the element of $H$ that contains $v$. The regions of
$H$ at $v$ are the regions at $m_v$, and similarly for every point of the drawing. Let $u$ be a
vertex of $H'$ with $u \ne v$, such that every region of $H$ at $u$ has distance $D_H$ at least
$k - 2$ from every region of $H$ at $v$. Then $d(u, v) \ge 1 + 1 + (k - 2) = k$ by (1). If $H'$
deletes $m_v$, then $u \ne v$ for every vertex $u$ of $H'$, since $u$ lies in an element kept by
$H'$.

We use the rotation $\rho$ of the plane given by $\rho(x, y) = (2h + 1 - x, h - y)$. Since $h$ is
even, $\rho$ maps each element of $H$ onto an element: the vertex $(x, y)$ to the vertex
$(2h + 1 - x, h - y)$, the horizontal edge at $(x, y)$ to the horizontal edge at $(2h - x, h - y)$,
and the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ to the vertical edge at $(2h + 1 - x, h - 1 - y)$, which exists
because $(2h + 1 - x) + (h - 1 - y) = 3h - x - y$ is odd. It maps the square $(x, y)$ to the square
$(2h - x, h - 1 - y)$, and the brick $(X, Y)$ to the brick $(2h + 1 - X, h - 1 - Y)$. We let $\rho$
act on all positions by this formula. It exchanges the bottom and top terms of positions, and the
left and right terms, and so it preserves $\sigma_h$, and also $\|F - G\|$ for positions $F, G$.
Since $\rho$ maps the drawing of $H$ onto itself, it maps regions of $H$ to regions of $H$ and
preserves touching and depths. Let $\rho'$ be defined in the same way with $h'$ in place of $h$. Let
$H' \subseteq H$ and $P$ be as in (6). Then the subgraph $\rho(H')$ of $H$, whose drawing is the
image under $\rho$ of the drawing of $H'$, is a wall of height $h'$ whose branch vertex
corresponding to $(x, y)$ is the image under $\rho$ of the branch vertex of $H'$ corresponding to
$\rho'(x, y)$. Composing the homeomorphism of (3) for $H'$ with $\rho$ and $\rho'$ shows that (3)
applies to $\rho(H')$, and that the map $F \mapsto \rho'(P(\rho(F)))$ has the property of $P$ in (6)
for $\rho(H')$. Moreover $\rho(H')$ deletes an element $m$ if and only if $H'$ deletes $\rho(m)$.
Hence if $H'$ satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) for some $c$, then so does $\rho(H')$. We call
$\rho(H')$ the *turned* $H'$.

We come to the subwalls. Let $h' = h - 2k$. Let $1 \le i \le 4k - 1$ and $1 \le j \le 2k - 1$ with
$i + j$ even. The *moved wall* $T(i, j)$ is the subgraph of $H$ whose drawing is the drawing of the
elementary wall of height $h'$, as in Fig. 1, translated by $(i, j)$. Each vertex $(x, y)$ of the
elementary wall of height $h'$ is translated to the vertex $(x + i, y + j)$ of $H$, since
$1 \le x + i \le 2h' + 1 + 4k - 1 = 2h$ and $1 \le y + j \le h' + 2k - 1 = h - 1$. Each vertical
edge is translated to a vertical edge of $H$, since $i + j$ is even. So $T(i, j)$ is a wall of our
form, with $R(y) = y + j$ and $c_t(x) = x + i$, and we take $c_0 = 0$ and $a_t = a'_t = i$ and
$y_t = t - j$ for every strip $t$. By (4), $P(F) = F - (i, j)$ for every brick $F$ of $H$. The wall
$T(i, j)$ keeps exactly the elements whose midpoint minus $(i, j)$ is the midpoint of an element of
the elementary wall of height $h'$. In particular, the midpoint $(p, q)$ of every element kept by
$T(i, j)$ satisfies $i \le p \le i + 2h' + 1$ and $j \le q \le j + h'$.

(7) *The moved wall $T(i, j)$ satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) with $c = 2k$, and so $d' \ge d - 4k$
for $H' = T(i, j)$.*

Since all moves equal $(i, j)$, we have $\|F - G\| = \|P(F) - P(G)\|$, which gives (a). By (5) with
$h - h' = 2k$, the drops are $j$, $2k - j$, $\lceil i/2 \rceil$ and $\lceil (4k - i)/2 \rceil$, all
at most $2k$. This gives (b). For (c), let $m$ be an element deleted by $T(i, j)$, with midpoint
$(p, q)$. Let the *infinite wall* have all integer points as vertices, with horizontal and vertical
edges defined as for the elementary wall. Translating by $-(i, j)$ maps $m$ to a vertex or edge of
the infinite wall, since $i + j$ is even. A vertex or edge of the infinite wall whose midpoint
$(p", q")$ satisfies $0 \le p" \le 2h' + 1$ and $0 < q" < h'$ is an element of the elementary wall
of height $h'$, since the vertices and edges of the infinite wall in this box that are missing from
the elementary wall have $q" \in \{0, h'\}$. Hence $q \le j$, or $q \ge j + h'$, or
$p \le i - \frac{1}{2}$, or $p \ge i + 2h' + \frac{3}{2}$, since $p$ is a multiple of $\frac{1}{2}$.
In the first case choose a square at $m$ whose position $F = (X, Y)$ has $Y \le q - \frac{1}{2}$, so
that $Y \le j - 1$. The bottom term of $F$ is $Y + 1 \le j \le 2k - 1$, and the second coordinate of
$P(F)$ is $Y - j < 0$. In the second case choose a square with $Y \ge q - \frac{1}{2}$, so that
$Y \ge j + h'$. The top term of $F$ is $h - Y \le 2k - j \le 2k - 1$, and the second coordinate of
$P(F)$ is at least $h'$. In the third case choose a square with $X \le p - \frac{1}{2} \le i - 1$.
The left term of $F$ is at most $\lfloor i/2 \rfloor \le 2k - 1$, and the first coordinate of $P(F)$
is negative. In the fourth case choose a square with $X \ge p + \frac{1}{2} \ge i + 2h' + 2$. The
right term of $F$ is at most
$\lfloor (2h - 2h' - i)/2 \rfloor = \lfloor (4k - i)/2 \rfloor \le 2k - 1$, and the first coordinate
of $P(F)$ is at least $2h' + 2$. In each case $\sigma_h(F) \le 2k - 1$, which gives (c). By (6),
$d' \ge d - 4k$. This proves (7).

Since the drawing of the elementary wall of height $h'$ is mapped onto itself by $\rho'$, the
rotation $\rho$ maps the drawing of $T(i, j)$ onto the drawing of $T(4k - i, 2k - j)$. Thus
$\rho(T(i, j)) = T(4k - i, 2k - j)$.

Next, let $k = 1$, so that $h' = h - 2$. We use a variant of the construction of Fig. 2, turned
upside down. Let $Y_0, c_0$ be integers with $1 \le Y_0 \le h'$ and $1 \le c_0 \le 2h' + 1$. The
wall $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is the subgraph of $H$ defined as follows. Its branch vertex corresponding to
$(x, y)$ is the vertex $(x, y)$ of $H$ if $y \le Y_0 - 1$ and $x \le c_0 - 1$, the vertex
$(x + 4, y)$ if $y \le Y_0 - 1$ and $x \ge c_0$, and the vertex $(x + 2, y + 2)$ if $y \ge Y_0$. Its
horizontal edges are the paths of $H$ along rows between their ends. Its vertical edge at $(x, y)$
is the vertical edge of $H$ at the vertex of $H$ corresponding to $(x, y)$, if $y \ne Y_0 - 1$. If
$y = Y_0 - 1$, then $x \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$, and the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ is a staircase made of
five edges of the elementary wall. If $x \le c_0 - 1$, it goes from $(x, Y_0 - 1)$ up to $(x, Y_0)$,
right to $(x + 1, Y_0)$, up to $(x + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, right to $(x + 2, Y_0 + 1)$, and up to
$(x + 2, Y_0 + 2)$. If $x \ge c_0$, it goes from $(x + 4, Y_0 - 1)$ up to $(x + 4, Y_0)$, left to
$(x + 3, Y_0)$, up to $(x + 3, Y_0 + 1)$, left to $(x + 2, Y_0 + 1)$, and up to $(x + 2, Y_0 + 2)$.
These vertical steps are edges, since they start at vertices with odd coordinate sum, as
$x \equiv Y_0$.

(8) *The wall $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is a wall of height $h'$ of our form, and every move is one of $(0, 0)$,
$(2, 0)$, $(4, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(3, 1)$, $(2, 2)$. It deletes the vertical edges at $(x, y)$ with
$y \le Y_0 - 1$ and $c_0 \le x \le c_0 + 3$. If $c_0 \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$, it also deletes the
vertices $(c_0, Y_0)$, $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, $(c_0 + 2, Y_0)$ and $(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, the horizontal
edges at $(c_0 - 1, Y_0)$, $(c_0, Y_0)$, $(c_0, Y_0 + 1)$ and $(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, and the vertical
edge at $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$. Every element $m$ it deletes, with midpoint $(p, q)$, is of one of the
following kinds:*

(i) *a vertical edge at $(x, y)$ with $y \le Y_0 - 1$ and $c_0 \le x \le c_0 + 3$,*

(ii) *an element with $Y_0 \le q \le Y_0 + 1$,*

(iii) *an element with $p < 2$, or $p > 2h - 1$, or $q = h$,*

(iv) *if $c_0 = 1$, a vertex $(x, 0)$ or a horizontal edge at $(x, 0)$ with $1 \le x \le 4$.*

The branch vertices are vertices of $H$, since $x + 4 \le 2h' + 5 = 2h + 1$ and
$y + 2 \le h' + 2 = h$, and the staircases use only the rows $Y_0, Y_0 + 1 \le h - 1$. We check the
form with $R(y) = y$ for $y \le Y_0 - 1$ and $R(y) = y + 2$ for $y \ge Y_0$. The columns of the
branch vertices increase along each row. Each staircase meets rows $Y_0 - 1$ and $Y_0 + 2$ only in
its ends and has one vertical edge of $H$ in each of the strips $Y_0 - 1$, $Y_0$, $Y_0 + 1$. In rows
$Y_0$ and $Y_0 + 1$ the staircase for $x \le c_0 - 1$ uses the columns $x, x + 1$ and
$x + 1, x + 2$, and the staircase for $x \ge c_0$ uses the columns $x + 3, x + 4$ and
$x + 2, x + 3$. Since staircases on the same side differ by $2$ in $x$, and since the columns used
on the left are at most $c_0 + 1$ and those on the right at least $c_0 + 2$, the staircases are
mutually disjoint. So $S(Y_0, c_0)$ has our form. We take $c_0$ as given, and for every strip $t$ we
take $y_t = t$ and $(a_t, a'_t) = (0, 4)$ if $t \le Y_0 - 1$, $y_t = Y_0 - 1$ and
$(a_t, a'_t) = (1, 3)$ if $t = Y_0$, $y_t = Y_0 - 1$ and $(a_t, a'_t) = (2, 2)$ if $t = Y_0 + 1$,
and $y_t = t - 2$ and $(a_t, a'_t) = (2, 2)$ if $t \ge Y_0 + 2$. These satisfy the conditions of our
form, by the definition of the vertical edges, and $R(0) = 0$ and $R(h') = h$. By (4), the moves in
strips $t \le Y_0 - 1$ are $(\Delta X, 0)$ with $0 \le \Delta X \le 4$ and $\Delta X$ even, those in
strip $Y_0$ are $(1, 1)$ and $(3, 1)$, and those in higher strips are $(2, 2)$.

By the form, the vertical edges of $H$ that $S(Y_0, c_0)$ keeps in strips $t \le Y_0 - 1$ are in
columns $c_t(x) = x \le c_0 - 1$ or $c_t(x) = x + 4 \ge c_0 + 4$, which shows that the elements of
kind (i) are deleted. Now let $c_0 \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$. The vertices and edges of $H$ in rows $Y_0$
and $Y_0 + 1$ and in strip $Y_0$ that $S(Y_0, c_0)$ keeps are those of the staircases, since the
other rows and vertical edges of $S(Y_0, c_0)$ lie in rows $y \le Y_0 - 1$ or $y \ge Y_0 + 2$ and in
strips other than $Y_0$. The staircases are at the points $(x, Y_0 - 1)$ with
$x \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$, so that $x \equiv c_0 \pmod 2$, and hence $x \le c_0 - 2$ or $x \ge c_0$.
The staircase for $x \le c_0 - 2$ meets these rows and this strip in the vertices $(x, Y_0)$,
$(x + 1, Y_0)$, $(x + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, $(x + 2, Y_0 + 1)$, the horizontal edges at $(x, Y_0)$ and
$(x + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, and the vertical edge at $(x + 1, Y_0)$. The staircase for $x \ge c_0$ meets
them in the vertices $(x + 4, Y_0)$, $(x + 3, Y_0)$, $(x + 3, Y_0 + 1)$, $(x + 2, Y_0 + 1)$, the
horizontal edges at $(x + 3, Y_0)$ and $(x + 2, Y_0 + 1)$, and the vertical edge at $(x + 3, Y_0)$.
Hence a vertex $(x', Y_0)$ or a vertical edge at $(x', Y_0)$ that is kept has $x' \le c_0 - 1$ or
$x' \ge c_0 + 3$, a horizontal edge at $(x', Y_0)$ that is kept has $x' \le c_0 - 2$ or
$x' \ge c_0 + 3$, a vertex $(x', Y_0 + 1)$ that is kept has $x' \le c_0$ or $x' \ge c_0 + 2$, and a
horizontal edge at $(x', Y_0 + 1)$ that is kept has $x' \le c_0 - 1$ or $x' \ge c_0 + 2$. This shows
that the other listed elements are deleted.

Now let $m$ be deleted and not of kinds (i) and (ii). Suppose first that $q \le Y_0 - \frac{1}{2}$.
Then $m$ is a vertex or a horizontal edge in a row $y \le Y_0 - 1$, since the vertical edges of $H$
in strips $t \le Y_0 - 1$ in other columns than $c_0, \dots, c_0 + 3$ are kept. The path of row $y$
of $S(Y_0, c_0)$ runs along row $y$ of $H$ from column $0$, or from column $1$ if $y = 0$ and
$c_0 \ge 2$, or from column $5$ if $y = 0$ and $c_0 = 1$, to column $2h' + 5 = 2h + 1$. So it is all
of row $y$ of $H$ unless $y = 0$ and $c_0 = 1$, and $m$ is of kind (iv). Suppose next that
$q \ge Y_0 + \frac{3}{2}$. The part of the drawing of $S(Y_0, c_0)$ above height $Y_0 + 1$ is the
part of the drawing of the elementary wall of height $h'$ above height $Y_0 - 1$, translated by
$(2, 2)$. Indeed, the rows $y \ge Y_0$ and the vertical edges at $(x, y)$ with $y \ge Y_0$ are
translated by $(2, 2)$, and so is the last step of each staircase. So $m$ translated by $-(2, 2)$ is
a vertex or edge of the infinite wall which is not an element of the elementary wall of height $h'$,
while $0 < q - 2 \le h'$. As in the proof of (7), it follows that $p - 2 < 0$, or $p - 2 > 2h' + 1$,
or $q - 2 = h'$, and so $m$ is of kind (iii). This proves (8).

We call $S(Y_0, c_0)$ *good* if $c_0 \ge 2Y_0$ when $Y_0$ is odd, and $c_0 + 2Y_0 \le 2h' + 2$ when
$Y_0$ is even.

(9) *If $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is good, then it satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) with $c = 2$.*

Each of the six moves $\mu$ of (8) satisfies $|\mu_1 - 2| + |\mu_2| \le 2$, and so any two moves
$\mu, \mu'$ satisfy $|\mu_1 - \mu'_1| + |\mu_2 - \mu'_2| \le 4$ and $|\mu_2 - \mu'_2| \le 2$. Hence
$\|\mu - \mu'\| \le 2$, and so $\|F - G\| \le \|P(F) - P(G)\| + 2$ for all bricks $F, G$, since
$F - G = (P(F) - P(G)) + (\mu - \mu')$ where $\mu, \mu'$ are the moves of $F, G$. This gives (a). By
(5) with $h - h' = 2$, all the drops are at most $2$, since $0 \le \Delta Y \le 2$ and
$0 \le \Delta X \le 4$. This gives (b).

For (c) let $m$ be a deleted element, of one of the kinds of (8). Let $(x, y)$ be such that $m$ is
the vertex $(x, y)$ or an edge at $(x, y)$. If $m$ is of kind (iii) or (iv), let $F = (X, Y)$ be the
position of the square $(x, y)$, which is at $m$. If $p < 2$ then $x \le 1$ and $X \le 2$, so the
left term of $F$ is at most $1$. If $p > 2h - 1$ then $x \ge 2h - 1$ and $X \ge 2h - 1$, so the
right term of $F$ is at most $1$. If $q = h$, then $Y = h$ and $F$ is not a brick of height $h$. If
$m$ is of kind (iv), the bottom term of $F$ is $1$. So in these cases $F$ is as required.

Let $m$ be of kind (ii). Then $m$ has a square in strip $Y_0$ at it: the square $(x, Y_0)$ if
$y = Y_0$, and the square $(x, Y_0)$ below $m$ if $m$ is a vertex or horizontal edge in row
$Y_0 + 1$. Let $F = (X, Y_0)$ be its position. If $F$ is not a brick of height $h$ we are done, so
we may assume that it is. Its move is $(1, 1)$ or $(3, 1)$. By (4), the move is $(1, 1)$ if
$X \le c_0$, it is $(3, 1)$ if $X \ge c_0 + 3$, and otherwise it is $(X - c_0, 1)$ or
$(X - c_0 + 1, 1)$. So if the move is $(1, 1)$ then $X \le c_0 + 1$, and if it is $(3, 1)$ then
$X \ge c_0 + 2$. By (5), the bottom and top terms drop by $1$. Suppose that $Y_0$ is odd, so that
$X$ is odd. If the move is $(1, 1)$, the left term drops by $\lceil 1/2 \rceil = 1$, and the right
term drops from $(2h + 1 - X)/2$ to $\lfloor (2h - 1 - X)/2 \rfloor = (2h - 1 - X)/2$, that is, by
$1$. If the move is $(3, 1)$, the right term drops by $\lceil 1/2 \rceil = 1$. The left term of
$P(F)$ is $\lfloor (X - 2)/2 \rfloor = (X - 3)/2$, and since $X$ is odd and
$X \ge c_0 + 2 \ge 2Y_0 + 2$ because $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is good, we have $X \ge 2Y_0 + 3$. So this left
term plus $1$ is at least $Y_0 + 1$, the bottom term of $F$. Now suppose that $Y_0$ is even, so that
$X$ is even. If the move is $(3, 1)$, the left term drops from $X/2$ to
$\lfloor (X - 2)/2 \rfloor = X/2 - 1$, and the right term by $1$. If the move is $(1, 1)$, the left
term drops by $1$. The right term of $P(F)$ is $\lfloor (2h' + 3 - X)/2 \rfloor$, and since
$X \le c_0 + 1 \le 2h' + 3 - 2Y_0$ because $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is good, it is at least $Y_0$. So this
right term plus $1$ is at least $Y_0 + 1$, the bottom term of $F$. In all cases each term of $P(F)$
plus $1$ is at least some term of $F$, as required.

Let $m$ be the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ of kind (i), so that $y \le Y_0 - 1$ and
$c_0 \le x \le c_0 + 3$. Suppose first that $Y_0$ is odd, and let $F = (X, y)$ be the position of
the square $(x, y)$, so that $X = x + 1$ and $c_0 + 1 \le X \le c_0 + 4$. In the definition of
$P(F)$ in (4), with $a_y = 0$ and $a'_y = 4$, the first case does not occur. So $z = c_0 - 1$ if
$X \le c_0 + 3$, and $z = c_0$ if $X = c_0 + 4$. In both cases $P(F) = (X', y)$ with
$X' \in \{c_0 - 1, c_0\}$, and the move is $(X - X', 0)$ with $X - X' \in \{2, 4\}$, since it is
positive and even. By (5), the bottom term does not drop, and the right term drops by at most
$\lceil (4 - 2)/2 \rceil = 1$. The left term of $P(F)$ is
$\lfloor (X' + 1)/2 \rfloor \ge \lfloor c_0/2 \rfloor \ge Y_0 \ge y + 1$, using $c_0 \ge 2Y_0$. So
it is at least the bottom term of $P(F)$, which equals that of $F$. The right term of $P(F)$ is
$\lfloor (2h' + 2 - X')/2 \rfloor \le \lfloor (2h' + 3 - 2Y_0)/2 \rfloor = h' + 1 - Y_0 \le h' - y$,
which is the top term of $P(F)$. So the top term of $P(F)$ plus $1$ is at least its right term plus
$1$, which is at least the right term of $F$. Now suppose that $Y_0$ is even, and let $F = (X, y)$
be the position of the square $(x - 1, y)$, so that $X = x - 1$ and $c_0 - 1 \le X \le c_0 + 2$. If
$X = c_0 - 1$, we are in the first case of (4) and $P(F) = F$. Otherwise we are in the third case,
$P(F) = (X', y)$ with $X' \in \{c_0 - 1, c_0\}$, and the move $(X - X', 0)$ satisfies
$X - X' \in \{0, 2\}$. So the bottom term does not drop, and the left term drops by at most $1$. The
left term of $P(F)$ is
$\lfloor (X' + 1)/2 \rfloor \le \lfloor (c_0 + 1)/2 \rfloor \le h' + 1 - Y_0 \le h' - y$, using
$c_0 \le 2h' + 2 - 2Y_0$, and this is the top term of $P(F)$. The right term of $P(F)$ is
$\lfloor (2h' + 2 - X')/2 \rfloor \ge \lfloor (2h' + 2 - c_0)/2 \rfloor \ge Y_0 \ge y + 1$, the
bottom term of $F$. In both cases each term of $P(F)$ plus $1$ is at least some term of $F$. This
proves (9).

(10) *If $k = 1$, there is a wall $H' \subseteq H$ of height $h - 2$ which satisfies (a), (b), (c)
of (6) with $c = 2$ and deletes $m_v$.*

If $T(1, 1)$ or $T(3, 1)$ deletes $m_v$, we take it as $H'$, by (7). So we may assume that both keep
$m_v$. Let $(x, y)$ be such that $m_v$ is the vertex $(x, y)$ or an edge at $(x, y)$. Since
$T(1, 1)$ and $T(3, 1)$ keep $m_v$, the elementary wall of height $h'$ has an element of the same
kind at $(x - 1, y - 1)$ and at $(x - 3, y - 1)$. Hence $3 \le x \le 2h' + 2$ and
$1 \le y \le h' + 1$. Moreover $x \le 2h' + 1$ if $m_v$ is a horizontal edge, and $y \le h'$ if
$m_v$ is a vertical edge. Since $h = h' + 2$, the rotation $\rho$ maps the vertex $(x, y)$ to the
vertex $(2h' + 5 - x, h' + 2 - y)$, the horizontal edge at $(x, y)$ to the horizontal edge at
$(2h' + 4 - x, h' + 2 - y)$, and the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ to the vertical edge at
$(2h' + 5 - x, h' + 1 - y)$.

We take as $H'$ the wall $S(Y_0, c_0)$ or the turned $S(Y_0, c_0)$, for integers $Y_0, c_0$ with
$c_0 \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$ such that $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is defined and good, that is, such that
$1 \le Y_0 \le h'$ and $1 \le c_0 \le 2h' + 1$, and $c_0 \ge 2Y_0$ if $Y_0$ is odd and
$c_0 + 2Y_0 \le 2h' + 2$ if $Y_0$ is even. Then $H'$ satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) with $c = 2$, by
(9) and the remark about turned walls. By (8), $S(Y_0, c_0)$ deletes $m_v$ if $m_v$ is one of the
elements listed there, and the turned $S(Y_0, c_0)$ deletes $m_v$ if $\rho(m_v)$ is one of them,
since it deletes $m_v$ if and only if $S(Y_0, c_0)$ deletes $\rho(m_v)$.

For each kind of $m_v$ and each parity of $x$ and $y$, we give below two or three candidates for
$H'$, in order. In every candidate $c_0 \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$. For each candidate we name the element
listed in (8) that $m_v$ is, or that $\rho(m_v)$ is for a turned candidate, and we give a condition
that holds if and only if $S(Y_0, c_0)$ is defined and good. We take as $H'$ the first candidate
whose condition holds, and we show that the condition of the last candidate holds if the earlier
ones fail. Each condition comes from putting the values of $Y_0$ and $c_0$ into the inequalities
above. In this, $1 \le c_0 \le 2h' + 1$ always follows from the bounds on $x$ and the parity of $x$,
and $1 \le Y_0 \le h'$ follows from the bounds on $y$ and the parity of $y$, except that
$Y_0 = h' + 2 - y$ with $y$ odd needs $y \ge 3$, $Y_0 = y$ with $y$ odd needs $y \le h'$, and
$Y_0 = y + 1$ needs $y \le h' - 1$. These are part of the conditions below, or follow from them and
the bounds.

Let $m_v$ be a vertex. If $x$ and $y$ are even, the candidates are $S(y - 1, x - 1)$, where $m_v$ is
the vertex $(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y - 1$; then $S(y, x - 2)$, where $m_v$
is the vertex $(c_0 + 2, Y_0)$, with the condition $x + 2y \le 2h' + 4$; and then the turned
$S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the vertex $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the condition
$x + 2y \ge 2h' + 6$. If the second condition fails, then $x + 2y \ge 2h' + 5$, and so the third
holds, since $x + 2y$ is even.

If $x$ and $y$ are odd, the candidates are the turned $S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where
$\rho(m_v)$ is the vertex $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the condition that $y \ge 3$ and $x \le 2y$; and
then $S(y, x)$, where $m_v$ is the vertex $(c_0, Y_0)$, with the condition that $y \le h'$ and
$x \ge 2y$. If the first condition fails, then $y = 1$ or $x > 2y$. If $y = 1$, then $y \le h'$ and
$x \ge 3 > 2y$. If $x > 2y$, then $2y < x \le 2h' + 2$, and so $y \le h'$. In both cases the second
condition holds.

If $x$ is odd and $y$ is even, the candidates are the turned $S(h' + 1 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where
$\rho(m_v)$ is the vertex $(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, with the condition $x \le 2y + 2$; then
$S(y, x - 1)$, where $m_v$ is the vertex $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the condition $x + 2y \le 2h' + 3$;
and then the turned $S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 3 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the vertex $(c_0 + 2, Y_0)$,
with the condition $x + 2y \ge 2h' + 5$. If the second condition fails, then $x + 2y \ge 2h' + 4$,
and so the third holds, since $x + 2y$ is odd.

If $x$ is even and $y$ is odd, the candidates are $S(y, x - 1)$, where $m_v$ is the vertex
$(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y + 1$, which gives $y \le h'$ since $x \le 2h' + 2$;
and then the turned $S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 5 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the vertex $(c_0, Y_0)$, with
the condition that $y \ge 3$ and $x \le 2y + 1$. If the first condition fails, then $x \le 2y$, and
so $y \ge 3$, since $x \ge 3$ and $y$ is odd. So the second condition holds.

Let $m_v$ be a horizontal edge. If $x$ and $y$ are even, the candidates are $S(y - 1, x - 1)$, where
$m_v$ is the horizontal edge at $(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y - 1$; and then
the turned $S(h' + 1 - y, 2h' + 3 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the horizontal edge at
$(c_0 + 1, Y_0 + 1)$, with the condition $x \le 2y + 1$, which holds if the first fails.

If $x$ and $y$ are odd, the candidates are $S(y, x)$, where $m_v$ is the horizontal edge at
$(c_0, Y_0)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y$, which gives $y \le h'$ since $x \le 2h' + 1$; and then
the turned $S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the horizontal edge at $(c_0, Y_0)$,
with the condition that $y \ge 3$ and $x \le 2y$. If the first condition fails, then $x < 2y$, and
so $y \ge 3$, since $x \ge 3$ and $y$ is odd. So the second condition holds.

If $x$ is odd and $y$ is even, the candidates are $S(y - 1, x)$, where $m_v$ is the horizontal edge
at $(c_0, Y_0 + 1)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y - 2$; and then the turned
$S(h' + 1 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the horizontal edge at $(c_0, Y_0 + 1)$, with the
condition $x \le 2y + 2$, which holds if the first fails.

If $x$ is even and $y$ is odd, the candidates are $S(y, x + 1)$, where $m_v$ is the horizontal edge
at $(c_0 - 1, Y_0)$, with the condition $x + 1 \ge 2y$, which gives $y \le h'$ since $x \le 2h'$ as
$x$ is even; and then the turned $S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 5 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the horizontal
edge at $(c_0 - 1, Y_0)$, with the condition that $y \ge 3$ and $x \le 2y + 1$. If the first
condition fails, then $x \le 2y - 2$, and so $y \ge 3$, since $x \ge 3$. So the second condition
holds.

Let $m_v$ be a vertical edge, so that $x + y$ is odd. If $y$ is even, the candidates are the turned
$S(h' + 1 - y, 2h' + 4 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the vertical edge at $(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the
condition $x \le 2y + 2$; and then $S(y + 1, x)$, where $m_v$ is the vertical edge at
$(c_0, Y_0 - 1)$, with the condition that $y \le h' - 1$ and $x \ge 2y + 2$. If the first condition
fails, then $x \ge 2y + 3$, and so $2y + 3 \le 2h' + 2$, which gives $y \le h' - 1$. So the second
condition holds.

If $y$ is odd, the candidates are $S(y, x - 1)$, where $m_v$ is the vertical edge at
$(c_0 + 1, Y_0)$, with the condition $x \ge 2y + 1$; and then the turned
$S(h' + 2 - y, 2h' + 5 - x)$, where $\rho(m_v)$ is the vertical edge at $(c_0, Y_0 - 1)$, with the
condition that $y \ge 3$ and $x \le 2y + 1$. If the first condition fails, then $x \le 2y$, and so
$y \ge 3$, since $x \ge 3$ and $y$ is odd. So the second condition holds.

In every case the chosen wall $H'$ deletes $m_v$ and satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) with $c = 2$.
This proves (10).

Now let $k \ge 2$. Let $(p, q)$ be the midpoint of $m_v$, and let $\delta$ be the least of the
numbers $\lfloor q \rfloor$, $h - \lceil q \rceil$, $\lfloor p/2 \rfloor$ and
$\lfloor (2h + 1 - p)/2 \rfloor$. These are the distances of $v$ from the bottom, top, left and
right sides of $H$, counted in bricks. Every square at $m_v$ has a position $G = (X, Y)$ with
$\sigma_h(G) \ge \delta$. For $\lceil q \rceil - 1 \le Y \le \lfloor q \rfloor$ gives
$Y + 1 \ge \lceil q \rceil \ge \lfloor q \rfloor$ and
$h - Y \ge h - \lfloor q \rfloor \ge h - \lceil q \rceil$, and $|X - p| \le 1$ gives $X + 1 \ge p$
and $2h + 2 - X \ge 2h + 1 - p$. Hence every region of $H$ at $m_v$ has depth at least $\delta$.

(11) *If $k \ge 2$ and $\delta \le k$, then there are $i, j$ as in (7) such that $T(i, j)$ deletes
$m_v$, and every region of $H$ at an element kept by $T(i, j)$ has distance $D_H$ at least $k - 2$
from every region of $H$ at $m_v$.*

We choose $i, j$ by a side of $H$ at distance $\delta$ from $v$, so that $T(i, j)$ leaves a wide
margin of $H$ on that side. In the *bottom* case, where $\delta = \lfloor q \rfloor$, let
$j = \min(2k - 1, k + 1 + \delta)$ and $i = 2k + (j \bmod 2)$. Otherwise, in the *top* case, where
$\delta = h - \lceil q \rceil$, let $j = \max(1, k - 1 - \delta)$ and $i = 2k + (j \bmod 2)$.
Otherwise, in the *left* case, where $\delta = \lfloor p/2 \rfloor$, let
$i = \min(4k - 1, 2k + 2 + 2\delta)$ and $j = k + ((i + k) \bmod 2)$. Otherwise we are in the
*right* case, where $\delta = \lfloor (2h + 1 - p)/2 \rfloor$, and we let
$i = \max(1, 2k - 2 - 2\delta)$ and $j = k + ((i + k) \bmod 2)$. These satisfy the conditions of
(7), using $k \ge 2$ and $\delta \le k$. In the first two cases $i + j$ is even,
$k + 1 \le j \le 2k - 1$ or $1 \le j \le k - 1$, and $i \in \{2k, 2k + 1\}$. In the last two cases
$i + j$ is even, $j \in \{k, k + 1\}$, and $2k + 2 \le i \le 4k - 1$ or $1 \le i \le 2k - 2$.

Let $m$ be an element kept by $T(i, j)$, let $s$ be a square at $m$ with position $F = (X, Y)$, and
let $s'$ be a square at $m_v$ with position $G = (X', Y')$. By the remark after (2), it is enough to
show that the regions $R, R'$ of $H$ containing $s, s'$ satisfy
$\text{depth}(R) + \text{depth}(R') \ge k - 2$ and $\|F - G\| \ge k - 2$. The midpoint of $m$ lies
in $[i, i + 2h' + 1] \times [j, j + h']$, and so $i - 1 \le X \le i + 2h' + 2$ and
$j - 1 \le Y \le j + h'$. Hence the terms of $F$ are at least $j$, $2k - j$, $\lfloor i/2 \rfloor$
and $\lfloor (2h - i - 2h')/2 \rfloor = \lfloor (4k - i)/2 \rfloor$, and so
$\text{depth}(R) \ge \min(j, 2k - j, \lfloor i/2 \rfloor, \lfloor (4k - i)/2 \rfloor)$. In each of
the four cases this minimum is at least $k - 1 - \delta$. For in the first two cases
$\lfloor i/2 \rfloor = k$ and $\lfloor (4k - i)/2 \rfloor \ge k - 1$, and the smaller of $j$ and
$2k - j$ is at least $\min(k - 1, 2k - (k + 1 + \delta))$ in the first case and at least
$\min(k - 1 - \delta, k + 1)$ in the second. In the last two cases $j \ge k$ and $2k - j \ge k - 1$,
and the smaller of $\lfloor i/2 \rfloor$ and $\lfloor (4k - i)/2 \rfloor$ is at least
$\min(k - 1, \lfloor (2k - 2 - 2\delta)/2 \rfloor)$. Since $\text{depth}(R') \ge \delta$, we get
$\text{depth}(R) + \text{depth}(R') \ge k - 1$.

Now we bound $\|F - G\|$. In the bottom case $Y' \le \lfloor q \rfloor = \delta$, so
$\|F - G\| \ge Y - Y' \ge j - 1 - \delta$, which is $k$ if $j = k + 1 + \delta$, and
$2k - 2 - \delta \ge k - 2$ if $j = 2k - 1$. In the top case
$Y' \ge \lceil q \rceil - 1 = h - \delta - 1$ and $Y \le j + h' = h - 2k + j$, so
$\|F - G\| \ge Y' - Y \ge 2k - 1 - \delta - j$, which is $k$ if $j = k - 1 - \delta$, and
$2k - 2 - \delta \ge k - 2$ if $j = 1$. In the left case $p < 2\delta + 2$, so $X' \le p + 1$ gives
$X' \le 2\delta + 2$, and $\|F - G\| \ge (X - X')/2 \ge (i - 3 - 2\delta)/2$. If
$i = 2k + 2 + 2\delta$ this is $k - \frac{1}{2}$, and so $\|F - G\| \ge k$ since it is an integer.
If $i = 4k - 1$ this is $2k - 2 - \delta \ge k - 2$. In the right case $p > 2h - 1 - 2\delta$, so
$X' \ge p - 1$ gives $X' \ge 2h - 1 - 2\delta$, while $X \le i + 2h' + 2 = i + 2h - 4k + 2$. So
$\|F - G\| \ge (X' - X)/2 \ge (4k - 3 - 2\delta - i)/2$, which is $k - \frac{1}{2}$ if
$i = 2k - 2 - 2\delta$ and $2k - 2 - \delta$ if $i = 1$. In all cases $\|F - G\| \ge k - 2$.

Finally $T(i, j)$ deletes $m_v$, by the bounds on the midpoints of kept elements. In the bottom case
$q < \delta + 1 \le j$, since $2k - 1 \ge k + 1 \ge \delta + 1$. In the top case
$q > h - \delta - 1 \ge h - 2k + j = j + h'$, since $j \le 2k - 1 - \delta$. In the left case
$p < 2\delta + 2 \le i$, since $4k - 1 \ge 2k + 2 \ge 2\delta + 2$. In the right case
$p > 2h - 1 - 2\delta \ge i + 2h' + 1$, since $i \le 4k - 2 - 2\delta$. This proves (11).

For $v$ far from the sides we use a wall with a large hole. Let $k \ge 2$ and $b = k - 1 \ge 1$, so
that $h' = h - 2b - 2$, and let $e$ be the even one of $b + 1$ and $b + 2$. Let $Y_0, c_0$ be
integers with $1 \le Y_0 \le h'$ and $1 \le c_0 \le 2h' + 1$, and let $Y_1 = Y_0 + b - 1$. For every
integer $t$ let $w_t = \max(0, b - |t - Y_1|)$. Thus $w_t = 0$ unless $Y_0 \le t \le Y_0 + 2b - 2$,
consecutive values of $w_t$ differ by $1$ for $Y_0 - 1 \le t \le Y_0 + 2b - 1$, and $w_{Y_1} = b$.
The wall $L(Y_0, c_0)$ is the subgraph of $H$ defined as follows. Let $R(y) = y$ if $y \le Y_0 - 1$
and $R(y) = y + 2b$ if $y \ge Y_0$. The branch vertex corresponding to $(x, y)$ is the vertex of $H$
in row $R(y)$ and column $x + e$ if $x \le c_0 - 1$, and column $x + e + 2b$ if $x \ge c_0$. The
horizontal edges are the paths of $H$ along rows between their ends. The vertical edge at $(x, y)$
is the vertical edge of $H$ at the vertex corresponding to $(x, y)$ if $y \ne Y_0 - 1$. If
$y = Y_0 - 1$, the vertical edge at $(x, y)$ is the staircase which has, in each strip $t$ with
$Y_0 - 1 \le t \le Y_0 + 2b - 1$, the vertical edge of $H$ in column $x + e - w_t$ if
$x \le c_0 - 1$, and in column $x + e + 2b + w_t$ if $x \ge c_0$, together with the horizontal edges
of $H$ joining the consecutive ones. So the band of rows $Y_0, \dots, Y_0 + 2b - 1$ of $H$ is not
used as rows of $L(Y_0, c_0)$, and the staircases on the left of $c_0$ move one step to the left in
each of the first $b$ of these rows, and back to the right in the others. The staircases on the
right of $c_0$ do the mirror image. Between them lies a hole shaped like a hexagon, which is the
reason for this construction.

(12) *The wall $L(Y_0, c_0)$ is a wall of height $h'$ of our form, with $c_0$ as given, $y_t = t$
for $t \le Y_0 - 1$, $y_t = Y_0 - 1$ for $Y_0 \le t \le Y_0 + 2b - 1$, $y_t = t - 2b$ for
$t \ge Y_0 + 2b$, and $a_t = e - w_t$, $a'_t = e + 2b + w_t$ for every $t$. Let $x_L$ be the largest
integer with $x_L \le c_0 - 1$ and $x_L \equiv Y_0 \pmod 2$, and let $B_0 = (x_L + 1 + e + b, Y_1)$.
Then all bricks $F$ of $H$ with $\|F - B_0\| \le b$ lie in the same region of $L(Y_0, c_0)$. If
moreover $2Y_0 \le h$, then $L(Y_0, c_0)$ satisfies (a), (b), (c) of (6) with $c = 2k$.*

The staircases are paths of $H$. For since $|t + 1 - Y_1| - |t - Y_1| = \pm 1$, consecutive vertical
edges of a staircase are in adjacent columns. Moreover
$w_t \equiv b + t - Y_1 \equiv t + Y_0 + 1 \pmod 2$, so the column $x + e - w_t$ plus $t$ is odd,
since $x \equiv Y_0$ and $e$ is even. The same holds on the right. Since
$w_{Y_0 - 1} = w_{Y_0 + 2b - 1} = 0$, each staircase starts and ends at the branch vertices it
should join, and it meets rows $Y_0 - 1$ and $Y_0 + 2b$ only in its ends. The columns used lie
between $e - b \ge 1$ and $2h' + 1 + e + 3b \le 2h + 1$, and the rows used are at most
$h' + 2b = h - 2$. In each row of the band, a staircase uses two adjacent columns, and the
staircases on the same side differ by $2$ in $x$. The left staircases use columns at most
$c_0 - 1 + e$, and the right ones columns at least $c_0 + e + 2b$. So the staircases are mutually
disjoint, and $L(Y_0, c_0)$ has our form with the values given. Here $w_t = 0$ outside the band, and
$t - y_t$ is $0$, $t - Y_0 + 1$ or $2b$, which has the parity of $a_t$ and $a'_t$ in each case.

By (4), the move $(\Delta X, \Delta Y)$ of a brick $F = (X, Y)$ satisfies
$e - w_Y \le \Delta X \le e + 2b + w_Y$, and $\Delta Y = 0$ if $Y \le Y_0 - 1$,
$\Delta Y = Y - Y_0 + 1$ if $Y_0 \le Y \le Y_0 + 2b - 1$, and $\Delta Y = 2b$ if $Y \ge Y_0 + 2b$.
In the band, $\Delta Y - b = Y - Y_1$ and $|Y - Y_1| = b - w_Y$. Outside the band $w_Y = 0$ and
$|\Delta Y - b| = b$. Hence in all cases

$$
|\Delta X - e - b| + |\Delta Y - b| \le (b + w_Y) + (b - w_Y) = 2b,
$$

and $0 \le \Delta Y \le 2b$, with $\Delta Y = 0$ only if $Y \le Y_0 - 1$. Moreover
$\Delta X \ge e - w_Y \ge b + 1 - w_Y \ge 1$, and $\Delta X \ge 2$ unless $w_Y = b$, that is, unless
$Y = Y_1$.

Let $F = (X, Y)$ be a brick with $\|F - B_0\| \le b$. Then $|Y - Y_1| \le b$, so $Y$ is in the band
or $Y = Y_0 - 1$, and $y_Y = Y_0 - 1$. Moreover
$|X - (x_L + 1 + e + b)| \le 2b - |Y - Y_1| = b + w_Y$. Hence
$X - a_Y = X - e + w_Y \ge x_L + 1 \ge c_0 - 1$ and
$X - a'_Y = X - e - 2b - w_Y \le x_L + 1 \le c_0$. In the definition of $P(F)$ in (4), this gives
$z = c_0 - 1$ in the first and third cases, and $z = c_0 = x_L + 1$ in the second case. In each case
the position of $(z, Y_0 - 1)$ is $(x_L + 1, Y_0 - 1)$, since $x_L + 1 \in \{c_0 - 1, c_0\}$ and

## 14. Proof of (7.7), page 93: the algorithm

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "The second follows since the proofs of (7.5) and of (7.6) may
easily be converted to algorithms." It does not say how the steps of these proofs that are not
explicit are carried out, or why the time is $\le C\varepsilon(G)$.

**Proof.** We give the details for the steps that need a method. We assume that a wall is given with
its identification with a subdivision of an elementary wall, so that the position of each of its
vertices in the drawing of Fig. 1 is known: a vertex of the elementary wall, or a point of the line
that represents one of its edges. A wall that is a subgraph of it gets its positions from these. The
numbers $k = \binom{p}{2}$ and $l$ depend only on $p$, and so do the numbers of stars, legs and ends
handled below.

For (7.6), each application of (7.3) with $r = 0$ chooses $H_i$ among a bounded number of explicit
subwalls of $H_{i-1}$, according to the position of $v_i$ in $H_{i-1}$ (see the proof of (7.3)), and
this takes time $O(\varepsilon)$. Then we extend every leg of every star as in (1). Let $P_j$ be a
leg with ends $v, u_j$, and let $q$ be the first vertex of the bottom row of $H_i$. In the
elementary wall of $H_{i-1}$ there is a path from the position of $u_j$ to that of $q$ that runs
along the row of $u_j$ to a vertical edge, along this edge to the next row, and so on until it
reaches the row of $q$, and then along this row to $q$ (if $u_j$ is inside an edge of the elementary
wall, the path first runs along this edge). We follow the corresponding path of $H_{i-1}$ from $u_j$
and stop at its first vertex in $V(H_i)$. This gives a minimal path $Q_j$ of $H_{i-1}$ between $u_j$
and $V(H_i)$, in time $O(\varepsilon)$. If $v \in V(Q_j)$, the new leg is the subpath of $Q_j$
between $v$ and $w_j$, as in (1), and otherwise it is $P_j \cup Q_j$. In this way a new leg may
consist of $v$ alone, when $v = w_j$ (this is why the proof of (7.6) uses (1) only once). We allow
such legs before the last step. The argument of (1) applies to them unchanged and gives the bound
$m + 4(k - i)$ on the distances between the ends of the legs, since it uses only these ends. After
step $k$ no center is in $V(H_k)$, and so every leg has at least two vertices, and we have $k$
external $(l, m)$-stars over $H_k$.

For (7.5) we follow the induction on $k$. Given $S_1, \dots, S_k$, we obtain $S'_i$ by deleting from
$S_i$ the leg that contains $v_k$, or any one leg if there is none. There is at most one such leg,
since $v_k$ is not the center of $S_i$ and the legs of $S_i$ meet only in its center. We apply the
algorithm to $S'_1, \dots, S'_{k-1}$ and obtain $Q_1, \dots, Q_{k-1}$. Next we label each vertex of
$Q_j$ with $j$ ($1 \le j \le k - 1$), number the vertices of each $Q_j$ in order, and traverse each
leg $P_i$ of $S_k$ from its end in $V(H)$, recording for each $j$ the first vertex of $P_i$ with
label $j$. This takes time $O(\varepsilon)$, since the legs of $S_k$ meet only in $v_k$, and it
decides whether some $j$ has $\ge 2k$ values of $i$ with $P_i \cap Q_j$ non-null. If so, it gives
the vertices $u_{i_1}, \dots, u_{i_{2k}}$ in order on $Q_j$, and the paths $R_1, \dots, R_k$ are
formed in time $O(\varepsilon)$. Otherwise we need the distances $d(u_i, x)$ for the $2(k - 1)$ ends
$x$ of $Q_1, \dots, Q_{k-1}$, and we compute them from the positions of $u_i$ and $x$. By the
formula for $d$ in the proof of (7.3) (given above, with the proof of (7.3)), for distinct vertices
$s, t$ of $H$, $d(s, t)$ is $2$ plus the minimum of $D_H(R, R')$ over the regions $R$ at $s$ and
$R'$ at $t$. Moreover $D_H$ of a brick and the infinite region is the depth of the brick, and $D_H$
of two bricks is the smaller of their lattice distance and the sum of their depths. Let $H$ have
height $h$. We may take the drawing of the elementary wall to have as its vertices the points
$(x, y)$ with integers $0 \le x \le 2h + 1$ and $0 \le y \le h$, other than $(0, 0)$ and
$(2h + 1, h)$, with the rows horizontal, and with $(x, y)$ joined to $(x, y + 1)$ when $x + y$ is
odd. We name each brick $(X, y)$, where the brick lies between the rows $y$ and $y + 1$
($0 \le y < h$) and $X$ is the abscissa of its middle. Then the lattice distance of the bricks
$(X, y)$ and $(X', y')$ is
$$\max(|y - y'|, \frac{1}{2}(|X - X'| + |y - y'|)),$$
and the depth of the brick $(X, y)$ is
$$\min(y + 1, h - y, \lfloor (X + 1)/2 \rfloor, \lfloor (2h + 2 - X)/2 \rfloor).$$
The regions at a vertex are read off from its position, and so each distance $d(u_i, x)$ is computed
in constant time. Thus each of the $\binom{p}{2}$ steps of the induction takes time
$O(\varepsilon)$. Together with the algorithm of (7.4), this gives a model of $K_p$ in time
$\le C\varepsilon(G)$.

$q_1, q_2$ and $V(Q') \subseteq V(Q)$. Then $P' \cup Q'$ is a cross in $(C, \Omega)$.

Conversely, let $P' \cup Q'$ be a cross in $(C, \Omega)$, where $P', Q'$ have ends $p_1, p_2$ and
$q_1, q_2$. For each edge $e$ of $C$, with ends $x, y$ say, choose a path $J_e$ of $G$ between $x$
and $y$ with no internal vertex in $Z$, which exists since $C$ is a core. Let $U_1$ be the union of
$V(P')$ and of the sets $V(J_e)$ ($e \in E(P')$), and define $U_2$ similarly from $Q'$. We claim
that $U_1 \cap U_2 = \emptyset$. First, $V(P') \cap V(Q') = \emptyset$. Next, if $e \in E(Q')$ then
$V(J_e) \cap Z$ is the set of ends of $e$, which lie in $V(Q')$, and so $J_e$ is disjoint from
$V(P')$. Similarly $J_e$ is disjoint from $V(Q')$ for $e \in E(P')$. Finally, let $e_1 \in E(P')$
with ends $x_1, y_1$ and $e_2 \in E(Q')$ with ends $x_2, y_2$, and suppose that
$v \in V(J_{e_1}) \cap V(J_{e_2})$. Then $v \notin Z$, since $J_{e_1}$ and $J_{e_2}$ meet $Z$ only
in their ends, and $\{x_1, y_1\} \cap \{x_2, y_2\} = \emptyset$. But then subpaths of $J_{e_1}$ and
$J_{e_2}$ are paths of $G$ from $v$ to the four distinct vertices $x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2$ of $Z$, each
with no other vertex in $Z$, contrary to the fact that $Z$ is trisecting. This proves that
$U_1 \cap U_2 = \emptyset$. Now replacing each edge $e$ of $P'$ by $J_e$ gives a walk of $G$ between
$p_1$ and $p_2$ with all its vertices in $U_1$, and so $G$ has a path $P$ between $p_1$ and $p_2$
with $V(P) \subseteq U_1$. Similarly $G$ has a path $Q$ between $q_1$ and $q_2$ with
$V(Q) \subseteq U_2$. Then $P \cup Q$ is a cross in $(G, \Omega)$.

`GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_8_4_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM13/Section8a.lean`. The paths $P', Q'$ come
from `GraphMinor.GM13.exists_core_path_of_society_path`, and the disjointness of $J_{e_1}$ and
$J_{e_2}$ is `GraphMinor.GM13.disjoint_core_jumps`, in the same file.

## 16. Proof of (9.2), page 99: a graph in place of $F$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says "We can find $F$ in time $O(\varepsilon)$, for fixed
$l, m, p, k, r$", and on page 101, "Since the size of $F$ is bounded (exept for divalent vertices)
by a function of $l, m$, and $p$, we can find an $(l, m)$-star or a model of $K_p$ in $H \cup F$ in
time $\le C\varepsilon(G)$". It does not say how $F$ is found, nor how $H \cup F$ is searched.

**Proof.** In fact the algorithm needs only a subgraph $F'$ of $G$ with the same property, of
bounded size except for divalent vertices, and we find such a subgraph as follows. Let
$1 \le i \le k$. Since $H_i$ is non-dividing, there is an $H$-path of $G$ from inside $H_i$ to
outside $H_i$, and we find one, $Q_i$ say, in time $O(\varepsilon)$ by breadth-first search from the
set of vertices inside $H_i$, using only edges not in $E(H)$ and continuing only from vertices not
in $V(H)$, until a vertex of $H$ outside $H_i$ is reached. We build a subgraph $F'$ of $G$ in $k$
steps, starting from the null graph. At step $i$, if no internal vertex of $Q_i$ belongs to $F'$, we
add $Q_i$ to $F'$. Otherwise let $z$ be the first internal vertex of $Q_i$ (from its end inside
$H_i$) that belongs to $F'$, and add the subpath of $Q_i$ between its end inside $H_i$ and $z$. Then
$z \notin V(H)$, since $z$ is an internal vertex of the $H$-path $Q_i$. Let $j < i$ be the first
step at which a path containing $z$ was added, and let $A$ be the subpath of this path between its
end inside $H_j$ and $z$. The subpath of $Q_i$ between its end inside $H_i$ and $z$ meets $A$ only
in $z$, because no vertex of $Q_i$ before $z$ is in $F'$ except possibly the first, which is inside
$H_i$, while the only vertex of $A$ in $V(H)$ is its end inside $H_j$. So the union of these two
subpaths is an $H$-path of $F'$ from inside $H_i$ to inside $H_j$, and so to outside $H_i$, since
$H_i$ and $H_j$ are disjoint. Thus at the end $E(F' \cap H) = \emptyset$, and for $1 \le i \le k$
the graph $F'$ contains an $H$-path from inside $H_i$ to outside $H_i$.

The algorithm uses $F'$ in place of $F$, as follows. By the choice of $z$, each of the $k$ paths
added to $F'$ meets the earlier ones only in its ends. Hence the vertices of $H \cup F'$ that are
neither branch vertices of $H$ (the vertices that correspond to vertices of the elementary wall) nor
ends of these $k$ paths have valency $2$ in $H \cup F'$, and so the size of $H \cup F'$ is bounded,
except for divalent vertices, by a function of $k$ and the height of $H$. Moreover $H_1, \dots, H_k$
are non-dividing subwalls of $H$ in the graph $H \cup F'$, and so by the first assertion of (9.2),
applied to $H \cup F'$ in place of $G$, there is an $(l, m)$-star over $H$ in $H \cup F'$, or
$H \cup F'$ has a $K_p$-minor. Thus at the end of the proof (page 101), where the algorithm searches
$H \cup F$, it searches $H \cup F'$ instead. To do so, we call the branch vertices of $H$ and the
ends of the $k$ paths *nodes*, and we replace the internal vertices of each path of $H \cup F'$
between two nodes, with no node as an internal vertex, by a single new vertex. This takes time
$O(\varepsilon)$. The new graph has bounded size, and in it the images of $H, H_1, \dots, H_k$ are a
wall and non-dividing subwalls of it, with the same distances as before. We find an $(l, m)$-star
over the new wall or a model of $K_p$ in the new graph by examining all its subgraphs, in time
bounded in terms of $l, m, p, k$ and the height of $H$. Replacing each new vertex by the path that
it replaced then gives an $(l, m)$-star over $H$ or a model of $K_p$ in $G$, in time
$O(\varepsilon)$.

## 17. Algorithm (10.1), page 107: the width after adding vertices

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** The description finds "a branch-decomposition of $\tilde{A}$ of width
$\le \omega + q$ (this exists, since $\tilde{A}$ is obtained from $A$ by adding $q$ extra
vertices)", where $A$ has branch-width at most $\omega$. By the convention of page 70, a graph with
at most one edge has branch-width $0$. Adding vertices to such a graph can raise its branch-width to
$2$, which is more than $\omega + q$ when $\omega + q < 2$. For example, take $\omega = 0$, $q = 1$
and $A = K_2$, and join the new vertex to both ends of the edge. Then $\tilde{A} = K_3$ has
branch-width $2 > \omega + q$.

**Correction.** Use the width $\max(\omega, 2) + q$, both for the branch-decomposition of
$\tilde{A}$ and in the running time of the third step. This width is still a constant, which is all
that (4.1) needs, so the algorithm and its running time are unchanged.

`GraphMinor/GM13/Section10a.lean`, and `GraphMinor.GM13.theorem_10_1` in
`GraphMinor/GM13/Results.lean`.
