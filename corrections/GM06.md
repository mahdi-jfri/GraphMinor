# Graph Minors VI: corrections

N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. VI. Disjoint paths across a disc*, J. Combin. Theory
Ser. B **41** (1986), 115–138.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM06/GM06.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM06/GM06.pdf) in the
repository [GraphMinorPapers](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers).

The corrections are in the order of the pages. The corrections to Reductions 1 and 4 have the same
cause. Section 5 allows $k = 1$, and for one path $P_1$ the complement of $P_1$ in the annulus is
connected. So the strip $\Sigma_0 = \Sigma_1$ and the region $R_1$ no longer say on which side of
$P_1$ an edge or a path lies, and Reductions 1 and 4 can change the problem.

## 1. Proof of (3.1), page 120: the dual of the dual

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that (3.1) follows from standard arguments of duality theory and
leaves its proof to the reader. It also draws $G^*$ "in the usual way" without listing the
conditions that the proof uses.

**Proof.** We give the argument. We read "in the usual way" as follows. Each region of $G$ contains
exactly one vertex of $G^*$, and each vertex of $G^*$ lies in a region of $G$. For each edge $e$ of
$G$, the corresponding edge $e^*$ of $G^*$ meets the drawing of $G$ only in points of $e$, the edge
$e$ meets the drawing of $G^*$ only in points of $e^*$, and $e, e^*$ have exactly one point in
common. The ends of $e^*$ are the vertices of $G^*$ in the regions of $G$ incident with $e$, and
each region of $G^*$ incident with $e^*$ contains a point of $e$. Finally, distinct vertices of $G$
lie in distinct regions of $G^*$. Then (3.1) says that the drawing of $G$ satisfies these conditions
with the roles of $G$ and $G^*$ exchanged, and also condition (ii) above with the roles exchanged.

No vertex of $G$ lies in the drawing of $G^*$, since the vertices of $G^*$ lie in regions of $G$,
and each edge $e^*$ meets the drawing of $G$ only in points of $e$. So each vertex of $G$ lies in a
region of $G^*$. Next let $e$ be an edge of $G$ with ends $u, v$. Removing from $e$ its point in
$e^*$ leaves two connected pieces, one with $u$ and one with $v$ in its closure. They are disjoint
from the drawing of $G^*$, and so they lie in the regions of $G^*$ containing $u$ and $v$
respectively. Hence these two regions are incident with $e^*$, since they contain points of $e$
arbitrarily close to the point in $e^*$. They are the only regions of $G^*$ incident with $e^*$,
since every such region contains a point of $e$.

Now let $r$ be a region of $G^*$. Then $r$ contains at most one vertex of $G$, since distinct
vertices of $G$ lie in distinct regions of $G^*$. If $r$ is incident with some edge $e^*$, then $r$
contains an end of $e$, by the previous paragraph. If $r$ is incident with no edge of $G^*$, then
every point of the frontier of $r$ is a vertex of $G^*$. Since $\Sigma$ minus finitely many points
is connected, it follows that $r$ includes $\Sigma$ minus the vertices of $G^*$, and so $r$ contains
every vertex of $G$. There is such a vertex, since $G$ is connected. Thus each region of $G^*$
contains exactly one vertex of $G$. Next, if $V(G) \cap bd(\Sigma)$ is non-empty, then by (ii) above
its points alternate around $bd(\Sigma)$ with those of $V(G^*) \cap bd(\Sigma)$, and so each segment
of $bd(\Sigma)$ with respect to $V(G^*) \cap bd(\Sigma)$ contains exactly one vertex of $G$. If
$V(G) \cap bd(\Sigma)$ is empty, then so is $V(G^*) \cap bd(\Sigma)$, since there are no segments.
Next, distinct vertices of $G^*$ lie in distinct regions of $G$, since each region of $G$ contains
only one vertex of $G^*$. Finally, let $e$ be an edge of $G$. The conditions on the points of $e$
and $e^*$ are symmetric in $e$ and $e^*$. The ends of $e$ are the vertices of $G$ in the regions of
$G^*$ incident with $e^*$, by the second paragraph. Each region of $G$ incident with $e$ contains an
end of $e^*$, and so contains a point of $e^*$, since it is open and the end is in the closure of
$e^*$. This proves (3.1).

## 2. Proof of (3.2), page 120: feasibility in the dual

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper leaves the proof of (3.2) to the reader, with a hint for one
direction.

**Proof.** We give the argument, which follows the hint. Let $U = V(G^*) \cap bd(\Sigma)$. Suppose
first that $\Delta$ is feasible in $G$, and let $H$ be a realization of $\Delta$ in $G$. Let $K^*$
be the subgraph of $G^*$ with vertex set $V(G^*)$ whose edges are the edges $e^*$ for the edges $e$
of $G$ not in $H$. We claim that for $u_1, u_2 \in U$, there is a path of $K^*$ between $u_1$ and
$u_2$ if and only if $u_1$ and $u_2$ belong to the same member of $\Delta^*$.

Suppose that there is such a path $P$, and that $\{v_1, v_2\}$ crosses $\{u_1, u_2\}$ for some
$\delta \in \Delta$ and $v_1, v_2 \in \delta$. Let $Q$ be the path of $H(\delta)$ between $v_1$ and
$v_2$. The drawings of $P$ and $Q$ are compact connected subsets of $\Sigma$, and their ends on
$bd(\Sigma)$ cross, and so they meet. Let $z$ be a common point. Then $z$ is in the drawing of $G$,
and so it is not a vertex of $G^*$, since the vertices of $G^*$ lie in regions of $G$. So $z$ lies
in an edge $f^*$ of $K^*$. Since $f^*$ meets the drawing of $G$ only in points of $f$, the point $z$
lies in $f$. But $f$ meets no vertex of $G$ and no other edge of $G$, and $f$ is not an edge of $H$,
and so $z$ is not in $Q$. This is a contradiction, and so $u_1, u_2$ belong to the same member of
$\Delta^*$.

Conversely, suppose that they do. Let $\Gamma$ be the drawing of the subgraph of $G$ with vertex set
$V(G)$ and edge set $E(H)$. We show first that no component of $\Gamma$ has two vertices $v_1, v_2$
in $bd(\Sigma)$ such that $\{v_1, v_2\}$ crosses $\{u_1, u_2\}$. Such vertices are in
$\bigcup \Delta$, which is a subset of $V(H)$, and they are joined by a path of $H$. So they lie in
the same component $H(\delta)$ of $H$, and hence both belong to $\delta$, since every other member
of $\Delta$ is included in another component of $H$. Since $u_1, u_2$ are in the same member of
$\Delta^*$, it follows that $\{v_1, v_2\}$ does not cross $\{u_1, u_2\}$. Next we show that $u_1$
and $u_2$ lie in the same region of $\Gamma$. Let $B_1, B_2$ be the two arcs of $bd(\Sigma)$ with
ends $u_1, u_2$. Let $K_1$ be the union of the drawings of the components of $\Gamma$ whose vertices
in $bd(\Sigma)$ all lie in $B_2$, and let $K_2$ be the union of the drawings of the other
components. Then $K_1, K_2$ are disjoint closed sets with union the drawing of $\Gamma$. The arc
$B_1$ is disjoint from $K_1$, since $u_1, u_2$ are not in the drawing of $\Gamma$. The arc $B_2$ is
disjoint from $K_2$, since a component in $K_2$ has a vertex in $bd(\Sigma) - B_2$, and a vertex in
$B_2$ would make a pair that crosses $\{u_1, u_2\}$. By Janiszewski's theorem, applied in the disc
to $K_1$ and $K_2$, the points $u_1, u_2$ lie in the same component of $\Sigma$ minus
$K_1 \cup K_2$, that is, in the same region $r$ of $\Gamma$. Now let $Z$ be the set of regions of
$G$ whose vertex of $G^*$ is joined to $u_1$ by a path of $K^*$, and let $r_1, r_2$ be the regions
of $G$ containing $u_1, u_2$. Both are included in $r$, since they are connected and disjoint from
the drawing of $\Gamma$, and $r_1 \in Z$. Each point of $r$ lies in a region of $G$ included in $r$,
or in an edge of $G$ not in $H$, and such an edge lies in $r$ together with every region of $G$
incident with it. Since $r$ is connected, it follows that if $r_2 \notin Z$, then some edge $e$ of
$G$ not in $H$ is incident with a region in $Z$ and a region not in $Z$. But then $e^*$ is an edge
of $K^*$ joining the vertices of $G^*$ in these two regions, which is impossible. Hence $r_2 \in Z$,
and since $u_2$ is the vertex of $G^*$ in $r_2$, this proves the claim.

By the claim, each member of $\Delta^*$ is included in the vertex set of a component of $K^*$, and
distinct members of $\Delta^*$ are included in distinct components. Choosing a spanning tree in each
of these components gives a realization of $\Delta^*$ in $G^*$. So $\Delta^*$ is feasible in $G^*$.

Now suppose that $\Delta^*$ is feasible in $G^*$. Since $G$ is connected, (3.1) applies, and so the
drawing of $G$ is a drawing of the dual of $G^*$, and $V(G) \cap bd(\Sigma)$ contains exactly one
point of each segment of $bd(\Sigma)$ with respect to $U$. Hence what we just proved applies to
$G^*$ and $\Delta^*$ in place of $G$ and $\Delta$, and shows that $\Delta^{**}$ is feasible in $G$.
Since $\Delta$ is cross-free, $\Delta^{**} = \Delta$ by (2.2), and so $\Delta$ is feasible in $G$.

## 3. Proof of (3.6), page 122: necessity of (i) and (ii)

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that the necessity of (i) and (ii) is easy and gives no proof.

**Proof.** To see this, let $H$ be a realization of $\Delta$ in $G$. We use the fact that two
compact connected subsets of the disc $\Sigma$ must meet if they contain pairs of points of
$bd(\Sigma)$ that cross. If $a, c \in \delta$ and $b, d \in \delta'$ for distinct
$\delta, \delta' \in \Delta$, and $\{a, c\}$ crosses $\{b, d\}$, then by this fact the drawings of a
path of $H(\delta)$ between $a$ and $c$ and a path of $H(\delta')$ between $b$ and $d$ meet. That is
impossible, since $H(\delta)$ and $H(\delta')$ are disjoint subgraphs of $G$. So (i) holds. For
(ii), let $A_1, \dots, A_k$ be an $(x, y)$-chain. For each term $A_i$ that is a region, choose a
curve in $\Sigma$ whose interior lies in $A_i$, from $x$ if $i = 1$ and from $A_{i-1}$ if $i > 1$,
to $y$ if $i = k$ and to $A_{i+1}$ if $i < k$. This is possible since the two ends lie in the
closure of $A_i$, and any two points in the closure of a region are joined by such a curve. Let $F$
be the union of these curves and of the terms that are vertices. Then $F$ is compact and connected,
it contains $x$ and $y$, and it meets the drawing of $G$ only in the terms that are vertices. Now
let $\delta \in \Delta$ be one of the members counted in $\Delta(x, y)$. If $x \in \delta$, then $x$
is a vertex of $G$ and so lies in no region, and hence $A_1 = x$ by condition (ii) in the definition
of a chain. Similarly, if $y \in \delta$ then $A_k = y$. If there exist $u, v \in \delta$ such that
$\{u, v\}$ crosses $\{x, y\}$, then the drawing of a path of $H(\delta)$ between $u$ and $v$ meets
$F$, by the fact above, and the common point is a term of the chain. In each case some term of the
chain that is a vertex belongs to $H(\delta)$. Since the subgraphs $H(\delta)$ $(\delta \in \Delta)$
are pairwise disjoint, distinct members of $\Delta$ give distinct terms. Hence $\Delta(x, y)$ is at
most the length of the chain, and so (ii) holds.

## 4. Proof of (4.2), pages 126–127: $a$ and $b$ must be consecutive

**Kind.** Proof gap.

**What goes wrong.** On page 126 the proof takes any two distinct $a, b \in S_i \cap V(H')$ and
argues: "Since (4.1)(i) does not apply, $P(a, b, S_i(a, b))$ exists; since reduction 2(ii) does not
apply, every vertex of it is in $bd\Sigma$ ...". But (4.1) and Reduction 2 speak only of segments,
that is, components of $bd(\Sigma) - (V(H) \cap bd(\Sigma))$. If $a, b$ are not consecutive among
the vertices of $H'$ in $S_i$, then $S_i(a, b)$ contains a vertex of $H'$, so it is not a segment,
and the facts that (4.1) and Reduction 2 do not apply say nothing about $P(a, b, S_i(a, b))$. The
same holds for the use of (4.1)(iii) in the next sentences.

**Correction.** Take $a, b$ consecutive in $S_i$, that is, with no vertex of $H'$ in $S_i(a, b)$.
Since $V(H') \cap C_i = V(H) \cap S_i$, the arc $S_i(a, b)$ then contains no vertex of $H$, so it is
a segment with ends $a, b$, and the paragraph applies word for word. Its two uses need only
consecutive pairs.

- On page 126, the conclusion "if $a \in X_j$ then $b \in X_j$" passes to all of $V(H') \cap S_i$:
  list these vertices in their order along $S_i$. Each is consecutive to the next, so all of them
  are in the same set $X_j$.
- On page 127, "For all distinct $a, b \in X_1$, $P(a, b, S_1(a, b))$ is a subgraph of $K$" becomes
  "for all consecutive $a, b \in X_1$". This is still enough for $K$ to be connected, since the
  vertices of $X_1$, in their order along $S_1$, are each consecutive to the next.

The statement of (4.2) holds as printed.

`GraphMinor.GM06.relation_of_consecutive_finite` in `GraphMinor/GM06/Section4Consecutive.lean` (the
passage from consecutive pairs to all pairs), and `GraphMinor.GM06.theorem_4_2` in

## 5. Reduction 1, page 128: the side of $P_1$ when $k = 1$

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** Reduction 1 deletes an edge that is incident with some $s_i$ and contained in
$\Sigma_{i-1}$. With this reduction, (5.5) and (5.6) are false for $k = 1$. In the polar coordinates
of the paper, let $\Sigma$ be the annulus $1 \le r \le 2$, and let $G$ consist of the vertices
$s_1 = (1, 0)$ and $t = (2, 0)$ and the two edges $P(u) = (1 + u, 0)$ and $B(u) = (1 + u, 2\pi u)$,
$0 \le u \le 1$, which meet only at their ends. Let $\mathcal{L} = \{P\}$, so
$\theta(\mathcal{L}) = 0$. The strip $\Sigma_0 = \Sigma_1$ is the whole complement of $P$, so
Reduction 1 may delete $B$.

- Before the deletion, $\{B\}$ is a linkage with winding number $1$, so $1$ is feasible in $G$. By
  (5.2), every helix $f$ in $G$ has $\phi_G(f) \ge 1$.
- After the deletion, the radial helix $f'(u) = (1 + u, \pi)$ misses the drawing, so
  $\phi_{G'}(f') = 0$. No helix $f$ in $G$ has $\phi_G(f) \le 0$, contrary to (5.5).
- In $G'$ the only path from $C_1$ to $C_2$ is $P$, so $1$ is not feasible in $G'$, while it is
  feasible in $G$. This is contrary to (5.6).

**Correction.** For $k \ge 2$, Reduction 1 is unchanged. For $k = 1$, the edge must moreover leave
$s_1$ on the side of the strip that precedes $s_1$: in the universal cover of the annulus, some
connected lift of the edge lies in the lift of the strip that precedes the chosen lift of $s_1$, and
is incident with that lift of $s_1$. The edge $B$ above has no such lift. With this reading and the
correction to Reduction 4 below, (5.5) and (5.6) hold for all $k \ge 1$.

## 6. Reduction 4, page 129: the side of $P_1$ when $k = 1$

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** Reduction 4 replaces the part of $P_i$ before a vertex $v$ by a path $Q_i$ all
of whose vertices and edges are incident with $R_i$. The proof of (5.6) on page 133 uses that this
does not change the winding number, $\theta(\mathcal{L}') = \theta(\mathcal{L})$. For $k = 1$ this
fails. With $\Sigma$ as in the previous correction, let $G$ consist of $s_1 = (1, 0)$, $t = (2, 0)$
and three edges from $s_1$ to $t$, for $0 \le x \le 1$:
$$P(x) = (1 + x, 0), \qquad A(x) = (1 + x, -x(1-x)), \qquad B(x) = (1 + x, 2\pi x),$$
with angles read modulo $2\pi$. For $0 < x < 1$ their angles have the representatives
$0 < 2\pi x < 2\pi - x(1-x) < 2\pi$, so the three edges meet only at their ends. Let
$\mathcal{L} = \{P\}$. The region $R_1$, which contains $(1, \pi)$, is the set of points
$(1 + x, a)$ with $0 \le x < 1$ and $2\pi x < a < 2\pi - x(1-x)$. Its closure contains all of $A$
and $B$ but no inner point of $P$. So Reduction 4 applies with $v = t$ and $Q_1 = B$, and it changes
the winding number from $0$ to $1$. The only paths from $s_1$ to $t$ are the three edges, with
winding numbers $0$, $0$ and $1$, so the feasible values are exactly $0$ and $1$. Thus
$\theta(\mathcal{L}) + 1 = 1$ is feasible in $G$, but $\theta(\mathcal{L}') + 1 = 2$ is not feasible
in $G' = G$, contrary to (5.6).

**Correction.** For $k \ge 2$, Reduction 4 is unchanged. For $k = 1$, "incident with $R_1$" is read
in the universal cover of the annulus. Take the lift of $R_1$ that contains the lift of the gap
following the chosen lift of $s_1$. The vertex $v$ and the part of $P_1$ before it are tested on the
chosen lift of $P_1$, and the lift of $Q_1$ that starts at the chosen lift of $s_1$ must reach the
same lift of $v$ through the closure of that lift of $R_1$. In the example, the lift of $B$ from
angle $2\pi$ ends at angle $4\pi$, while the lift of $t$ on $P$ is at angle $2\pi$, so the step is
not allowed. With this reading, the new and old paths reach the same lift of $v$, so the winding
number does not change, and (5.6) holds for all $k \ge 1$.

## 7. Proof of (5.4), page 130: the four reductions

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that (5.4) is trivial for Reduction 4 and almost equally trivial
for the other reductions. It does not say why $\theta(\mathcal{L}') = \theta(\mathcal{L})$, or how a
linkage of the contracted graph of Reduction 3 gives one of $G$.

**Proof.** We give the details. Let $\mathcal{L}''$ be a linkage in $G'$ with
$\theta(\mathcal{L}'') = \theta(\mathcal{L}') + 1/k$. Suppose first that the reduction is 1, 2 or 4.
Then $G'$ is a subgraph of $G$, and so $\mathcal{L}''$ is a linkage in $G$. Moreover
$\theta(\mathcal{L}') = \theta(\mathcal{L})$. For reductions 1 and 2 this holds since the linkage is
not changed. For reduction 4 with $k \ge 2$ it holds since some $P_j$ with $j \ne i$ is not changed,
and $\theta(P)$ is the same for all members $P$ of a linkage. For reduction 4 with $k = 1$ it holds
since, by the reading of reduction 4 given above, the lift of the new path from the chosen lift of
$s_1$ ends at the same point as the chosen lift of $P_1$. Hence $\theta(\mathcal{L}) + 1/k$ is
feasible in $G$.

Now suppose that the reduction is 3, and let $e$ be the edge joining $s_i$ and $v$. Moving $v$ to
$s_i$ along $e$ is done by a continuous map $\pi$ of $\Sigma$ to itself, which maps the closure of
$e$ to $s_i$, is one-to-one elsewhere, and is joined to the identity by a deformation that fixes
$bd(\Sigma)$. Each path of $\mathcal{L}'$ is drawn as the image under $\pi$ of the corresponding
path of $\mathcal{L}$, and so the two have the same winding number, since a deformation fixing
$bd(\Sigma)$ does not change the winding number of a curve between points of $bd(\Sigma)$. Hence
$\theta(\mathcal{L}') = \theta(\mathcal{L})$. The map $\pi$ maps $V(G) - \{s_i\}$ one-to-one onto
$V(G')$, with $v$ mapped to $s_i$, and maps the edges of $G$ other than $e$ one-to-one onto the
edges of $G'$, preserving incidence. Read each path of $\mathcal{L}''$ back in $G$ by this
correspondence, and add $s_i$ and $e$ at the start of the path that now starts at $v$. Since $e$ is
the only edge of $G$ at $s_i$, this gives a linkage $\mathcal{M}$ in $G$. Its image under $\pi$ is
$\mathcal{L}''$, and so $\theta(\mathcal{M}) = \theta(\mathcal{L}'') = \theta(\mathcal{L}) + 1/k$,
by the same argument.

## 8. Algorithm (5.7), page 133: the running time

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that the running time of (5.7) is polynomially bounded, "as may
easily be checked". Reduction 4 does not make $G$ smaller, so the number of steps needs an argument.

**Proof.** We check this. We apply reduction 4 only when none of reductions 1, 2, 3 applies. Each of
reductions 1, 2, 3 makes $|V(G)| + |E(G)|$ smaller, and reduction 4 does not change $G$. After an
application of reduction 4 one of the other reductions applies, as we observed after the list of
reductions, and so no two successive steps both use reduction 4. Let $b = 0$ if the last step used
reduction 4, and $b = 1$ otherwise (in particular before the first step). A step by reduction 1, 2
or 3 decreases $|V(G)| + |E(G)|$ by at least $1$ and increases $b$ by at most $1$, and a step by
reduction 4 decreases $b$ from $1$ to $0$. Hence each step decreases $2(|V(G)| + |E(G)|) + b$ by at
least $1$. This number is $2(|V(G)| + |E(G)|) + 1$ for the original graph, and at least $1$ at the
end, since the last step does not use reduction 4. So the number of steps is at most twice
$|V(G)| + |E(G)|$ for the original graph. Each step takes polynomial time. The regions of $G$, the
strips $\Sigma_i$, and the vertices and edges incident with each $R_i$ may be read from the drawing,
and with them reductions 1, 2 and 3 are tested directly. For reduction 4, a vertex $v$ and a path
$Q_i$ may be found by a search from $s_i$ in the subgraph of the vertices and edges incident with
$R_i$, read on the side of $P_i$ that reduction 4 refers to. When no reduction applies, (5.1) asks
only whether some $s_i$ has valency $1$ and a neighbour in $C_2$. To find a linkage $\mathcal{L}'$
with $\theta(\mathcal{L}') = \theta(\mathcal{L}) + 1/k$, at the end we build the paths $Q_i$ of the
proof of (5.1) by following the boundary of $R_i$ from $s_i$ until it first meets $P_{i+1}$. Then we
undo the steps in reverse order. Deletions need no change, since a linkage of a subgraph is a
linkage of the graph, and a contraction by reduction 3 is undone by adding the contracted edge at
the start of the path that begins at its end, as in the proof of (5.4).
