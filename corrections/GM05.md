# Graph Minors V: corrections

N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. V. Excluding a planar graph*, J. Combin. Theory Ser.
B **41** (1986), 92–114.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM05/GM05.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM05/GM05.pdf) in the
repository [GraphMinorPapers](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers).

The corrections are in the order of the pages.

## 1. Proofs of (1.1)–(1.4), page 93: forests, series-parallel graphs, minors, complete graphs and grids

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper says that (1.1)–(1.4) "are easy to prove" and gives no proofs. It
also does not define series-parallel graphs, which (1.2) uses.

**Proof.** We give the proofs of (1.1)–(1.4). The *simple graph* of a graph is obtained by deleting
its loops and replacing each class of parallel edges by one edge. A graph and its simple graph have
the same tree-decompositions, since (ii) holds for a loop at $v$ as soon as $v$ is in some $X_t$,
which is true by (i). Let $(T, \chi)$ be a tree-decomposition of a graph $G$, and let $t \in V(T)$
have valency 1 in $T$, with neighbour $s$. We shall use two facts. First, if $X_t \subseteq X_s$,
then deleting $t$ from $T$ and $X_t$ from $\chi$ gives a tree-decomposition of $G$, because $X_s$
can play the part of $X_t$ in (i) and (ii), and the paths of $T \setminus t$ are paths of $T$.
Second, if $v \in X_t - X_s$, then every neighbour $x$ of $v$ is in $X_t$. For by (ii) some $X_q$
contains $v$ and $x$, and if $q \ne t$ then $s$ lies on the path of $T$ between $t$ and $q$, and so
$v \in X_t \cap X_q \subseteq X_s$ by (iii), which is false.

*Proof of* (1.1). Suppose first that $G$ has tree-width $\le 1$ and has a circuit $C$. Replacing
each $X_t$ by $X_t \cap V(C)$ gives a tree-decomposition of $C$ of width $\le 1$, since (i)–(iii)
for $C$ follow from (i)–(iii) for $G$. In $C$ every vertex has two distinct neighbours, since $G$ is
simple. We show by induction on $|V(T)|$ that no non-null graph $G'$ in which every vertex has two
distinct neighbours has a tree-decomposition $(T, \chi)$ of width $\le 1$. If $|V(T)| = 1$, then by
(i) the only bag is $V(G')$, which contains a vertex of $G'$ and its two neighbours. This is
impossible. Otherwise let $t$ have valency 1 in $T$, with neighbour $s$. If some $v \in X_t - X_s$,
then $X_t$ contains $v$ and its two neighbours, by the second fact, which is impossible. So
$X_t \subseteq X_s$, and the first fact gives a tree-decomposition of $G'$ of width $\le 1$ with
fewer bags, contrary to induction. Hence $G$ is a forest. Conversely, we show by induction on
$|V(G)|$ that every forest $G$ has tree-width $\le 1$. If $V(G) = \emptyset$, a single empty bag
will do. Otherwise some vertex $v$ of $G$ has at most one neighbour (for instance an end of a
longest path of $G$), and by induction $G \setminus v$ has a tree-decomposition $(T, \chi)$ of width
$\le 1$. Choose $t_0 \in V(T)$ such that $X_{t_0}$ contains the neighbour of $v$, if there is one,
by (i). Add to $T$ a new vertex $t'$ adjacent to $t_0$, with $X_{t'}$ consisting of $v$ and its
neighbours. This is a tree-decomposition of $G$ of width $\le 1$. For (iii), $v$ is in no other bag,
and $X_{t'} - \{v\} \subseteq X_{t_0}$, where $t_0$ lies on the path of $T$ from $t'$ to every other
vertex.

*Proof of* (1.2). The paper does not define series-parallel graphs. We use the usual definition by
series and parallel reductions, in the following form. A graph $G$ is *series-parallel* if its
simple graph can be reduced to the null graph by repeatedly deleting a vertex with at most two
neighbours and, if it has two, joining them by an edge if they are not already adjacent. We call
this a *reduction* at the vertex.

We first show that some bag $X_t$ contains a vertex of $G$ and all its neighbours, if $G$ is
non-null. We use induction on $|V(T)|$. If $|V(T)| = 1$, any vertex will do, since the only bag is
$V(G)$ by (i). Otherwise let $t$ have valency 1 in $T$, with neighbour $s$. If $X_t \subseteq X_s$
we apply induction to the tree-decomposition given by the first fact. Otherwise we take any
$v \in X_t - X_s$, by the second fact.

Now suppose that $G$ has a tree-decomposition of width $\le 2$. We show by induction on $|V(G)|$
that $G$ is series-parallel. By the remark above we may assume that $G$ is simple, and that it is
non-null. Some bag $X_t$ contains a vertex $v$ and all its neighbours, and so $v$ has at most two
neighbours, since $|X_t| \le 3$. Let $G'$ be the graph obtained by the reduction at $v$. Removing
$v$ from every bag gives a tree-decomposition of $G'$ of width $\le 2$. Indeed (i) and (iii) hold.
For (ii), the edges of $G'$ that are edges of $G$ are not incident with $v$, and the new edge of
$G'$, if any, has both ends in $X_t - \{v\}$. By induction $G'$ is series-parallel, and therefore so
is $G$. Conversely, suppose that $G$ is series-parallel. Again we may assume that $G$ is simple. We
use induction on the number of reductions. Let the first reduction be at $v$, giving $G'$. By
induction $G'$ has a tree-decomposition $(T, \chi)$ of width $\le 2$, and it is also a
tree-decomposition of $G \setminus v$, since every edge of $G \setminus v$ is an edge of $G'$. Some
bag $X_{t_0}$ contains all neighbours of $v$. If $v$ has at most one neighbour this follows from
(i). If $v$ has two neighbours, they are adjacent in $G'$, and it follows from (ii). Add to $T$ a
new vertex $t'$ adjacent to $t_0$, with $X_{t'}$ consisting of $v$ and its neighbours. As in the
proof of (1.1), this is a tree-decomposition of $G$, and its width is $\le 2$.

*Proof of* (1.3). Let $(T, \chi)$ be a tree-decomposition of $G$ whose width $w$ is the tree-width
of $G$. We show that $H$ has a tree-decomposition of width $\le w$. Since $H$ is a minor of $G$,
there are disjoint non-empty sets $Y(v) \subseteq V(G)$ $(v \in V(H))$, each inducing a connected
subgraph of $G$, such that for every edge of $H$ with distinct ends $u, v$, some edge of $G$ has one
end in $Y(u)$ and the other in $Y(v)$. (Take for $Y(v)$ the set of vertices of $G$ that are
contracted into $v$.) For $t \in V(T)$ let $X'_t$ be the set of all $v \in V(H)$ with
$Y(v) \cap X_t \ne \emptyset$. Choosing for each $v \in X'_t$ a vertex in $Y(v) \cap X_t$ gives
distinct vertices for distinct $v$, since the sets $Y(v)$ are disjoint, and so $|X'_t| \le |X_t|$.
We claim that $(T, (X'_t))$ is a tree-decomposition of $H$. (i) holds since each $Y(v)$ is
non-empty. For (ii), by the remark above we need only consider edges with two ends. If $u, v$ are
the ends of an edge of $H$, some edge of $G$ has ends $x \in Y(u)$ and $y \in Y(v)$, and some $X_t$
contains $x$ and $y$, and then $u, v \in X'_t$. For (iii), we first show:

(1) *Let $K$ be a connected subgraph of $G$, and let $a, b, c \in V(T)$, where $c$ lies on the path
of $T$ between $a$ and $b$. If $K$ has a vertex in $X_a$ and a vertex in $X_b$, then $K$ has a
vertex in $X_c$.*

Let $x \in V(K) \cap X_a$. We show, by induction on the length of a walk of $K$ from $x$ to $y$,
that for every $b \in V(T)$ with $y \in X_b$, and every $c$ on the path of $T$ between $a$ and $b$,
some vertex of $K$ is in $X_c$. If the walk has length 0 then $y = x$, and
$x \in X_a \cap X_b \subseteq X_c$ by (iii). Otherwise let $y'$ be the vertex of the walk before
$y$. By (ii) some $X_q$ contains $y'$ and $y$. The path of $T$ between $a$ and $b$ is contained in
the union of the paths between $a$ and $q$ and between $q$ and $b$, and so $c$ lies on one of them.
If $c$ lies on the path between $a$ and $q$, we apply induction to the walk from $x$ to $y'$, since
$y' \in X_q$. If $c$ lies on the path between $q$ and $b$, then $y \in X_q \cap X_b \subseteq X_c$
by (iii). This proves (1).

Now if $v \in X'_a \cap X'_b$ and $c$ lies on the path of $T$ between $a$ and $b$, then by (1)
applied to the subgraph induced on $Y(v)$, some vertex of $Y(v)$ is in $X_c$, and so $v \in X'_c$.
This proves (iii), and (1.3) follows.

*Proof of* (1.4). A single bag $V(K_n)$ shows that $K_n$ has tree-width $\le n - 1$. Conversely, in
a tree-decomposition of $K_n$, some bag contains a vertex and all its neighbours, as shown in the
proof of (1.2), and so it contains all $n$ vertices. Hence $K_n$ has tree-width $n - 1$.

Now let $\theta \ge 2$, and for $1 \le i, j \le \theta$ let $r(v_{ij}) = \theta(i - 1) + j - 1$, so
that $r$ maps the vertices of the $\theta$-grid one-to-one onto $\{0, \dots, \theta^2 - 1\}$, and
adjacent vertices have values of $r$ differing by $1$ or $\theta$. Let $L = \theta^2 - \theta$, let
$T$ be the path with vertices $0, 1, \dots, L - 1$ in order, and for $0 \le k \le L - 1$ let $X_k$
be the set of vertices $v$ with $k \le r(v) \le k + \theta$. Each $X_k$ has at most $\theta + 1$
vertices. A vertex $v$ is in $X_k$ for $k = \min(r(v), L - 1)$, and the ends $x, y$ of an edge, with
$r(x) < r(y)$, are both in $X_k$ for $k = \min(r(x), L - 1)$, since
$r(y) \le \min(r(x) + \theta, \theta^2 - 1)$. The bags containing $v$ are those with
$r(v) - \theta \le k \le r(v)$, and they form a subpath of $T$. So this is a tree-decomposition of
width $\le \theta$.

For the converse we need two more facts.

(2) *Let $A_j$ $(j \in J)$ be non-empty sets of vertices of a tree $T$, each containing the path of
$T$ between any two of its members, such that $A_i \cap A_j \ne \emptyset$ for all $i, j \in J$.
Then some vertex of $T$ is in every $A_j$.*

We use induction on $|V(T)|$. If $|V(T)| = 1$ this holds since each $A_j$ is non-empty. Otherwise
let $t$ have valency 1 in $T$, with neighbour $s$. If $A_i \subseteq \{t\}$ for some $i$, then
$t \in A_j$ for every $j$, since $A_i \cap A_j \ne \emptyset$. Otherwise each $A_j$ has a vertex
other than $t$, and so if $t \in A_j$ then $s \in A_j$, since $s$ lies on the path from $t$ to that
vertex. Hence the sets $A_j - \{t\}$ are non-empty, each contains the path of $T \setminus t$
between any two of its members, and any two of them meet, since if $t \in A_i \cap A_j$ then
$s \in A_i \cap A_j$. By induction some vertex of $T \setminus t$ is in all of them. This proves
(2).

(3) *Let $(T, \chi)$ be a tree-decomposition of $G$, and let $M_j$ $(j \in J)$ be connected
subgraphs of $G$ such that for all $i, j \in J$, either $M_i$ and $M_j$ have a common vertex or some
edge of $G$ joins them. Then some bag $X_t$ contains a vertex of every $M_j$.*

For $j \in J$, let $A_j$ be the set of $t \in V(T)$ such that $X_t$ contains a vertex of $M_j$. It
is non-empty by (i), and it contains the path of $T$ between any two of its members, by (1). For
$i, j \in J$, a bag containing a common vertex of $M_i$ and $M_j$ (which exists by (i)), or both
ends of an edge joining them (which exists by (ii)), is in $A_i \cap A_j$. So (3) follows from (2).

Now let $(T, \chi)$ be a tree-decomposition of the $\theta$-grid. Let $R$ be the subgraph induced on
$\{v_{1j} : 1 \le j \le \theta\}$, and let $C$ be the subgraph induced on
$\{v_{i1} : 2 \le i \le \theta\}$. For $2 \le i, j \le \theta$, let $Q_{ij}$ be the subgraph induced
on the union of $\{v_{ij'} : 2 \le j' \le \theta\}$ and $\{v_{i'j} : 2 \le i' \le \theta\}$. These
subgraphs are connected, and any two of them have a common vertex or are joined by an edge. For
$v_{11} \in V(R)$ is adjacent to $v_{21} \in V(C)$. For $2 \le i, j \le \theta$, the vertex $v_{1j}$
of $R$ is adjacent to $v_{2j} \in V(Q_{ij})$, and the vertex $v_{i1}$ of $C$ is adjacent to
$v_{i2} \in V(Q_{ij})$. Finally, for $2 \le i, j, i', j' \le \theta$, the vertex $v_{ij'}$ belongs
to both $Q_{ij}$ and $Q_{i'j'}$. By (3), some bag $X_t$ contains a vertex $x$ of $R$, a vertex $y$
of $C$, and a vertex of every $Q_{ij}$. Let $S$ be the set of vertices $v_{ij} \in X_t$ with
$i, j \ge 2$. If for every $i \ge 2$ some $v_{ij}$ with $j \ge 2$ is in $X_t$, then
$|S| \ge \theta - 1$. Otherwise there exists $i \ge 2$ such that no $v_{ij}$ with $j \ge 2$ is in
$X_t$. Then for each $j \ge 2$, the vertex of $Q_{ij}$ in $X_t$ is $v_{i'j}$ for some $i' \ge 2$,
and so again $|S| \ge \theta - 1$. The vertices $x$ and $y$ are distinct and not in $S$. Hence
$|X_t| \ge \theta + 1$, and the $\theta$-grid has tree-width $\theta$.

## 2. Statement of (1.5), page 93: every graph, not every planar graph

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** (1.5) is printed "For every planar graph $H$, there is a number $w$ such that
every planar graph with no minor isomorphic to $H$ has tree-width $\le w$." The paper proves and
uses this for every graph with no minor isomorphic to $H$. Its restatement (2.1) on page 95 reads
"every graph with no minor isomorphic to $H$", and the proof of (8.8) on page 111 applies (1.5) to
all graphs.

**Correction.** Delete the second "planar". The printed statement is weaker than the corrected one,
so it is still true, but it is not the statement the paper uses.

## 3. Statement of (4.3), page 98: the case $d = n = 0$

**Kind.** Degenerate case.

**What goes wrong.** (4.3) concludes that $n < d\theta^2/2$ or
$m < 2^{d\theta^2/2}(\theta^2 + 2d\theta^{2\theta})$. When $d = n = 0$, both can fail. Take
$A_1 = A_2 = \emptyset$, $m = \theta^2$ and $D_1 = \dots = D_m$ the null graph. Each $D_k$ has
$0 \le d$ components and separates $A_1$ and $A_2$, and there are $n = 0$ paths, but $n = 0$ and
$m = \theta^2$. The result is Graph Minors I, (2.8), with $r = 0$, and the same degenerate case
appears there (see the correction to (2.8) in [GM01.md](GM01.md)).

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis $d + n > 0$. The only use of (4.3), in the proof of (4.4), has
$n = p + q + m \ge p > 0$, by the correction to (4.4) below.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM05.theorem_4_3` in `GraphMinor/GM05/Results.lean`.

## 4. Statement of (4.4), page 98: the case $p = 0$

**Kind.** Degenerate case.

**What goes wrong.** (4.4) gives a $(p, q)$-web with a spider, and a spider is a connected subgraph
of $G$. Take $p = q = 0$ and $G$ the null graph, which the paper allows (the proof of (8.4) on page
110 says "if $G$ is null the result is true"). Then $m = n = 0$, and the empty families form a
$(0, 0)$-web in $G$. But the null graph has no non-null connected subgraph, so there is no spider.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis $p > 0$. The only use of (4.4), in the proof of (4.5), has
$p = \phi_{k+1}$. Since $\phi_{\theta_1} = \theta^2/2$ and
$\phi_k = \phi_{k+1} 2^{\phi_{k+1}\theta^2} \ge \phi_{k+1}$ (page 94), we have
$\phi_{k+1} \ge \theta^2/2 > 0$.

## 7. Statement of (8.11), page 113: a whole sphere

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (8.11) states that if $r$ is a connected open subset of a surface $\Sigma$ and
every subset of $r$ homeomorphic to a circle bounds a closed disc in $\Sigma$, then some open disc
of $\Sigma$ contains $r$. Let $\Sigma$ be a sphere and $r = \Sigma$. Then $r$ is connected and open,
and every circle in it bounds a closed disc. But $r$ is compact and an open disc is not, so no open
disc contains $r$. The same holds whenever $r$ is a whole component of $\Sigma$ homeomorphic to a
sphere.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that $r$ is not a whole component of $\Sigma$ homeomorphic to a
sphere. The corrected statement is true. Its use in (8.12) is discussed in the correction of (8.12) below.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM05.theorem_8_11` in `GraphMinor/GM05/Results.lean` (the corrected
statement), and `GraphMinor.GM05.sphere_counterexample_to_8_11` in
`GraphMinor/GM05/OpenDiscCounterexample.lean` (the counterexample).

## 8. Proof of (8.11), page 113: an open disc including $r$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper assumes (8.11), with (8.9) and (8.10), without proof.

**Proof.** The paper assumes (8.11), as it says before (8.9). We prove the corrected statement. The
set $r$ lies in one component $\Sigma_0$ of $\Sigma$, since it is connected. A closed disc whose
boundary lies in $\Sigma_0$ lies in $\Sigma_0$, since it is connected, and so the hypothesis holds
with $\Sigma_0$ in place of $\Sigma$. Thus we may assume that $\Sigma$ is connected. If $\Sigma$ is
a sphere, then $r \ne \Sigma$, and for any point $p \in \Sigma - r$ the set $\Sigma - \{p\}$ is an
open disc including $r$. So we may assume that $\Sigma$ is not a sphere. Let us call
$Z \subseteq \Sigma$ *flat* if every closed curve in $Z$ is null-homotopic in $\Sigma$. Then $r$ is
flat. For otherwise, by (11.2) of [12] (Graph Minors VII, J. Combin. Theory Ser. B 45 (1988),
212–254), some subset of $r$ homeomorphic to a circle is not null-homotopic in $\Sigma$, but it
bounds a closed disc in $\Sigma$ by hypothesis, and so it is null-homotopic. By Zorn's lemma, $r$ is
included in a maximal set $Y$ that is open, path-connected and flat. (The union of a chain of such
sets containing $r$ is again such a set, since a closed curve in it is compact and so lies in one
member of the chain.) Now let $C \subseteq Y$ be homeomorphic to a circle. Since $C$ is
null-homotopic in $\Sigma$, it bounds a closed disc $D \subseteq \Sigma$, by a theorem of Epstein
(D. B. A. Epstein, Curves on 2-manifolds and isotopies, Acta Math. 115 (1966), 83–107, Theorem 1.7).
The set $Y \cup D$ equals $Y \cup (D - C)$, and so it is open. It is path-connected, since $Y$ and
$D$ are path-connected and both include $C$. It is flat by (11.3) of [12], applied with
$\Delta = D$, since $Y$ is flat and $Y \cap bd(D) = C$ is arc-connected. By the maximality of $Y$,
we have $D \subseteq Y$. Thus every subset of $Y$ homeomorphic to a circle bounds a closed disc
included in $Y$, and by (4.2) of [12], $Y$ is an open disc, since $\Sigma$ is not a sphere. This
proves (8.11).

`GraphMinor.GM05.lemma_8_11_connected_surface` in
`GraphMinor/GM05/OpenDiscContainmentConnected.lean` and `GraphMinor.GM05.flat_of_ambient_discs`,

## 9. Statement of (8.12), page 113: an unused sphere

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** Before (8.12), $\Sigma$ is an orientable surface in which $H$ can be drawn with
$g(\Sigma)$ minimal, where $g(\Sigma)$ is the list of the genera of the components in decreasing
order, compared lexicographically up to the length of the shorter list. A proper prefix of a list is
not comparable with it in this order, so a minimal $\Sigma$ may have a spherical component that a
drawing of $H$ does not use. For example, let $H = K_5$ and let $\Sigma$ be the disjoint union of a
torus and a sphere, with $g(\Sigma) = (1, 0)$. Since $K_5$ is not planar, every orientable surface
in which it can be drawn has a component of genus at least $1$, so $\Sigma$ is minimal. A drawing of
$K_5$ in the torus has the whole sphere as a region, and no open disc contains it.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that the drawing meets every component of $\Sigma$ homeomorphic
to a sphere. Then no region is a whole spherical component, and the printed proof works, with (8.10)
and the corrected (8.11). The surface can be chosen so that every drawing of $H$ in it meets every
spherical component: among the minimal surfaces, take one with the fewest components. If a drawing
missed a spherical component, deleting that component would remove a final $0$ from $g(\Sigma)$, so
the result would still be minimal, with one component fewer. With this choice of $\Sigma$, the
proofs of (8.13) and (8.14) apply the corrected (8.12), and (8.14) holds as printed.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM05.theorem_8_12`, `GraphMinor.GM05.theorem_8_13` and
`GraphMinor.GM05.theorem_8_14` in `GraphMinor/GM05/Results.lean`.

**What is missing.** The proof of (8.14) takes "$2k' + 1$ drawings of $H$ in $\Sigma$, chosen so
that no point of $\Sigma$ is in more than two of them, and only finitely many points of $\Sigma$
are in more than one of them", without saying how to choose them. It also gives briefly the
counting step and the step from (8.13) to graphs drawn in $\Sigma$.

**Proof.** We give the details, in three steps.

First, no graph drawn in $\Sigma$ has two disjoint $H$-expansions. For if $G'$ is drawn in $\Sigma$
and has disjoint $H$-expansions $A_1, A_2$, then contracting edges of $A_1 \cup A_2$ shows that the
graph consisting of two disjoint copies of $H$ is isomorphic to a minor of $G'$. A minor of a graph
drawn in $\Sigma$ can also be drawn in $\Sigma$, and restricting such a drawing to the two copies
gives two disjoint drawings of $H$ in $\Sigma$, contrary to (8.13).

Second, we choose the drawings. Let $m = 2k' + 1$. Subdividing every edge of $H$ once gives a graph
$J$ without loops, and $H$ is isomorphic to a minor of $J$ (contract one of the two halves of each
edge). A drawing of $H$ in $\Sigma$ gives a drawing of $J$. Choose mutually disjoint closed discs
$R_w$ $(w \in V(J))$, with $w$ in the interior of $R_w$, such that an edge of $J$ meets $R_w$ only
if it is incident with $w$, and then in a simple curve from $w$ to the boundary of $R_w$. For each
edge $e$ of $J$, with ends $u$ and $v$, choose $m$ mutually disjoint simple curves
$M_1(e), \dots, M_m(e)$ running beside $e$, each with one end on the boundary of $R_u$, the other
end on the boundary of $R_v$, and no other point in any of the discs, such that curves for distinct
edges are disjoint. We call the ends of these curves *ports*, and so the ports on the boundary of
each $R_w$ are distinct. Now fix $w \in V(J)$, and view $R_w$ as a round disc. For $i = 1, \dots, m$
in turn, choose a point $x_i$ of the boundary of $R_w$, different from the ports and from
$x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}$, and join $x_i$ by straight chords to the ports of the curves $M_i(e)$ for the
edges $e$ incident with $w$. The point $x_i$ and these chords form the $i$th *star* at $w$. A line
meets the boundary circle in at most two points, and so a new chord lies on no line of an earlier
chord. Only finitely many choices of $x_i$ send a new chord through a point where two earlier chords
meet, and we choose $x_i$ to avoid them. Then two stars at $w$ meet in only finitely many points,
and no point is in three stars at $w$.

For $1 \le i \le m$, the $i$th stars at all the vertices of $J$, together with the curves $M_i(e)$
$(e \in E(J))$, form the $i$th drawing. No point of $\Sigma$ is in more than two of these drawings,
and only finitely many are in more than one, since the curves $M_i(e)$ are disjoint from each other
and from the stars except at their ports. Let $G$ be the graph drawn by their union, as above, with
the points $x_i$, the ports, and the points in two drawings as vertices. For $1 \le i \le m$, each
$i$th star is drawn by a connected subgraph of $G$, and each $M_i(e)$ is drawn by an edge of $G$
joining the $i$th stars at the ends of $e$. Contracting the edges of the $i$th stars gives $J$, and
so the union of the $i$th stars and the edges $M_i(e)$ is a $J$-expansion in $G$. Contracting also
one of the two halves of each edge of $H$ shows that it is an $H$-expansion $H'_i$ in $G$, all of
whose vertices lie in $i$th stars. Hence no vertex of $G$ is in more than two of
$H'_1, \dots, H'_m$, because it lies in some disc $R_w$, and no point of $R_w$ is in three stars at
$w$.

Third, let $X \subseteq V(G)$ with $|X| \le k'$. Each vertex in $X$ is in at most two of
$H'_1, \dots, H'_m$, and so $X$ meets at most $2k' < m$ of them. Thus some $H'_i$ is an
$H$-expansion in $G \setminus X$. By the first step, $G$ has no two disjoint $H$-expansions. So for
$k = 2$, neither (i) nor (ii) in the definition of the Erd\"os–P\'osa property holds for $G$, and
this is true for every $k'$.
