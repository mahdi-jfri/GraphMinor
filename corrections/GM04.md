## 1. Proof of (3.1), page 234: the separation given by an edge of a tree-decomposition

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** (3.1) says that for an edge $f$ of $T$ with head $t_1$ and tail $t_2$,
$(\tau \times T^f, \tau \times T_f)$ is a separation of $G$. The paper says that this is almost
identical with results of its references [9] and [8], and omits the proof.

**Proof.** We give the proof. Let $G_1 = \tau \times T^f$ and $G_2 = \tau \times T_f$. By (i),
$G_1^-$ and $G_2^-$ are subhypergraphs of $G^-$, and $G_1^- \cup G_2^- = G^-$ by (ii), since every
vertex of $T$ is in $T^f$ or in $T_f$.

(1) $G_1^- \cap G_2^- = \tau(t_1)^- \cap \tau(t_2)^-$.

Since $t_1 \in V(T^f)$ and $t_2 \in V(T_f)$, the right side is a subhypergraph of the left side.
Conversely, let $v \in V(G_1^- \cap G_2^-)$. Then $v$ is a vertex of $\tau(s)^-$ and of $\tau(r)^-$
for some $s \in V(T^f)$ and $r \in V(T_f)$. Since $T^f$ and $T_f$ are the components of
$T \setminus f$, the path of $T$ between $s$ and $r$ uses $f$, and so it passes through $t_1$ and
$t_2$. By (iv), $v$ is a vertex of $\tau(t_1)^-$ and of $\tau(t_2)^-$. Now suppose that some edge
$e$ is in $E(G_1^- \cap G_2^-)$. Then $e$ is an edge of $\tau(s)^-$ and of $\tau(r)^-$ for some
$s \in V(T^f)$ and $r \in V(T_f)$, and $s \ne r$ since $T^f$ and $T_f$ are disjoint. This is
contrary to (iii), and so $G_1^- \cap G_2^-$ has no edges. This proves (1).

By (1) and (iii), $E(G_1^- \cap G_2^-) = E(\tau(t_1)^- \cap \tau(t_2)^-) = \emptyset$, since
$t_1 \ne t_2$. So $(G_1^-, G_2^-)$ is a separation of $G^-$. Since $T_f$ has root $o(T)$, we have
$\pi(G_2) = \pi(\tau(o(T))) = \pi(G)$ by (v). Since $T^f$ has root $t_1$, we have
$\bar{\pi}(G_1) = \bar{\pi}(\tau(t_1)) = V(\tau(t_1)^- \cap \tau(t_2)^-)$ by (vi), and this is
$V(G_1^- \cap G_2^-)$ by (1). Hence $(G_1, G_2)$ is a separation of $G$.

`GraphMinor/GM04/Decomposition/Basic.lean`. The statement is `GraphMinor.GM04.theorem_3_1` in

## 2. Proof of (3.2), page 234: contracting a tree-decomposition

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** (3.2) says that contracting $T$ onto a set $F \subseteq E(T)$, and replacing
each component $T_s$ of $T \setminus F$ by $\tau \times T_s$, gives a tree-decomposition
$(S, \sigma)$ of $G$. The paper says "The proof is clear, and we omit it."

**Proof.** We give the details. The vertices of $S$ are $o(T)$ and the heads in $T$ of the edges in
$F$, and $o(S) = o(T)$, since $o(T)$ is the head of no edge. An edge of $T$ is in $F$ exactly when
its head is in $V(S)$. Hence for $x \in V(T)$ and $s \in V(S)$, we have $x \in V(T_s)$ if and only
if $x \in V(T^s)$ and no vertex of the directed path of $T$ from $s$ to $x$ except $s$ is in $V(S)$.
In particular every vertex of $T$ is in $T_s$ for exactly one $s \in V(S)$, and $T_s$ is a subtree
of $T^s$.

(1) *Let $u, s \in V(S)$ and $x \in V(T_s)$. Then $x \in V(T^u)$ if and only if $s \in V(S^u)$.*

First, if $s \in V(S^u)$ then $s \in V(T^u)$. For let $f \in F$, with head $t$ and tail $t'$ in $T$,
and let $s'$ be the tail of $f$ in $S$. Then $s'$ is the root of the component of $T \setminus F$
containing $t'$, and so $t' \in V(T^{s'})$, and $t \in V(T^{s'})$. Applying this to the edges of the
directed path of $S$ from $u$ to $s$ shows that $s \in V(T^u)$. Second, if $x \in V(T^u)$ then
$s \in V(T^u)$. For $u$ and $s$ both lie on the directed path of $T$ from $o(T)$ to $x$. If
$s \notin V(T^u)$, then $u \ne s$ and $u$ lies on the directed path of $T$ from $s$ to $x$, which is
impossible by the description of $T_s$ above, since $u \in V(S)$ and $x \in V(T_s)$. Third, if
$s \in V(T^u)$ then $s \in V(S^u)$. We prove this by induction on the length of the directed path of
$S$ from $o(S)$ to $s$. We may assume that $u \ne s$. Then $s \ne o(T)$, since $s \in V(T^u)$, and
so $s$ is the head of an edge $f \in F$. Let $t'$ be the tail of $f$ in $T$, and $s'$ its tail in
$S$. Since $u \ne s$ we have $t' \in V(T^u)$, and $t' \in V(T_{s'})$, so $s' \in V(T^u)$ by the
second statement, and $s' \in V(S^u)$ by induction. Hence $s \in V(S^u)$. Now (1) follows. For if
$x \in V(T^u)$, then $s \in V(T^u)$ by the second statement, and so $s \in V(S^u)$ by the third.
Conversely, if $s \in V(S^u)$, then $s \in V(T^u)$ by the first statement, and
$x \in V(T^s) \subseteq V(T^u)$. This proves (1).

(2) *Let $s_1, s_2, s_3 \in V(S)$, where $s_2$ lies on the path of $S$ between $s_1$ and $s_3$, and
let $x_1 \in V(T_{s_1})$ and $x_3 \in V(T_{s_3})$. Then some vertex of $T_{s_2}$ lies on the path of
$T$ between $x_1$ and $x_3$.*

Let $a$ be the vertex of the path of $T$ between $x_1$ and $x_3$ nearest to $o(T)$. Thus
$x_1, x_3 \in V(T^a)$, and a vertex $t$ of $T$ lies on this path if and only if $t \in V(T^a)$ and
one of $x_1, x_3$ is in $V(T^t)$. Since $s_2$ lies on the path of $S$ between $s_1$ and $s_3$, one
of $s_1, s_3$ is in $V(S^{s_2})$, and so one of $x_1, x_3$ is in $V(T^{s_2})$, by (1). If
$s_2 \in V(T^a)$, then $s_2$ lies on the path of $T$ between $x_1$ and $x_3$, and
$s_2 \in V(T_{s_2})$, as required. Otherwise, since $s_2$ lies on the directed path of $T$ from
$o(T)$ to $x_1$ or to $x_3$, and $a$ lies on both, we have $a \in V(T^{s_2})$. Let $u \in V(S)$ with
$a \in V(T_u)$. Then $x_1, x_3 \in V(T^a) \subseteq V(T^u)$, and so $s_1, s_3 \in V(S^u)$ by (1).
The subtree $S^u$ contains $s_1$ and $s_3$, and so it contains the path of $S$ between them, and in
particular $s_2 \in V(S^u)$. Also $u \in V(S^{s_2})$, by (1) applied to $a$, since
$a \in V(T^{s_2})$. Hence $u = s_2$, and so $a$ is a vertex of $T_{s_2}$ on the path of $T$ between
$x_1$ and $x_3$. This proves (2).

Now we check (i)–(vi) for $(S, \sigma)$. (i) holds by (i) for $(T, \tau)$. For (ii), each vertex and
each edge of $G^-$ belongs to $\tau(x)^-$ for some $x \in V(T)$, by (ii) for $(T, \tau)$, and
$x \in V(T_s)$ for some $s \in V(S)$, and then it belongs to $\sigma(s)^-$. For (iii), let
$s_1, s_2 \in V(S)$ be distinct, and suppose that $e$ is an edge of $\sigma(s_1)^-$ and of
$\sigma(s_2)^-$. Then $e$ is an edge of $\tau(x_1)^-$ and of $\tau(x_2)^-$ for some
$x_1 \in V(T_{s_1})$ and $x_2 \in V(T_{s_2})$, and $x_1 \ne x_2$ since $T_{s_1}$ and $T_{s_2}$ are
disjoint. This is contrary to (iii) for $(T, \tau)$. For (iv), let $s_2$ lie on the path of $S$
between $s_1$ and $s_3$. If $s_2$ is $s_1$ or $s_3$ there is nothing to prove, and so we may assume
that $s_1 \ne s_3$. Then $\sigma(s_1)^- \cap \sigma(s_3)^-$ has no edges, as we just showed. Let $v$
be one of its vertices. Then $v$ is a vertex of $\tau(x_1)^-$ and of $\tau(x_3)^-$ for some
$x_1 \in V(T_{s_1})$ and $x_3 \in V(T_{s_3})$. By (2), some $x_2 \in V(T_{s_2})$ lies on the path of
$T$ between $x_1$ and $x_3$, and so $v$ is a vertex of $\tau(x_2)^-$ by (iv) for $(T, \tau)$. Thus
$v$ is a vertex of $\sigma(s_2)^-$. For (v), the root of $T_{o(S)}$ is $o(S) = o(T)$, and so
$\pi(\sigma(o(S))) = \pi(\tau(o(T))) = \pi(G)$ by (v) for $(T, \tau)$. For (vi), let $f \in F$ have
head $t_1$ and tail $t_2$ in $T$. Then $t_1$ is the head of $f$ in $S$, and its tail in $S$ is the
vertex $s \in V(S)$ with $t_2 \in V(T_s)$. Since $t_1$ is the root of $T_{t_1}$, we have
$\bar{\pi}(\sigma(t_1)) = \bar{\pi}(\tau(t_1)) = V(\tau(t_1)^- \cap \tau(t_2)^-)$ by (vi) for
$(T, \tau)$. This set is contained in $V(\sigma(t_1)^- \cap \sigma(s)^-)$, since
$t_1 \in V(T_{t_1})$ and $t_2 \in V(T_s)$. For the converse inclusion, $T_{t_1}$ is a subtree of
$T^{t_1} = T^f$. Also $T_s$ is a subtree of $T_f$. For if some $x \in V(T_s)$ is in $V(T^{t_1})$,
then $s \in V(S^{t_1})$ by (1), which is false since $s$ is the tail of the edge $f$ of $S$ with
head $t_1$. Hence $\sigma(t_1)^- \subseteq (\tau \times T^f)^-$ and
$\sigma(s)^- \subseteq (\tau \times T_f)^-$. So $V(\sigma(t_1)^- \cap \sigma(s)^-)$ is contained in
$V((\tau \times T^f)^- \cap (\tau \times T_f)^-)$, which is $V(\tau(t_1)^- \cap \tau(t_2)^-)$ by (1)
in the proof of (3.1). This proves (vi), and so $(S, \sigma)$ is a tree-decomposition of $G$.

## 4. Proof of (6.1), page 242: rerouting the paths patch by patch

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** In the proof that (i)' implies (iii), the paper chooses disjoint paths
$P_1, \dots, P_k$ of $K$ with $\sum |E(P_i)|$ minimum and pairings $\delta_e$, and says that
$N_{V(G)} \cup \bigcup (\delta_e : e \in E(G))$ "is a realization of $P$ satisfying (iii), as is
easily seen (for example by rerouting the paths patch by patch)". It also states
$A_e \cap B_e = \emptyset$ and $|A_e| = |B_e|$ without proof.

**Proof.** We give the details. First, the properties of $P_1, \dots, P_k$ stated above follow from
the minimality of $\sum |E(P_i)|$. If some vertex of $P_i$ other than its first were in $X_1$, or
some vertex other than its last were in $X_2$, then a proper subpath of $P_i$ would be a path of $K$
from $X_1$ to $X_2$ disjoint from the other paths. If two vertices of $P_i$ that are not consecutive
in $P_i$ were adjacent in $K$, then using the edge of $K$ between them in place of the part of $P_i$
between them would give a shorter path of $K$ with the same ends, and with vertex set a subset of
$V(P_i)$. In both cases the sum would decrease.

We call the edges of $P_1, \dots, P_k$ *steps*. Since $P_1, \dots, P_k$ are mutually vertex-disjoint
paths, distinct steps $f$ have distinct vertices $f^-$, and distinct vertices $f^+$. Hence
$|A_e| = |\{f : e(f) = e\}| = |B_e|$ for each $e \in E(G)$. Suppose that some vertex $x$ is in
$A_e \cap B_e$, say $x = f_1^- = f_2^+$ where $e(f_1) = e(f_2) = e$. Then $f_1$ and $f_2$ are edges
of the same path $P_i$, the one containing $x$, and $f_1$ is the edge of $P_i$ after $f_2$. So
$f_2^-$ and $f_1^+$ are not consecutive in $P_i$, and they are both incident with $e$, and hence
adjacent in $K$. This is impossible, and so $A_e \cap B_e = \emptyset$. Let $H$ be the realization
displayed above.

Let $f$ be a step, and let $e = e(f)$. Since $\delta_e$ pairs $A_e, B_e$ and $f^- \in A_e$, the
vertex $f^-$ is adjacent in $\delta_e$ to exactly one vertex, which is in $B_e$. So this vertex is
$g^+$ for a unique step $g$, and $e(g) = e$. We put $\pi(f) = g$. If $\pi(f_1) = \pi(f_2) = g$, then
$e(f_1) = e(f_2) = e(g)$, and $f_1^-$ and $f_2^-$ are both adjacent to $g^+$ in the pairing
$\delta_{e(g)}$, so $f_1^- = f_2^-$ and $f_1 = f_2$. Thus $\pi$ is injective. Moreover, since every
edge of each $\delta_e$ joins a vertex of $A_e$ to a vertex of $B_e$, two vertices $x, y$ are
adjacent in $H$ if and only if $\{x, y\} = \{f^-, \pi(f)^+\}$ for some step $f$.

Now we reroute the paths. For $1 \le i \le k$, let $W_i$ be the walk of $H$ that starts at the first
vertex of $P_i$ and proceeds as follows. When it is at a vertex of the form $f^-$, where $f$ is a
step (and $f$ is unique), it moves to $\pi(f)^+$, and we say that this move *uses* $f$. When it is
at a vertex that is not of this form, it stops. Every vertex of $W_i$ is a vertex of some $P_j$.

(1) *If the $m_1$th vertex of $W_{i_1}$ equals the $m_2$th vertex of $W_{i_2}$, then $i_1 = i_2$ and
$m_1 = m_2$.*

We use induction on $m_1$. The first vertex of a path $P_j$ is not $f^+$ for any step $f$, since
$f^+$ is not the first vertex of the path containing $f$, and the paths are disjoint. Hence if one
of $m_1, m_2$ is $0$, then so is the other, and then the first vertices of $P_{i_1}$ and $P_{i_2}$
are equal, and so $i_1 = i_2$. If $m_1, m_2 > 0$, then the common vertex is
$\pi(f_1)^+ = \pi(f_2)^+$, where $f_1^-$ and $f_2^-$ are the preceding vertices of $W_{i_1}$ and
$W_{i_2}$. Then $\pi(f_1) = \pi(f_2)$, since distinct steps have distinct vertices $f^+$, and so
$f_1 = f_2$ since $\pi$ is injective. Hence the preceding vertices are equal. By induction
$i_1 = i_2$ and $m_1 - 1 = m_2 - 1$. This proves (1).

By (1), distinct moves of $W_1, \dots, W_k$ use distinct steps, since a move from $f^-$ uses $f$.
Hence the total number of moves is at most $\sum |E(P_i)|$. So each $W_i$ stops, and by (1),
$W_1, \dots, W_k$ are mutually vertex-disjoint paths of $H$.

(2) *The last vertex of each $W_i$ is in $X_2$.*

The last vertex $z$ of $W_i$ is a vertex of some $P_j$, and it is not $f^-$ for any step $f$. Every
vertex of $P_j$ except its last is $f^-$ for the edge $f$ of $P_j$ that leaves it. So $z$ is the
last vertex of $P_j$, and $z \in X_2$. This proves (2).

Any two vertices adjacent in $H$ are both incident with some edge of $G$, and so they are adjacent
in $K$. So $W_1, \dots, W_k$ are mutually vertex-disjoint paths of $K$ from $X_1$ to $X_2$, by (2),
and by the minimality of $\sum |E(P_i)|$ they have at least this many edges in total. Since the
moves use distinct steps, it follows that every step is used by exactly one move. Hence every edge
of $H$ is an edge of some $W_i$, since the edge between $f^-$ and $\pi(f)^+$ is the move that uses
$f$.

(3) *No vertex of $W_i$ except its first is in $X_1$, and no vertex except its last is in $X_2$.*

Let $v$ be a vertex of $W_i$, and let $v \in V(P_j)$. If $v \in X_1$, then $v$ is the first vertex
of $P_j$, which is the first vertex of $W_j$, and so $v$ is the first vertex of $W_i$, by (1). If
$v \in X_2$, then $v$ is the last vertex of $P_j$, and so $v$ is not $f^-$ for any step $f$. Thus
$W_i$ stops at $v$, and $v$ is its last vertex. This proves (3).

Finally, every edge of $H$ is an edge of some $W_j$, and the paths $W_j$ are disjoint. So an edge of
$H$ with an end in $W_i$ is an edge of $W_i$. Hence $W_1, \dots, W_k$ are components of $H$, and
every vertex of $G$ in none of them is an isolated vertex of $H$. By (2) and (3), $H$ satisfies
(iii).

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM04.theorem_6_1_disjointPaths_imp_iii` in
`GraphMinor/GM04/Patchwork/Menger.lean`. The statement (6.1) is `GraphMinor.GM04.theorem_6_1` in
`GraphMinor/GM04/Results.lean`.

## 5. Statement of (8.2)(ii), page 248: the sets $\Delta(e)$

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** (8.2) gives conditions (i)–(vi) under which an $\Omega$-patchwork
$P = (G, \mu, \Delta, \phi)$ is simulated in a removable $\Omega$-patchwork
$P' = (G', \mu', \Delta', \phi')$. Condition (ii) asks only that, for
$e \in \operatorname{dom}(\mu)$, $\gamma$ maps $\mu(e)$ to $\mu'(\alpha(e))$. It does not compare
$\Delta(e)$ with $\Delta'(\alpha(e))$, while condition (viii) of an expansion (page 244) requires
that $\mu(e) \to \mu'(\eta(e))$ maps $\Delta(e)$ to $\Delta'(\eta(e))$. Let $S$ and $S'$ have one
vertex each, let $G$ and $G'$ have the same vertex set $V = \{u, v\}$ and one edge each, $e$ and
$\alpha(e)$, with $e \in \operatorname{dom}(\mu)$, $\alpha(e) \in \operatorname{dom}(\mu')$ and
$\mu(e) = \mu'(\alpha(e))$, and let $\gamma$ be the identity. Let $\Delta(e) = \{N_V, K_V\}$ and
$\Delta'(\alpha(e)) = \{N_V\}$, where $N_V$ and $K_V$ are the graphs on $V$ with no edge and with
one edge, and let $\phi(e) \le \phi'(\alpha(e))$. Then $P'$ is removable, and (i)–(vi) hold: (iii)
is empty since $e \in \operatorname{dom}(\mu)$, and (v) and (vi) are empty since $S$ and $S'$ have
no vertex besides their centres. But an expansion of $P$ in $P'$ must map $e$ to $\alpha(e)$, the
only edge of $G'$, and then (viii) fails because $K_V \notin \Delta'(\alpha(e))$. So $P$ is not
simulated in $P'$.

**Correction.** Add to (ii): "and $\mu(e) \to \mu'(\alpha(e))$ maps $\Delta(e)$ to
$\Delta'(\alpha(e))$". With this condition the printed proof works. The only use of (8.2) is in the
proof of (8.4) on page 250, and there Claims (4) and (6)(ii) give
$\Delta_i(e) = \Delta_{i'}(\alpha(e))$ before (8.2) is applied. So the use satisfies the added
condition.

## 6. Derivation of (1.5) from (9.1), page 253: simulation of $P(G)$ gives a minor

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After defining the patchwork $P(G)$ of a graph $G$, the paper says "If $P(G)$
is simulated in $P(G')$ then $G$ is isomorphic to a minor of $G'$, and hence (9.1) implies (1.5)."
It gives no proof of the first statement, and does not check that $P(G)$ is robust, which (9.1)
needs.

**Proof.** We give the details. Let $n \ge 0$, and let $G_1, G_2, \dots$ be a countable sequence of
graphs, each of tree-width $\le n$. Each $P(G_i)$ is robust. For let $e \in E(G_i)$, and let
$X, Y \subseteq V(e)$ be disjoint with $|X| = |Y|$. Since $|V(e)| \le 2$, either
$X = Y = \emptyset$, and then $N_{V(e)}$ pairs $X, Y$, or $|X| = |Y| = 1$ and $V(e) = X \cup Y$, and
then $K_{V(e)}$ pairs $X, Y$. By (9.1) there exist $i' > i \ge 1$ such that $P(G_i)$ is simulated in
$P(G_{i'})$. So it remains to prove the first statement.

Let $P(G) = ((G, 0), \mu, \Delta, \phi)$ and $P(G') = ((G', 0), \mu', \Delta', \phi')$, and let
$\eta$ be a realizable expansion of $P(G)$ in $P(G')$. We refer to the conditions (i)–(ix) in the
definition of an expansion.

(1) *For $v \in V(G)$ and $e \in E(G)$, $v$ is an end of $e$ if and only if $\eta(v)$ contains an
end of $\eta(e)$.*

Since $\operatorname{dom}(\mu) = E(G)$, by (vi) $\mu(e)$ and $\mu'(\eta(e))$ have the same length.
If $v$ is an end of $e$, then $v$ is the $j$th term of $\mu(e)$ for some $j$, since
$\bar{\mu}(e) = V(e)$, and $\eta(v)$ contains the $j$th term of $\mu'(\eta(e))$ by (vi), which is an
end of $\eta(e)$. Conversely, let $x \in \eta(v)$ be an end of $\eta(e)$. Then $x$ is the $j$th term
of $\mu'(\eta(e))$ for some $j$. Let $w$ be the $j$th term of $\mu(e)$. Then $w$ is an end of $e$,
and $x \in \eta(w)$ by (vi). Since $x \in \eta(v) \cap \eta(w)$, it follows that $v = w$ by (ii).
This proves (1).

(2) *For $v \in V(G)$ and $e \in E(G)$, $e$ is a loop at $v$ if and only if every end of $\eta(e)$
is in $\eta(v)$.*

Suppose that $e$ is a loop at $v$, and let $x$ be an end of $\eta(e)$. As in the proof of (1),
$x \in \eta(w)$ for some end $w$ of $e$, and $w = v$ since $v$ is the only end of $e$. Conversely,
suppose that every end of $\eta(e)$ is in $\eta(v)$, and let $a$ be an end of $e$. By (1), $\eta(a)$
contains an end of $\eta(e)$, which is also in $\eta(v)$, and so $a = v$ by (ii). This proves (2).

(3) *For each $v \in V(G)$, any two vertices in $\eta(v)$ are joined by a path of
$G' \setminus \eta(E(G))$ with all its vertices in $\eta(v)$.*

Let $R$ be a realization of $P(G') \setminus \eta(E(G))$ that realizes $\eta$, and let $D$ be the
component of $R$ with vertex set $\eta(v)$. Each edge of $R$ joins two vertices $a, b$ that are
adjacent in $\delta_{e'}$ for some $e' \in E(G') - \eta(E(G))$, where
$\delta_{e'} \in \Delta'(e') = \{N_{V(e')}, K_{V(e')}\}$. Since $N_{V(e')}$ has no edges,
$\delta_{e'} = K_{V(e')}$, and so $a$ and $b$ are the two ends of $e'$. Thus each edge of $D$ may be
replaced by an edge of $G' \setminus \eta(E(G))$ with the same ends, and (3) follows since $D$ is
connected.

Now for each $v \in V(G)$ choose a spanning tree $F_v$ of the subgraph of $G' \setminus \eta(E(G))$
with vertex set $\eta(v)$ and with all the edges of $G' \setminus \eta(E(G))$ that have both ends in
$\eta(v)$. It exists by (3). The ends of each $\eta(e)$ lie in sets $\eta(v)$, as in the proof of
(1). Let $G''$ be the subgraph of $G'$ with vertex set the union of the sets $\eta(v)$
$(v \in V(G))$, and with edge set $\eta(E(G))$ together with the edges of all the trees $F_v$.
Contract the edges of every $F_v$. Each $\eta(v)$ becomes a single vertex, and we identify it with
$v$. Then distinct edges of $G$ give distinct edges $\eta(e)$, by (iii), and by (1) and (2) each
$\eta(e)$ becomes an edge with the same ends as $e$. So the graph obtained is isomorphic to $G$, and
$G$ is isomorphic to a minor of $G'$.

## 7. Statement of (9.2)(c), page 253: distinct $v_1, v_2$

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** Part (c) of the conclusion of (9.2) asks that "for $v_1, v_2 \in N_i$,
$\sigma(v_1)$ and $\sigma(v_2)$ are disjoint". Taking $v_1 = v_2$, this asks that the component
$\sigma(v_1)$ of $\Xi$ be disjoint from itself, which is impossible since it is non-empty. So the
conclusion fails as soon as some $N_i$ is non-empty. For example, let every
$(U_i, N_i, \pi_i, \mu_i, \phi_i)$ be the painting with $U_i = N_i = \{p\}$, no cells and empty
marches. Its hypergraph has tree-width $0$, and (b) gives $p$ a component $\sigma(p)$.

**Correction.** Part (c) reads "for distinct $v_1, v_2 \in N_i$, $\sigma(v_1)$ and $\sigma(v_2)$ are
disjoint", which is what the proof gives.
