## 1. Proof of (2.2), Claim (2), pages 53–54: the enlarged graph

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** In the proof of Claim (2) of (2.2), a new edge $e$ joining $v_{i-1}$ and
$v_{i+1}$ is added in a region $R$, and the paper says: "We claim that this enlarged graph satisfies
(i)–(iv), contrary to (v). Verifying this is straightforward and is left to the reader. (The only
part which is not quite trivial is checking the radius, using the condition $d' - 1 < d$, and this
too is easy.)"

**Proof.** We give the details. Let $G''$ and $M''$ be the enlarged graph and drawing, let $H''$ be
$H$ with the new edge $e$ added, and let $N''$ be its drawing. The edge $e$ joins two vertices of
$H$ and lies in $R$, and so (ii) and (iv) still hold, and $G$ is still isomorphic to a minor of
$G''$. Since $v_{i-1} \in D$ and $v_{i+1} \in D'$ lie in different components of $H \setminus v$,
they are distinct and not adjacent in $G'$, and so $G''$ is simple. The perimeter of the infinite
region of $N''$ is the closed walk above with $v_{i-1}, e_{i-1}, v, e_i, v_{i+1}$ replaced by
$v_{i-1}, e, v_{i+1}$. It has the same vertices, since $v = v_j$ is still on it, and so (iii) holds.
It remains to show that $\rho(M'') \le d$. Call a region of $M'$ *inner* if it is contained in the
infinite region of $N$ and, when $d' > 1$, it is inside $C_{d'-1}$. Call a region of $M''$ *inner*
if the same holds with $N''$ in place of $N$. The region $R$ is an inner region of $M'$, and $M''$
differs from $M'$ only inside $R$. So every region of $M''$ is inner, or is the triangle, or is a
region of $M'$ that is not inner.

($\alpha$) *Let $d' > 1$ and $1 \le h \le d'-1$. Every vertex of $C_h$ is incident with a region of
$M'$ outside $C_h$ that has distance at most $h-1$ in $M'$, by a sequence of regions outside $C_h$.*

We use induction on $h$. By (S1) no vertex is outside $C_1$, and by (S2) no edge is outside $C_1$.
So the infinite region of $M'$ is bounded by $C_1$, and it is incident with every vertex of $C_1$.
This proves the case $h = 1$. Now let $1 \le h < d'-1$ and $u \in V(C_{h+1})$. Some region $Q$ of
$M'$ inside $C_h$ and outside $C_{h+1}$ is incident with $u$. By (S1) and (S2) no vertex lies
strictly between $C_h$ and $C_{h+1}$, and every edge strictly between them has an end in $C_h$. So
$Q$ is incident with a vertex of $C_h$, for otherwise it would be bounded by $C_{h+1}$ alone, and
would contain $C_h$. By induction that vertex is incident with a region outside $C_h$ of distance at
most $h-1$, by a sequence of regions outside $C_h$. Adding $Q$ to this sequence proves ($\alpha$)
for $h+1$.

Since $M''$ differs from $M'$ only inside $C_{d'-1}$, ($\alpha$) also holds for $M''$.

($\beta$) *Every inner region of $M''$ has distance at most $d'-1$ in $M''$.*

If $d' = 1$, then $N'' = M''$, and the only inner region is the infinite region of $M''$. Let
$d' > 1$, and let $Q$ be an inner region of $M''$. Then $Q$ is incident with a vertex of $C_{d'-1}$.
For otherwise, since every vertex inside $C_{d'-1}$ is in $H$ by (S1), every edge on the boundary of
$Q$ is an edge of $H''$. Then $Q$ would be a region of $N''$, contained in the infinite region of
$N''$ and so equal to it. This is impossible, since that region contains $C_{d'-1}$ and $Q$ is
inside $C_{d'-1}$. By ($\alpha$) with $h = d'-1$, that vertex is incident with a region of distance
at most $d'-2$, and so $Q$ has distance at most $d'-1$.

($\gamma$) *Every vertex of $H''$ on the perimeter of the infinite region of $N''$ is incident with
an inner region of $M''$, which has distance at most $d'-1$.*

For such a vertex $x$ is incident with a region of $M''$ contained in the infinite region of $N''$.
That region is inside $C_{d'-1}$ if $d' > 1$, since $x$ is. So it is inner, and it has distance at
most $d'-1$ by ($\beta$).

($\delta$) *Every region $Q$ of $M''$ that is a region of $M'$ and is not inner has distance at most
$d$ in $M''$.*

Let $R_0, R_1, \dots, R_k = Q$ be regions of $M'$ as in the definition of $d(Q)$ in $M'$, with
$k \le d$. If none of them is inner, they are regions of $M''$, and consecutive ones share a vertex
of $M''$, and so $Q$ has distance at most $k$ in $M''$. Otherwise let $m$ be the largest index such
that $R_m$ is inner. Then $m < k$, and $R_{m+1}, \dots, R_k$ are regions of $M''$. Moreover
$m \ge d'-1$. For two regions incident with a common vertex are on the same side of every $C_h$ that
does not contain that vertex, and so two consecutive regions of the sequence are separated by at
most one of $C_1, \dots, C_{d'-1}$, while $R_0$ is outside $C_1$ and $R_m$ is inside $C_{d'-1}$. Let
$w$ be a vertex incident with $R_m$ and $R_{m+1}$. If $w \in V(H)$, then $w$ is on the perimeter of
the infinite region of $N$, since $R_m$ is contained in that region, and this perimeter has the same
vertices as that of $N''$. By ($\gamma$), $w$ is incident with a region of $M''$ of distance at most
$d'-1$, and so $Q$ has distance at most $d'-1+k-m \le k \le d$. Otherwise $d' > 1$ and
$w \in V(C_{d'-1})$, by (S1). By ($\alpha$), $w$ is incident with a region of $M''$ of distance at
most $d'-2$, and so $Q$ has distance at most $d'-2+k-m < d$.

Finally, $v$ is incident with the triangle, and $v$ is on the perimeter of the infinite region of
$N''$. So the triangle has distance at most $d' \le d$, by ($\gamma$). By ($\beta$) and ($\delta$),
every other region of $M''$ also has distance at most $d$, and so $\rho(M'') \le d$, as required.

## 2. Proof of (2.3), page 55: the minor of $N(d, \lambda)$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After showing that the graph left by deleting the shell is a forest, the paper
says: "The rest of the proof is obvious, although tedious to write out, and we omit it." The text
below gives this part.

**Proof.** We give the omitted part. If $d = 0$, then $G$ itself is a forest, and so it is
isomorphic to a minor of some $Y_\lambda = N(0, \lambda)$. So we assume that $d \ge 1$. Let $F$ be
the forest above. By (S1) its vertices are inside $C_d$, and so all of $F$ is inside $C_d$, since
its edges do not meet $C_d$.

*Step 1: we may assume that $F$ is a tree or is null.* Suppose that $F$ has at least two components,
let $F_1$ be one of them, and let $F_2$ be the union of the others. We claim that the closure of
some region of $M$ inside $C_d$ contains a vertex of $F_1$ and a vertex of $F_2$. Since $F$ has no
circuit and is disjoint from $C_d$, the only circuit of the drawing formed by $F$ and $C_d$ is
$C_d$. Two points not in a drawing in the plane that are not separated by any circuit of the drawing
lie in the same region of it. So the set $\Omega$ of points inside $C_d$ that are not in $U(F)$ is a
region of this drawing, and in particular it is connected. Every vertex inside $C_d$ is in $F$, and
by (S2) every edge with its interior points inside $C_d$ is in $F$ or joins $C_d$ and $F$. So
$\Omega$ is the union of the regions of $M$ inside $C_d$ and the edges of $M$ joining $C_d$ and $F$.
Each such edge is in the closure of the two regions beside it. Let $v_1 \in V(F_1)$ and
$v_2 \in V(F_2)$. Since $U(F)$ has no interior point, $v_1$ is in the closure of $\Omega$, and so in
the closure of some region of $M$ inside $C_d$, and the same holds for $v_2$. Let $Z$ be the set of
regions of $M$ inside $C_d$ whose closure contains a vertex of $F_1$. If a region in $Z$ has $v_2$
in its closure, the claim holds. Otherwise $Z$ is non-empty, and so is the set of the other regions
of $M$ inside $C_d$. Suppose that no edge of $M$ in $\Omega$ has a region in $Z$ on one side and a
region not in $Z$ on the other. Then the regions in $Z$, with the edges in $\Omega$ beside them,
form an open subset of $\Omega$, the other regions with the edges beside them form another, and
these two sets are disjoint, non-empty and cover $\Omega$, which is impossible since $\Omega$ is
connected. So some edge $g$ in $\Omega$ has a region in $Z$ on one side and a region not in $Z$ on
the other. It joins a vertex of $C_d$ and a vertex $w$ of $F$, and $w$ is in the closure of both
regions. Since one of these regions is not in $Z$, $w$ is not in $F_1$, and so $w \in V(F_2)$. Thus
the region beside $g$ in $Z$ satisfies the claim. Now let $q$ be a region as in the claim, with
$u_1 \in V(F_1)$ and $u_2 \in V(F_2)$ in its closure, and add a new edge joining $u_1$ and $u_2$,
drawn in $q$. The new graph is simple, since an edge of $G$ joining $u_1$ and $u_2$ would be an edge
of $F$ joining two of its components. The circuits $C_1, \dots, C_d$ are still nested, and (S1) and
(S2) still hold, since the new edge joins two vertices inside $C_d$. The new forest $F$ has one
component fewer, and $G$ is isomorphic to a minor of the new graph. From now on we use only that $G$
is simple, that $C_1, \dots, C_d$ is a nested sequence of circuits satisfying (S1) and (S2), and
that $F$ is a forest inside $C_d$, and these properties hold for the new graph. Repeating this step,
we may assume that $F$ is a tree or is null.

*Step 2: the edges.* Every edge of $G$ is in some $C_j$, or joins $C_j$ and $C_{j+1}$ for some
$j < d$, or joins $C_d$ and $F$, or is in $F$. For an edge joining two vertices of $C_j$ is in $C_j$
by (S2), and an edge joining $C_j$ and $C_{j'}$ with $j' > j + 1$, or $C_j$ and $F$ with $j < d$,
would have one end inside $C_{j+1}$ and the other outside, and so it would cross $C_{j+1}$. For
$1 \le j \le d$, let $G_j$ be the subgraph of $G$ formed by $C_j, \dots, C_d$, $F$, and the edges
between them. By (S1), $G_1 = G$.

*Step 3: the cyclic orders.* We use the following consequence of the Jordan curve theorem.

(A) *Let $D'$ be a closed disc in the interior of a closed disc $D$ in the plane, and let
$A_1, \dots, A_q$ be arcs, each with one end in $bd(D')$, the other end in $bd(D)$, and its other
points in the interior of $D$ and not in $D'$, such that no point other than an end is in two of
them. Then, going once clockwise around $bd(D')$ from any point that is not an end of one of them,
we meet their ends in some order $A_1, \dots, A_q$, and going once clockwise around $bd(D)$ from a
suitable point, we meet their ends in the same order, where arcs with a common end are met
together.*

For two of the arcs, together with a portion of $bd(D')$ and a portion of $bd(D)$, bound a closed
disc that lies between $bd(D')$ and $bd(D)$, such that both portions run clockwise from one of the
two arcs to the other. Every other arc without its ends is connected and misses these two arcs, and
so it lies in this disc or outside it. So any three of the arcs meet both circles in the same cyclic
order, and (A) follows.

Let $j < d$. Each edge between $C_j$ and $C_{j+1}$ has its interior points between $C_j$ and
$C_{j+1}$, since its interior is connected, misses both circuits, and has an end on each. By (A),
applied to the discs bounded by $C_{j+1}$ and $C_j$, these edges can be listed so that their ends in
$C_{j+1}$, and also their ends in $C_j$, are in the clockwise cyclic order of these circuits.

Now suppose that $F$ is a tree. Choose a closed disc $K$ inside $C_d$ that contains $U(F)$ in its
interior and lies so close to $U(F)$ that it meets no edge of $M$ other than those of $F$ and those
joining $F$ and $C_d$, and meets each edge $g$ joining $F$ and $C_d$ only in points near the end of
$g$ in $F$. (Such discs exist since $U(F)$ is a tree. By a homeomorphism of the plane we may assume
that the edges of $F$ are polygonal, and then the points within a small enough distance of $U(F)$
form one.) Go once clockwise around $bd(K)$, starting from a point near a vertex $t$ of $F$ that is
on no edge of $M$. On the way we pass along the edges of $F$, each twice, and this gives a closed
walk $W = w_0, w_1, \dots, w_m$ of $F$ with $w_0 = w_m = t$ which passes each edge of $F$ twice.
Each $k$ with $0 \le k \le m$ is a *visit* of $W$ to $w_k$, and there $bd(K)$ passes near $w_k$,
through a sector at $w_k$ between two consecutive edges of $F$ (for $k = 0$ and $k = m$, through the
parts of such a sector after and before the starting point). An edge $g$ joining $w_k$ and $C_d$
lies near $w_k$ in one of these sectors, and we say that $g$ *leaves $F$ at* the corresponding
visit. The part of $g$ after its last point in $K$ is an arc from $bd(K)$ to $C_d$, and its end in
$bd(K)$ is where $bd(K)$ passes this visit. By (A), applied to $K$ and the disc bounded by $C_d$,
the edges joining $F$ and $C_d$ can be listed so that the visits at which they leave $F$ are in the
order of $W$, starting from visit $0$, and their ends in $C_d$ are in the clockwise cyclic order of
$C_d$.

*Step 4: two facts about $N(d', \lambda)$.* First, for $d' \ge 1$, $N(d', \lambda)$ is isomorphic to
a minor of $N(d', \lambda + 1)$, in such a way that each vertex of a circle is represented by a set
containing two consecutive vertices of the corresponding circle. To see this, number the radial
lines of $N(d', \lambda + 1)$ as $1, \dots, 3 \cdot 2^\lambda$ in order, so that for each $i$ the
end-vertices of $Y_{\lambda + 1}$ on the lines $2i - 1$ and $2i$ have a common neighbour $u_i$. On
each circle, contract the edges between the lines $2i - 1$ and $2i$, and delete the radial edges on
the even lines, which are now parallel to those on the odd lines. Then contract the edge joining
each $u_i$ to the end-vertex on the line $2i - 1$, and delete the edge joining it to the end-vertex
on the line $2i$. Deleting the end-vertices of $Y_{\lambda + 1}$ leaves $Y_\lambda$, with the
vertices $u_i$ as its end-vertices, in the same order. So the result is $N(d', \lambda)$, as
required. We call a model of $G_j$ as a minor of $N(d - j + 1, \lambda)$ *cyclic* if each vertex of
$C_j$ is represented by a set that contains an interval of the outer circle, and these intervals
cover that circle and are in the clockwise cyclic order of $C_j$. Composing a cyclic model with the
minor above doubles the length of each of its intervals, and so the intervals of a cyclic model may
be made as long as we wish.

Second, let $B_h$ $(h \ge 1)$ be the rooted binary tree in which each vertex at distance less than
$h - 1$ from the root has two children, a left one and a right one, and all leaves are at distance
$h - 1$ from the root. Its leaves are ordered from left to right. We show by induction on $|E(T)|$
that for every tree $T$, vertex $t$ of $T$, and closed walk $w_0, w_1, \dots, w_m$ of $T$ with
$w_0 = w_m = t$ that passes each edge of $T$ twice, there exist $h \ge 1$ and disjoint non-empty
sets $X(v) \subseteq V(B_h)$ $(v \in V(T))$, each inducing a connected subgraph, such that the root
of $B_h$ is in $X(t)$, for each edge of $T$ with ends $u, v$ some edge of $B_h$ joins $X(u)$ and
$X(v)$, and there are leaves $l_0, \dots, l_m$ of $B_h$, in order from left to right, with
$l_k \in X(w_k)$ for $0 \le k \le m$. If $T$ has no edge, then $m = 0$, and we take $h = 1$ and
$X(t) = \{l_0\}$, where $l_0$ is the root. Otherwise let $e$ be the first edge of the walk, joining
$t$ and $y = w_1$. Deleting $e$ from $T$ leaves two trees, $T_y$ containing $y$ and $T_t$ containing
$t$. Since the walk passes $e$ twice and $e$ is the only edge between $T_y$ and $T_t$, the walk
consists of $t$, then a closed walk of $T_y$ from $y$, then a closed walk of $T_t$ from $t$, and
these two walks pass each edge of $T_y$ and of $T_t$ twice. By induction they have sets as above.
Sets in $B_{h'}$ give sets in $B_{h' + 1}$, by copying them into the subtree at the left child of
the root and adding the root to $X(t)$, and so we may assume that both are in $B_{h'}$ for the same
$h'$. Let $h = h' + 2$, let $a$ and $b$ be the children of the root of $B_h$, and let $a_1, a_2$ and
$b_1, b_2$ be the children of $a$ and of $b$, all from left to right. The subtrees at $a_2$ and
$b_1$ are copies of $B_{h'}$. Copy the sets for $T_y$ into the subtree at $a_2$ and the sets for
$T_t$ into the subtree at $b_1$, and add to $X(t)$ the root, $a$, $b$, and the path from $a_1$ down
to its leftmost leaf, which we take as $l_0$. Then $X(t)$ induces a connected subgraph, since it
contains $b_1$, and the edge from $a$ to $a_2 \in X(y)$ joins $X(t)$ and $X(y)$. For
$l_1, \dots, l_m$ we take the leaves chosen for the walk of $T_y$ and then those chosen for the walk
of $T_t$. They are in order from left to right, after $l_0$, since the subtree at $a_2$ lies to the
left of the subtree at $b_1$. This completes the induction.

Now apply this to $F$, $t$ and $W$, and let $\kappa \ge 1$ and $\lambda = h + \kappa$. The subtree
of $Y_\lambda$ at a neighbour of its centre is a copy of $B_\lambda$, whose leaves are end-vertices
of $Y_\lambda$ in the order of the central circle, and its vertices at distance at most $h - 1$ from
its root form a copy of $B_h$. Use the sets $X(v)$ in this copy, and add to $X(w_k)$, for
$0 \le k \le m$, the descendants of $l_k$ that are not end-vertices of $Y_\lambda$. This gives a
model of $F$ as a minor of $Y_\lambda$ with its end-vertices deleted, in which the $2^\kappa$
end-vertices below $l_k$ form an interval of the central circle, *owned* by $k$, whose vertices are
adjacent to vertices of $X(w_k)$. These intervals are disjoint and in the order of $W$ around the
central circle, and their length $2^\kappa$ is as large as we wish.

*Step 5: the induction.* We show that $G_j$ has a cyclic model for $j = d, d - 1, \dots, 1$. For
$j = 1$, since $G_1 = G$, this proves (2.3).

Let $j = d$, let $p = |V(C_d)|$, and let $g_1, \dots, g_q$ be the edges joining $F$ and $C_d$,
listed as in Step 3, where $g_i$ leaves $F$ at the visit $k_i$ and ends at $x_i \in V(C_d)$. (If $F$
is null, then $q = 0$.) If $F$ is a tree, take the model of $F$ of Step 4 with
$2^\kappa \ge q(p + 1)$. Since $k_1 \le \dots \le k_q$, we can choose for each $i$ an end-vertex
$z_i$ in the interval owned by $k_i$, so that $z_1, \dots, z_q$ are in order around the central
circle, with at least $p$ other vertices of this circle between any two of them that are consecutive
in the cyclic order. Since $x_1, \dots, x_q$ are in the cyclic order of $C_d$, we can then divide
the central circle into intervals $I(x)$ $(x \in V(C_d))$, each with at least one vertex and in the
clockwise cyclic order of $C_d$, with $z_i \in I(x_i)$ for each $i$. (The spare vertices between
consecutive $z_i$ leave room for the intervals of the vertices of $C_d$ that lie strictly between
$x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$ in the cyclic order.) If $q = 0$, we only need $3 \cdot 2^{\lambda - 1} \ge p$.
Represent each $x \in V(C_d)$ by $I(x)$, each edge of $C_d$ by the edge of the central circle
between the intervals of its ends, which are consecutive since $C_d$ is a circuit, each vertex and
edge of $F$ as in Step 4, and each $g_i$ by the edge of $Y_\lambda$ joining $z_i$ to its neighbour,
which is in the set of the end $w_{k_i}$ of $g_i$ in $F$. This is a cyclic model of $G_d$ in
$N(1, \lambda)$.

Now let $j < d$, let $p = |V(C_j)|$, and let $g_1, \dots, g_q$ be the edges between $C_j$ and
$C_{j+1}$, listed as in Step 3, where $g_i$ joins $y_i \in V(C_j)$ and $x_i \in V(C_{j+1})$. Take a
cyclic model of $G_{j+1}$ in $N(d - j, \lambda)$ whose intervals each have at least $q(p + 1)$
vertices, which exists by the first fact of Step 4. Let $I(x)$ be its interval for
$x \in V(C_{j+1})$. The graph $N(d - j + 1, \lambda)$ consists of a copy of $N(d - j, \lambda)$, a
new outer circle, and a radial edge joining each vertex of the new circle to the vertex of the old
outer circle on the same radial line. Use the cyclic model of $G_{j+1}$ in the copy. Since
$x_1, \dots, x_q$ are in the cyclic order of $C_{j+1}$ and the intervals are long, we can choose
radial lines $\ell_1, \dots, \ell_q$, in order around the circles, such that $\ell_i$ meets the old
outer circle in $I(x_i)$, with at least $p$ other radial lines between any two of them that are
consecutive in the cyclic order. Since $y_1, \dots, y_q$ are in the cyclic order of $C_j$, we can
then divide the new outer circle into intervals $I'(y)$ $(y \in V(C_j))$, each with at least one
vertex and in the clockwise cyclic order of $C_j$, such that $\ell_i$ meets it in $I'(y_i)$, as in
the case $j = d$. Represent each $y \in V(C_j)$ by $I'(y)$, each edge of $C_j$ by the edge of the
new circle between the intervals of its ends, and each $g_i$ by the radial edge of $\ell_i$ joining
the two outer circles. With the model of $G_{j+1}$, this is a cyclic model of $G_j$ in
$N(d - j + 1, \lambda)$, by Step 2. This completes the induction and the proof of (2.3).

## 3. Proof of (2.5), page 55: the condition in the definition of $t'$

**Kind.** Misprint.

**What goes wrong.** The proof of (2.5) defines $X_t$ for each vertex $t$ of the tree $T$, "where
$t' = t$ if $t$ is the 'center' of $T$, and $t'$ is the neighbour of $t$ closer to the center if
$t'$ is not the center of $T$." The second condition is on $t'$, which is the vertex being defined.

## 4. Proof of (2.5), pages 55–56: the tree-decomposition of $N(d, \lambda)$

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper defines the sets $X_t$ and says: "It is easy to verify that
$(T, \mathcal{X})$ is a tree-decomposition of $N(d, \lambda)$ of width $\le 3d + 1$".

**Proof.** We give the details. If $d = 0$, then $N(0, \lambda) = Y_\lambda$ is a tree, and the sets
$\{t, t'\}$ $(t \in V(Y_\lambda))$, where $t'$ is the neighbour of $t$ closer to the center (and
$t' = t$ for the center), form a tree-decomposition of width $\le 1$ over the tree $Y_\lambda$. Now
let $d > 0$. Then $|X_t| \le 3d + 2$, and so the width is at most $3d + 1$. For each edge of $C$
with ends $u, v$, where $v$ follows $u$, let $R(u, v)$ be the region of $M$ inside $C$ incident with
this edge. It is bounded by the edge and the path of $Y_\lambda$ between $u$ and $v$, and the
vertices of $T$ on $R(u, v)$ are the internal vertices of this path, of which there is at least one.
Since the sets $P_v$ are disjoint, a vertex $x$ of the cylinder with $x \in P_v$ is in $X_t$ if and
only if $t$ is on $R(u, v)$. So the set of all $t$ with $x \in X_t$ is non-empty, and consists of
the internal vertices of the path of $Y_\lambda$ between $u$ and $v$. For each of them except the
one nearest to the center, the neighbour $t'$ closer to the center is also on this path. So this set
is the vertex set of a subtree of $T$. A vertex $s$ of $T$ is in $X_t$ if and only if $t = s$ or
$t' = s$, and so the set of these $t$ is the vertex set of a subtree of $T$. This proves (W1) and
(W3). For (W2), an edge of $T$ joins some $t \ne t'$ to $t'$, and both are in $X_t$. An edge of
$Y_\lambda$ joining an end-vertex $v$ to $t$ has $t$ on $R(u, v)$, and so
$P_v \cup \{t\} \subseteq X_t$. An edge on a radial line has both ends in some $P_v$. Finally, an
edge of a circle joins $P_u$ and $P_v$ for some edge of $C$ with ends $u, v$, where $v$ follows $u$.
Let $w$ be the vertex of $C$ that $u$ follows, and let $s$ be the neighbour of $u$ in $Y_\lambda$.
Then $s$ is on $R(w, u)$ and on $R(u, v)$, and so $P_u \cup P_v \subseteq X_s$.

## 5. Proof of (2.6), page 56: tree-width of minors

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper states (2.6), "If $G'$ is isomorphic to a minor of $G$ then the
tree-width of $G'$ is not greater than the tree-width of $G$", and says "The proof is clear."

**Proof.** To see this, let $(T, \mathcal{X})$ be a tree-decomposition of $G$ of width $w$, and let
$G'$ be a minor of $G$. Then there are disjoint non-empty sets $Y(v) \subseteq V(G)$
$(v \in V(G'))$, each inducing a connected subgraph of $G$, and for each edge $e$ of $G'$ an edge
$f(e)$ of $G$, such that if $e$ has ends $u, v$, then $f(e)$ has one end in $Y(u)$ and the other in
$Y(v)$. (Take for $Y(v)$ the set of vertices of $G$ that are contracted into $v$, and for $f(e)$ the
edge of $G$ that becomes $e$.) For $t \in V(T)$ let $X'_t$ be the set of all $v \in V(G')$ with
$Y(v) \cap X_t \ne \emptyset$. Choosing for each $v \in X'_t$ a vertex in $Y(v) \cap X_t$ gives
distinct vertices for distinct $v$, since the sets $Y(v)$ are disjoint, and so $|X'_t| \le |X_t|$.
We claim that $(T, (X'_t : t \in V(T)))$ is a tree-decomposition of $G'$, which therefore has width
$\le w$. For (W1), each $Y(v)$ is non-empty, and every vertex of $G$ is in some $X_t$. For (W2), let
$e$ be an edge of $G'$ with ends $u, v$. Then $f(e)$ has ends $a \in Y(u)$ and $b \in Y(v)$, and
$a, b \in X_t$ for some $t$, by (W2) for $G$. Then $u, v \in X'_t$. For (W3), let $t'$ be on the
path of $T$ between $t_1$ and $t_2$, and let $v \in X'_{t_1} \cap X'_{t_2}$. Suppose that
$Y(v) \cap X_{t'} = \emptyset$. Choose $y_1 \in Y(v) \cap X_{t_1}$ and $y_2 \in Y(v) \cap X_{t_2}$.
Then $t_1, t_2 \ne t'$. We use (3.1) and (3.2) below, which are proved there from (W1)–(W3) alone.
Since $Y(v)$ induces a connected subgraph of $G$ with no vertex in $X_{t'}$, applying (3.2) along a
path of $G|Y(v)$ from $y_1$ to $y_2$ shows that $T_{t'}(y_1) = T_{t'}(y_2)$. By (3.1) this branch of
$T$ at $t'$ contains $t_1$ and $t_2$. But $t_1$ and $t_2$ are in different branches of $T$ at $t'$,
since $t'$ is on the path of $T$ between them. This is a contradiction, and so
$Y(v) \cap X_{t'} \ne \emptyset$, that is, $v \in X'_{t'}$.

## 6. Proof of (2.8), page 56: nested circuits

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The paper introduces (2.8) as "the following lemma, which is easy and is left
to the reader", and gives no proof.

**Proof.** *Proof.* We may assume that $d \ge 1$. Since $G$ has radius $\ge d$, we have
$\rho(M) \ge d$, and so there is a region $R^*$ of $M$ with $d(R^*) \ge d$. Choose a point $p$ in
the infinite region of $M$ and a point $a$ in $R^*$. For $0 \le t \le d - 1$, let $U_t$ be the union
of the closures of all regions $R$ of $M$ with $d(R) \le t$. Then $U_t$ is connected, since each
such region is joined to the infinite region by a sequence of regions as in the definition of
$d(R)$, and consecutive regions of the sequence have a common vertex. Moreover $a \notin U_t$, since
$R^*$ is open and meets no other region. Let $B_t$ be the subgraph of $G$ consisting of the vertices
and edges that are incident both with a region $R$ with $d(R) \le t$ and with a region $R'$ with
$d(R') > t$. If a vertex is incident with a region $R$, then every region incident with it has
distance at most $d(R) + 1$.

(1) *For $0 \le t < t' \le d-1$, $B_t$ is contained in $U_t$, and no point of $B_{t'}$ is in $U_t$.*

The first statement holds by the definition of $B_t$. For the second, a vertex of $B_{t'}$ in $U_t$
would be incident with a region of distance $\le t$ and with a region of distance $> t' \ge t+1$,
which is impossible. If some other point of an edge $f$ of $B_{t'}$ is in $U_t$, then $f$ is
incident with a region of distance $\le t$, and so are its ends. But its ends are also incident with
a region of distance $> t'$, since $f$ is, and this is impossible as before. This proves (1).

(2) *For $0 \le t \le d-1$ there is a circuit $C_{t+1}$ of $B_t$ with $R^*$ inside and the infinite
region of $M$ outside.*

Suppose not. Then for every circuit of $B_t$, one of the two closed discs in the sphere bounded by
it contains neither $p$ nor $a$. It follows that $p$ and $a$ are in the same region of the drawing
of $B_t$, since two points in different regions of a drawing are separated by some circuit of it. So
there is a curve from $p$ to $a$ that meets no point of $B_t$. It starts in $U_t$ and ends outside
$U_t$, and so it passes through a point of the boundary of $U_t$. This point is in the closure of a
region of distance $\le t$ and in the closure of a region of distance $> t$, and so it is a vertex
or a point of an edge of $B_t$, which is impossible. So some circuit of $B_t$ is such that each of
its two closed discs contains $p$ or $a$. Since $p, a$ are not on the circuit, one disc contains $p$
and not $a$, and the other contains $a$ and not $p$. This proves (2).

Now let $0 \le t < t' \le d - 1$. By (1), $C_{t+1}$ is contained in $U_t$, and $C_{t'+1}$ is
disjoint from $U_t$. So $C_{t+1}$ and $C_{t'+1}$ have no common vertex. Moreover $U_t$ is connected,
contains the infinite region and is disjoint from $C_{t'+1}$, and so it is outside $C_{t'+1}$. In
particular $C_{t+1}$ is outside $C_{t'+1}$. So the part of the sphere inside $C_{t'+1}$ is
connected, contains $R^*$, and is disjoint from $C_{t+1}$, and therefore it is inside $C_{t+1}$.
Hence $C_{t'+1}$, which is in its closure and has no common vertex with $C_{t+1}$, is inside
$C_{t+1}$. Thus $C_1, \dots, C_d$ is a nested sequence of circuits of $M$.
