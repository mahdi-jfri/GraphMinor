# Graph Minors XIX: corrections

N. Robertson and P. D. Seymour, *Graph Minors. XIX. Well-quasi-ordering on a surface*, J. Combin.
Theory Ser. B **90** (2004), 325–385.

A corrected copy of the paper, with each correction below marked in place, is
[GM19/GM19.pdf](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers/blob/main/GM19/GM19.pdf) in the
repository [GraphMinorPapers](https://github.com/hajiaghayi/GraphMinorPapers).

The corrections are in the order of the pages.

## 1. Section 3, page 329: the remark on linear inflations

**Kind.** Statement mistake.

**What goes wrong.** Section 3 says "If $\sigma$ respects $\Phi$, and satisfies the second condition
in the definition of "linear", then $\sigma$ is linear, as the reader should verify." The first
condition of "linear" asks that each edge $e$ of $\Gamma$ borders a cuff exactly when $\sigma(e)$
borders it. Respecting $\Phi$ gives this when $e$ is a border edge. But nothing stops $\sigma$ from
mapping an internal edge to a border edge.

Here is an example. Let $\Sigma$ be the Möbius band obtained from the square $[0,1]^2$ by gluing
$(0, y)$ to $(1, 1-y)$ for $0 \le y \le 1$. Its one cuff $\Theta$ consists of the bottom and top
sides of the square. Let $p = (0.1, 0)$, $b_1 = (0.3, 0)$, $b_2 = (0.6, 0)$, $t_1 = (0.3, 1)$ and
$t_2 = (0.6, 1)$. They lie on $\Theta$ in this cyclic order. Let $\Phi$ have vertices $p$ and $t_2$,
a long side $S_1$ from $p$ through $b_1, b_2, t_1$ to $t_2$, and a short side $S_2$ from $t_2$ to
$p$. In both paintings below, each border edge is directed as its side.

The painting $\Gamma'$ has vertices $p, b_1, b_2, t_1, t_2$. Its edges are the five border edges
between consecutive ones; the vertical segments $b_1t_1$ and $b_2t_2$; the segment $pt_1$, directed
from $p$; and an edge $c$ directed from $b_2$ to $p$, which runs from $p$ to $(0, 1/2)$, which is
glued to $(1, 1/2)$, and from there along a segment to $b_2$. Its regions are the rectangle between
the two vertical segments and three triangles, on $p, b_1, t_1$, on $p, t_1, b_2$ and on
$p, b_2, t_2$.

The painting $\Gamma$ has vertices $p$, $u = b_1$ and $w = t_2$. Its border edges are $f_1$ from $p$
to $u$, $f_2$ from $u$ to $w$, and $f_3 = S_2$. Its internal edges are the segment $e$ from $u$ to
$w$; an edge $e'$ from $w$ to $u$ just to the right of $e$, so that $e$ and $e'$ bound a thin disc;
the segment $h$ from $w$ to $p$; and an edge $g$ from $p$ to $u$, which runs from $p$ to $(0, 1/2)$,
which is glued to $(1, 1/2)$, and from there along a segment to $u$. Its regions are the thin disc
and three triangles, with sides $f_1, e, h$, sides $h, f_2, g$, and sides $g, e', f_3$.

All vertices are in $bd(\Sigma)$, and each region of $\Gamma$ or $\Gamma'$ is an open disc whose
boundary passes each vertex at most once. So both paintings are internally 3-connected, and both fit
$\Phi$. Let $\sigma(p)$ be the vertex $p$, let $\sigma(u)$ be the segment $b_1t_1$ with its ends,
and let $\sigma(w)$ be the segment $b_2t_2$ with its ends. Let $\sigma(f_1) = pb_1$,
$\sigma(f_2) = t_1t_2$, $\sigma(f_3) = S_2$, $\sigma(e) = b_1b_2$, $\sigma(e') = b_2t_1$,
$\sigma(g) = pt_1$ and $\sigma(h) = c$. Then $\sigma$ is an inflation of $\Gamma$ in $\Gamma'$ which
respects $\Phi$. Every border edge of $\Gamma'$ is in $\sigma(E(\Gamma))$, so the second condition
holds. But $e$ is internal and $\sigma(e)$ is a border edge, so the first condition fails.

**Correction.** Add the hypothesis that $\sigma$ maps every internal edge of $\Gamma$ to an internal
edge of $\Gamma'$. Then the remark holds, and the copy adds the proof. The paper uses the remark
only at the end of the proof of 6.2 (page 343), to show that $\sigma'$ is linear. There the added
hypothesis holds: an internal edge $e$ with $\sigma'(e)$ in a cuff $\Theta$ would make $\sigma'(e)$
an edge of the circuit $C_2$ which is not some $\sigma'(e_i)$, so an edge of some path
$P_k \subseteq \sigma'(v_k)$; but $\sigma'(v_k)$ contains at most one end of $\sigma'(e)$. The copy
adds this argument to the proof of 6.2. So 6.2 and the later results are not affected.

**Lean.** The remark is not stated on its own in the formalization. The linearity in the proof of
6.2 is proved in `GraphMinor.GM19.theorem_6_2_of_6_6` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section6Two.lean`. There
an internal edge going to a border edge is excluded with `GraphMinor.GM19.cuff_cover` in
`GraphMinor/GM19/Section6Cover.lean`, and the third condition is
`GraphMinor.GM19.precedes_of_dirAgrees` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section6Cuff.lean`. The same steps in
the proof of 5.4 are `GraphMinor.GM19.side_subset_cuff` and `GraphMinor.GM19.glue_linear` in
`GraphMinor/GM19/Section5l.lean`, and `GraphMinor.GM19.immediatelyPrecedes_transfer` in
`GraphMinor/GM19/Section5i.lean`.

## 2. Proof of 2.1, page 331: $\sigma$ is linear

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** At the end of the proof that 3.1 implies 2.1, the paper builds an inflation
$\sigma$ of $\Gamma_i$ in $\Gamma_j$ from a linear inflation $\sigma'$ of $(\Gamma'_i, \phi'_i)$ in
$(\Gamma'_j, \phi'_j)$, and says "Moreover, it is clearly linear". The copy checks the three
conditions in the definition of "linear".

**Proof.** We check the three conditions in the definition of "linear". For the first, let
$e \in E(\Gamma_i)$ and let $\Theta$ be a cuff. If $|\tilde{e}| = 3$, then $e$ and $\sigma(e)$ are
internal, since border edges have size 2, and so neither borders $\Theta$. If $|\tilde{e}| = 2$,
then $e$ and $\sigma(e) = \sigma'(e)$ are edges of $\Gamma'_i$ and $\Gamma'_j$, and $e$ borders
$\Theta$ if and only if $\sigma'(e)$ does, since $\sigma'$ is linear. For the second, let $e'$ be a
border edge of $\Gamma_j$ with $e' \notin \sigma(E(\Gamma_i))$. Then $e'$ is a border edge of
$\Gamma'_j$, and it is not a short side, since $\sigma(s) = s$ for every short side $s$; so the
second term of $\phi'_j(e')$ is $\pm 1$. We claim that $e' \notin \sigma'(E(\Gamma'_i))$. For
suppose that $e' = \sigma'(c)$. Then $c$ has size 2, since $e'$ has. If $c \in E_i^2$, then
$\sigma(c) = \sigma'(c) = e'$, which is impossible. Otherwise $c$ is an edge of $sk(\Gamma_i)$ not
in $E(\Gamma_i)$, and so the second term of $\phi'_i(c)$ is $0$; but $\phi'_i(c) \le \phi'_j(e')$,
and $R$ is ordered by equality, a contradiction. This proves the claim. Since $\sigma'$ is linear,
it follows that $e'$ is an edge of $\sigma'(v)$ for some $v \in V(\Gamma_i)$. Both ends of $e'$ are
in $V(\sigma'(v)) = V(\sigma(v))$, and $e'$ is an edge of $sk(\Gamma_j)$; so $e'$ is an edge of
$\sigma(v)$, since $\sigma(v)$ is an induced subgraph of $sk(\Gamma_j)$. For the third, let $e$
border a cuff $\Theta$. Then $e \in E_i^2$ and $\sigma(e) = \sigma'(e)$. As in the proof of (2),
either $\gamma'_i(e) = \gamma_i(e)$ and $\gamma'_j(\sigma(e)) = \gamma_j(\sigma(e))$, or
$\gamma'_i(e)$ and $\gamma'_j(\sigma(e))$ are the reverses of $\gamma_i(e)$ and
$\gamma_j(\sigma(e))$, since these marches have two terms. In the first case the condition for
$\sigma$ is the condition for $\sigma'$. In the second case, orient $\Theta$ so that the tail of $e$
in $\Gamma_i$ immediately precedes $e$. Under the reverse orientation, the tail of $e$ in
$\Gamma'_i$ immediately precedes $e$; so, since $\sigma'$ is linear, the tail of $\sigma(e)$ in
$\Gamma'_j$ immediately precedes $\sigma(e)$ under the reverse orientation. Hence, under the first
orientation, the tail of $\sigma(e)$ in $\Gamma_j$ immediately precedes $\sigma(e)$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM19.exists_pull` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section2.lean`, where the three
conditions are the last three goals. The second condition uses the labels through
`GraphMinor.GM19.thinColour_cases`, and the third uses `GraphMinor.GM19.SameDir`, which comes from
`GraphMinor.GM19.exists_partner`, and `GraphMinor.GM19.ImmediatelyPrecedes.reverse`, in the same
file. This is used in `GraphMinor.GM19.theorem_2_1_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section2Proved.lean`.

## 3. Proof of 4.2, page 333: the parts of the claim called "mostly clear"

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The proof of 4.2 claims that a linear inflation $\sigma$ of
$(\Gamma'_i, \phi'_i)$ in $(\Gamma'_j, \phi'_j)$ is also a linear inflation of $(\Gamma_i, \phi_i)$
in $(\Gamma_j, \phi_j)$, and says "This is mostly clear, but let us show that the third condition in
the definition of "inflation" is satisfied". The copy adds the other parts.

**Proof.** The paintings $\Gamma_i$ and $\Gamma'_i$ have the same vertices and edges, and differ
only in the marches of edges of size 3; and the same holds for $\Gamma_j$ and $\Gamma'_j$. The first
two conditions in the definition of an inflation, the first two conditions in the definition of
"linear", and the definition of "respects $\Phi_\chi$" do not involve the marches. So $\sigma$
satisfies them as a map from $\Gamma_i$ to $\Gamma_j$, since it satisfies them as a map from
$\Gamma'_i$ to $\Gamma'_j$. For the third condition in the definition of "linear", the tail of an
edge is the first term of its march, and $\gamma_i(e), \gamma'_i(e)$ have the same first term for
every $e \in E(\Gamma_i)$, and similarly for $\Gamma_j$. So each edge has the same tail in
$\Gamma_i$ as in $\Gamma'_i$, and in $\Gamma_j$ as in $\Gamma'_j$, and the condition holds for
$\sigma$ since it holds for $\sigma$ as a linear inflation of $\Gamma'_i$ in $\Gamma'_j$. Moreover
$\phi_i(e) \le \phi_j(\sigma(e))$ for each $e \in E(\Gamma_i)$. If $|\tilde{e}| = 2$ this is
$\phi'_i(e) \le \phi'_j(\sigma(e))$; and if $|\tilde{e}| = 3$, then $\sigma(e)$ also has size 3, and
it follows by comparing the first terms of $\phi'_i(e)$ and $\phi'_j(\sigma(e))$.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM19.IsColouredLinearInflation.of_orient` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section4b.lean`,
used in `GraphMinor.GM19.theorem_4_2_proof` in the same file.

## 4. Proof of 4.3, page 334: the proof

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** After 4.3 the paper says "The result is well-known and the proof is easy, and
so we omit it."

**Proof.** We give the proof. Suppose that such a sequence exists. The relation $\preceq$ is
transitive, as one checks from its three conditions, and so $\Omega_j \preceq \Omega_i$ for all
$j \ge i \ge 1$. For each $i \ge 1$, $E(\Omega_{i+1}) \ne E(\Omega_i)$, since otherwise the third
condition in the definition of $\preceq$ would imply that $\Omega_{i+1} = \Omega_i$; so we may
choose $x_i \in E(\Omega_i) - E(\Omega_{i+1})$. Then $x_i \in E(\Omega_1)$ for all $i \ge 1$, and
since $\Omega_1$ is a well-quasi-order, there exist $j > i \ge 1$ such that $x_i \le x_j$ in
$\Omega_1$. Now $x_j \in E(\Omega_j) \subseteq E(\Omega_{i+1})$ and $\Omega_{i+1} \preceq \Omega_1$,
and so the second condition in the definition of $\preceq$ implies that $x_i \in E(\Omega_{i+1})$, a
contradiction. This proves 4.3.

restated as `GraphMinor.GM19.theorem_4_3` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Results.lean`. The transitivity of
$\preceq$ is `GraphMinor.GM19.QuasiOrder.IsSubIdeal.trans` in the same file.

## 5. Proof of 5.2, page 335: the proof

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** Before 5.2 the paper says that 5.2 follows easily "by choosing a line to
represent an edge of $K$, cutting the surface along it, and using induction on $|E(K)|$", and refers
to Section 3 of Graph Minors VII for similar proofs. The proof is only sketched.

**Proof.** We give the proof. Let $K$ be a feature in $\Phi$ with size $\le n$. First we replace $K$
and $bd(\Sigma)$ by a finite family of lines in $\Sigma$, any two of which meet only in common ends.
The closure of each edge of $K$ is a line or an $O$-arc; it gives one line of the family in the
first case, and two in the second, by splitting it at a point of the edge. The vertices of $\Phi$
and of $K$, together with a finite set of points of $bd(\Sigma)$ that depends only on $\Sigma$, cut
$bd(\Sigma)$ into further lines of the family. Each of these lies in the closure of one side of
$\Phi$, since its interior contains no vertex of $\Phi$. Thus the number of lines is at most a
number $N$ that depends only on $\Sigma$, $\Phi$ and $n$. We number the lines $1, 2, \dots$, and we
cut $\Sigma$ along all of them. This gives a surface $Y$, perhaps not connected, with a map onto
$\Sigma$ that is one-to-one off $bd(Y)$. Each line of the family not included in $bd(\Sigma)$ has
two lifts in $bd(Y)$, and each line in $bd(\Sigma)$ has one, and these lifts cover $bd(Y)$ and meet
only in common ends. Let $W$ be a component of $Y$, with $a$ handles, $b$ crosscaps and $c$ cuffs.
By the classification of surfaces, $W$ together with the lifts in $bd(W)$ is determined up to
homeomorphism by $a$, $b$, $c$ and the cyclic order of the lifts around each cuff of $W$; we call
these data the *form* of $W$, and we call the set of the forms of the components of $Y$ the *code*
of the family. Here $2a + b$ is at most the corresponding number for $\hat{\Sigma}$, because each
choice of values in $\mathbb{Z}_2$ on the $2a + b$ standard loops of $W$ gives a class in
$\mathbb{Z}_2$ on $\Sigma$ (since the map from $W$ to $\Sigma$ is one-to-one off $bd(W)$), and
different choices give different classes. Also $c$ is at most the number of lifts. Hence for each
number of lines there are only finitely many codes. If two families with the same number of lines
have the same code, then there is a homeomorphism of $\Sigma$ that maps each line of the first
family onto the line of the second with the same number, end to end; for the components with the
same form are homeomorphic by maps that keep the lifts, and these maps agree over each line, so
together they give a homeomorphism of $\Sigma$.

To each line we attach a *tag*, which says which side of $\Phi$ includes it (if it lies in
$bd(\Sigma)$), which of its ends are vertices of $\Phi$, and which ones, and which of its ends are
vertices of $K$. Now let $K, K'$ be features with size $\le n$ whose families have the same number
of lines, the same code and the same tags, and let $\alpha$ be the homeomorphism just described.
Then $\alpha(v) = v$ for every $v \in V(\Phi)$, since each such $v$ is an end of a line, and the
tags say that the corresponding end of the corresponding line is $v$. Hence $\alpha(S) = S$ for
every side $S$ of $\Phi$, since the closure of $S$ is the union of the lines whose tags assign them
to $S$. In the same way $\alpha(U(K)) = U(K')$ and $\alpha(V(K)) = V(K')$. If $\Sigma$ is not
orientable, or $\alpha$ preserves the orientation of $\Sigma$, it follows that $K, K'$ are
equivalent. Otherwise $\Sigma$ is orientable and $\alpha$ reverses its orientation, since a
homeomorphism of a connected orientable surface either preserves or reverses its orientation. If
$K', K''$ are both obtained from $K$ in this way, by $\alpha'$ and $\alpha''$ say, then
$\alpha'' \circ \alpha'^{-1}$ preserves the orientation, and so $K', K''$ are equivalent. Thus the
features with given number of lines, code and tags lie in at most two equivalence classes. Since
there are only finitely many choices of the number of lines (at most $N$), of the code and of the
tags, this proves 5.2.

**Lean.** `GraphMinor.GM19.theorem_5_2_proof` in `GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureCount.lean`. The family of
lines is `GraphMinor.GM19.exists_feature_lines` in `GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureLines.lean`; the
finiteness of the codes and the homeomorphism from a code are `GraphMinor.GM19.finite_codes` and
`GraphMinor.GM19.homeo_of_code` in `GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureCode.lean`; the bound on $2a + b$ is
`GraphMinor.GM19.genus_le` in `GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureGenus.lean`; the tags are used in
`GraphMinor.GM19.frame_homeo_of_lines` and `GraphMinor.GM19.finite_reps_of_labels` in
`GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureCount.lean`; and the two orientations are
`GraphMinor.GM19.homeo_orientation` in `GraphMinor/GM19/FeatureOrient.lean`.

## 6. Proof of 5.3, page 336: the first three conditions of "fit"

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The proof of 5.3 says "Verifying the first three conditions in the definition
of "fit" is easy and is omitted." The proof also uses, without comment, that
$\psi^{-1}(\Phi) \cap \Sigma_1$ is a frame, that is, that no two of its long sides have a common
end.

**Proof.** We give the details, and we check also that $\Phi_1 = \psi^{-1}(\Phi) \cap \Sigma_1$ is a
frame, that is, that no two long sides of $\Phi_1$ have a common end. Now $\psi$ maps $bd(\Sigma_1)$
into $bd(\Sigma) \cup U(K)$, and $bd(\Sigma) \cap U(K) \subseteq V(K)$, since
$bd(\Sigma) \subseteq U(\Gamma)$ and $U(\Gamma) \cap U(K) = V(\Gamma) \cap V(K)$. Since each side
$R$ of $\Phi_1$ is arcwise connected and disjoint from $\psi^{-1}(V(\Phi) \cup V(K))$, it follows
that $\psi(R)$ is included either in a side of $\Phi$ or in an edge of $K$.

Suppose that two long sides $R_1, R_2$ of $\Phi_1$ have a common end $p$. Near $p$, $bd(\Sigma_1)$
consists of $p$ and points of $R_1 \cup R_2$, and so it is mapped by $\psi$ into $bd(\Sigma)$.
Consequently $\psi$ maps the points of $\Sigma_1 \setminus bd(\Sigma_1)$ near $p$ onto all the
points of $\Sigma \setminus bd(\Sigma)$ near $\psi(p)$; and since
$\psi^{-1}(U(K)) \subseteq bd(\Sigma_1)$, no point of $U(K) \setminus bd(\Sigma)$ is near $\psi(p)$.
Since $K$ has no isolated vertices and no border edges, it follows that $\psi(p) \notin V(K)$, and
so $\psi(p) \in V(\Phi)$. For $i = 1, 2$, since $R_i$ is long, $\psi(R_i)$ is included in a long
side $S_i$ of $\Phi$, and $\psi(p)$ is an end of $S_i$. Since no two long sides of $\Phi$ have a
common end, $S_1 = S_2$. Now $\psi$ maps the points of $R_1$ and of $R_2$ near $p$ onto the points
of $S_1$ near its end $\psi(p)$, and so there exist $x_1 \in R_1$ and $x_2 \in R_2$, both near $p$,
with $\psi(x_1) = \psi(x_2)$. Near $x_1$ and near $x_2$, as near $p$, $\psi$ maps the points of
$\Sigma_1 \setminus bd(\Sigma_1)$ onto all the points of $\Sigma \setminus bd(\Sigma)$ near
$\psi(x_1)$; since $\psi$ is one-to-one on $\Sigma_1 \setminus bd(\Sigma_1)$, it follows that
$x_1 = x_2$, and so $R_1 \cap R_2 \ne \emptyset$, which is impossible. Thus $\Phi_1$ is a frame.

For the first condition,
$V(\Phi_1) = \psi^{-1}(V(\Phi) \cup V(K)) \cap \Sigma_1 \subseteq \psi^{-1}(V(\Gamma) \cup V(K)) \cap \Sigma_1 = V(\Gamma_1)$,
since $V(\Phi) \subseteq V(\Gamma)$. For the second, let $R$ be a short side of $\Phi_1$. If
$\psi(R)$ is included in a side $S$ of $\Phi$, then $S$ is short, by the definition of $\Phi_1$; so
$S$ is an edge of $\Gamma$, since $\Gamma$ fits $\Phi$, and therefore
$S \cap V(\Gamma) = \emptyset$. Otherwise $\psi(R)$ is included in an edge $f$ of $K$, and
$f \cap V(\Gamma) = \emptyset$ since $U(\Gamma) \cap U(K) = V(\Gamma) \cap V(K)$. In both cases $R$
is disjoint from $\psi^{-1}(V(\Gamma) \cup V(K))$, and so $R$ is an arcwise connected component of
$bd(\Sigma_1) \setminus V(\Gamma_1)$; that is, $R$ is an edge of $\Gamma_1$. For the third, let $e$
be a border edge of $\Gamma_1$. Every edge of $\Gamma_1$ not included in $bd(\Sigma_1)$ is of the
form $\psi^{-1}(f) \cap \Sigma_1$ for some internal edge $f$ of $\Gamma$, and is disjoint from
$bd(\Sigma_1)$, since $\psi$ maps $bd(\Sigma_1)$ into $bd(\Sigma) \cup U(K)$, which $f$ does not
meet. Hence $e \subseteq bd(\Sigma_1)$, and $\gamma_1(e)$ was defined so that the direction of $e$
agrees with the direction of the side of $\Phi_1$ including it.

steps are `GraphMinor.GM19.FrameData.pullFrameV_subset` and
`GraphMinor.GM19.FrameData.dirAgrees_pull` in `GraphMinor/GM19/Section5c.lean`;

## 7. Proof of 5.4, page 338: $\sigma$ is a linear inflation

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The proof of 5.4 says "It is easy to deduce that $\sigma$ is a linear inflation
of $(\Gamma_i, \phi_i)$ in $(\Gamma_j, \phi_j)$ (for similar arguments, see for example Section 8 of
[3])", where [3] is Graph Minors IV. The details use one more choice of subsequence, which the copy
adds after "By 5.1 we may assume that all the $K_i$'s are equal, to some $K$ say."

**Proof.** Since $V(K)$ has only finitely many subsets, we may also assume (by replacing our
sequence by a subsequence) that $V(K) \cap V(\Gamma_i)$ is the same for all $i \ge 1$.

We give the details. Let $\Phi' = \Phi_{\chi'}$. We use the following facts about $\psi$, which
follow from the definitions; they hold for $\Gamma_j$ as well as for $\Gamma_i$. Each point of
$\Sigma_\chi \setminus U(K)$ has exactly one point of $\Sigma_1$ over it, and
$\psi^{-1}(U(K)) \subseteq bd(\Sigma_1)$. Each edge of $\Gamma_i'$ not included in $bd(\Sigma_1)$ is
$\psi^{-1}(e)$ for an internal edge $e$ of $\Gamma_i$; it has the same size as $e$, and $\psi$ maps
$\gamma_{\Gamma'_i}(\psi^{-1}(e))$ to $\gamma_{\Gamma_i}(e)$. If $e$ is a border edge of $\Gamma_i$
then $\psi^{-1}(e)$ is a border edge of $\Gamma_i'$. An edge of $\Gamma_i'$ included in
$bd(\Sigma_1)$ that has a point over $bd(\Sigma_\chi)$ lies over $bd(\Sigma_\chi)$ and is
$\psi^{-1}(e)$ for a border edge $e$ of $\Gamma_i$, $\psi$ is one-to-one on its closure, and $\psi$
maps its march to that of $e$, since both edges are directed as the sides that include them and
$\psi$ maps the direction of each long side of $\Phi'$ onto that of a side of $\Phi_\chi$. An edge
of $\Gamma_i'$ included in $bd(\Sigma_1)$ with no point over $bd(\Sigma_\chi)$ lies over $U(K)$, and
both its ends are in $V(\Phi')$. Finally, since $\sigma'$ respects $\Phi'$, we have
$y \in V(\sigma'(y))$ for every $y \in V(\Phi')$, as we saw in Section 3; and so, since the
$\sigma'(y)$ are mutually disjoint, if $x \in V(\Phi')$ and $x \in V(\sigma'(y))$ for some
$y \in V(\Gamma_i')$, then $x = y$.

Let $e \in E(\Gamma_i)$. If $e$ is internal, then $\psi^{-1}(e)$ is an internal edge of $\Gamma_i'$,
and so is $\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e))$, since $\sigma'$ is linear; hence
$\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e)) = \psi^{-1}(e')$ for an internal edge $e'$ of $\Gamma_j$, and
$\sigma(e) = e'$. If $e$ borders a side $S$ of $\Phi_\chi$, then $\psi^{-1}(e)$ borders the side $R$
of $\Phi'$ with $\psi(R) \subseteq S$, and so does $\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e))$, since $\sigma'$ respects
$\Phi'$; hence $\sigma(e)$ is a border edge of $\Gamma_j$ bordering $S$, and
$\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e)) = \psi^{-1}(\sigma(e))$. In both cases $\sigma(e)$ has the same size as $e$,
$\psi$ maps the $k$th end of $\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e))$ to the $k$th end of $\sigma(e)$ for each $k$,
and $\phi_i(e) \le \phi_j(\sigma(e))$, since
$\phi_i'(\psi^{-1}(e)) \le \phi_j'(\sigma'(\psi^{-1}(e)))$. Since $\sigma'$ is one-to-one on edges,
so is $\sigma$. Also $\sigma$ respects $\Phi_\chi$.

Next we show that $V(\sigma(v)) \subseteq V(\Gamma_j)$ for $v \in V(\Gamma_i)$. Let
$y \in \psi^{-1}(v)$ and $x \in V(\sigma'(y))$. Then $x \in V(\Gamma_j')$, so
$\psi(x) \in V(\Gamma_j) \cup V(K)$. If $\psi(x) \notin V(\Gamma_j)$, then $x \in V(\Phi')$, so
$x = y$ and $\psi(x) = v \in V(\Gamma_i) \cap V(K)$; but
$V(K) \cap V(\Gamma_i) = V(K) \cap V(\Gamma_j)$, and so $\psi(x) \in V(\Gamma_j)$, a contradiction.
Now we show that $\psi(V(\sigma'(y)))$ induces a connected subdrawing of $sk(\Gamma_j)$, which the
argument above uses. Let $a, b \in V(\sigma'(y))$ be ends of an edge $f$ of $\Gamma_j'$, with
$\psi(a) \ne \psi(b)$. If $f$ lies over $U(K)$, then $a, b \in V(\Phi')$, and so $a = y = b$, which

**Proof.** We give the proof, by adapting the proofs of 10.2, 10.3 and 10.4; we first choose the
circuit $F$ with care. Let $(\Gamma, \phi, \lambda, \mathscr{T}) \in \mathscr{D}$ be $n$-flawed in
freedom. Call a circuit $F$ of $\Gamma^*$ *eligible* if $\frac{1}{2}|E(F)| \le n$ and $ins(F)$
includes a long side of $\Phi_\chi$ or more than $\frac{1}{2}|E(F)|$ vertices of $\Phi_\chi$. (Since
$\frac{1}{2}|E(F)| \le n < rep(\mathscr{T})$, $ins(F)$ is defined.) Choose an eligible $F$ with
$|E(F)|$ minimum, and subject to this with $|V(\Gamma^*) \cap ins(F)|$ maximum. By 8.7 there is a
circuit $F'$ which is $\lambda$-linked to $\mathscr{T}$, with $|E(F')| \le |E(F)|$ and
$ins(F) \subseteq ins(F')$; then $F'$ is eligible, and it satisfies the same two extremal
conditions, so we may assume that $F$ is $\lambda$-linked to $\mathscr{T}$.

(a) *Let $Q$ be a path of $\Gamma^*$ with ends $a, b \in V(F)$ and with no point in the interior of
$ins(F)$, and let $A, B$ be the two paths of $F$ between $a$ and $b$. If
$|E(Q)| \le |E(A)|, |E(B)|$, then $V(Q) \subseteq ins(F)$ and $|E(Q)|$ equals one of
$|E(A)|, |E(B)|$.* For one of the circuits $Q \cup A$, $Q \cup B$, say $X$, has $ins(X)$ meeting the
interior of $ins(F)$, and then $ins(F) \subseteq ins(X)$, since $U(X)$ is disjoint from the interior
of $ins(F)$. So $X$ is eligible, and $|E(X)| \le |E(F)|$. If $Q$ had a vertex not in $ins(F)$, or
were shorter than both $A$ and $B$, then $X$ would contradict the choice of $F$.

(b) *Exactly one pole $r^*$ is in $ins(F)$, and the closed disc $\bar{r}'$ of every other pole
$r'^*$ meets $ins(F)$ in at most one point.* If two poles were in $ins(F)$, their distance would be
at most $\frac{1}{2}|E(F)| \le n$. Let $r'^*$ be a pole not in $ins(F)$. Then $r'^* \notin V(F)$, so
$r'$ is disjoint from $U(F)$, and since $r'^* \notin ins(F)$ it follows that
$\bar{r}' \cap ins(F) \subseteq U(F) \cap V(\Gamma)$; and two vertices $u, v$ of $\Gamma$ in this
set give a path $Q$ with edges $ur'^*$, $r'^*v$ that contradicts (a), since $|E(A)|, |E(B)| \ge 2$
as $\Gamma^*$ is bipartite. If no pole were in $ins(F)$, then by this argument $ins(F)$ would
include no long side, and the vertices of $\Phi_\chi$ in $ins(F)$ would lie in
$U(F) \cap V(\Gamma)$, which has $\frac{1}{2}|E(F)|$ members; so $F$ would not be eligible.

$U(K) = U(F) \cap \Sigma_\chi$ and $V(K) = U(F) \cap V(\Gamma)$. Then $K$ is a feature, and

vertices of $\Gamma$ and poles, and so $|V(K)| \le \frac{1}{2}|E(F)|$. Moreover $K$ has at most
$|V(K)|$ edges, since $U(K)$ is either the $O$-arc $U(F)$ (if $r^* \notin V(F)$) or a line with both
ends in $V(K)$ (if $r^* \in V(F)$, since then the two edges of $F$ at $r^*$ lie in $\bar{r}$ and

## 28. Proof of 15.2, page 383: $\mathscr{T}'$ is respectful

**Kind.** Missing proof.

**What is missing.** The proof of 15.2 says "By Theorem 14.1 of [9], $\mathscr{T}'$ is a tangle in
$sk(\Gamma)$ of order $n+1$ and $rep(\mathscr{T}') = n+1$", where [9] is Graph Minors XVII, and then
applies Theorem 3.2 of Graph Minors XII to $\mathscr{T}'$. That theorem needs $\mathscr{T}'$ to be
respectful, which the paper does not prove.

**Proof.** Theorem 14.1 of Graph Minors XVII gives only that $\mathscr{T}'$ is a tangle; Theorem 3.2
of Graph Minors XII needs also that $\mathscr{T}'$ is respectful, and we prove this now. If
$\hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ is a sphere, every tangle in a drawing in $\hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ is respectful; so
we assume it is not. Call an open set $W \subseteq \hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ *flat* if any two paths in $W$
with the same ends are homotopic in $\hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ with their ends fixed. Let $F$ be an
$sk(\Gamma)$-normal $O$-arc with $|F \cap V(\Gamma)| \le n$. As in the proof of (6.4) of Graph
Minors XI, the union of the regions of $\Gamma$ in $\hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ and of small open discs
around the vertices in $F \cap V(\Gamma)$ is flat; this uses that
$|F \cap V(\Gamma)| \le n < rep(\mathscr{T})$, and that every circuit $C$ of $\Gamma^*$ with
$\frac{1}{2}|E(C)| < rep(\mathscr{T})$ bounds a disc, namely $ins(C)$. The regions of $sk(\Gamma)$
are the regions of $\Gamma$ and the edges $c$ of $\Gamma$ with $|\tilde{c}| = 3$, each of which is
an open disc. Adding these discs keeps the set flat: any two ends $a, b$ of such an edge $c$ lie in
the boundary of a region $r$ of $\Gamma$ that has a part of $bd(\bar{c})$ between $a$ and $b$ in its
boundary, so a path in $\bar{c}$ from $a$ to $b$ is homotopic to a path in $r \cup \{a, b\}$. Since
$F$ is $sk(\Gamma)$-normal, it lies in this flat set, so $F$ is null-homotopic, and hence $F$ bounds
a closed disc $\Delta$. Let $\Delta'$ be the closure of $\hat{\Sigma}_\chi \setminus \Delta$. Then
$(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta, sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta')$ is a separation of order at most $n$, so it or
its reverse is in $\mathscr{T}'$, and we may assume that
$(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta', sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta) \in \mathscr{T}'$, for otherwise $\Delta$ is as
required. So there exists $(A, B) \in \mathscr{T}$ with $V(A) = V(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta')$ and
$V(B) = V(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta)$, of order at most $n < rep(\mathscr{T})$. As in the proof of
(6.3) of Graph Minors XI, there is a subdrawing $N$ of $\Gamma^*$ such that every circuit $C$ of $N$
has $\frac{1}{2}|E(C)| < rep(\mathscr{T})$, and every edge of $A$ lies in $ins(C)$ for some circuit
$C$ of $N$; these circuits are null-homotopic, so by (7.5) of Graph Minors XI there is a closed disc
$D_1$ including $U(N)$, and then $D_1$ includes each such $ins(C)$, since $\hat{\Sigma}_\chi$ is not
a sphere. Hence every edge of $A$ lies in $D_1$. On the other hand, every $O$-arc in
$U(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta)$ is null-homotopic, since it lies in the disc $\Delta$; so by the flat
set above and the argument of claim (2) in the proof of (6.4) of Graph Minors XI (which uses (7.5)
of Graph Minors XI), there is a non-null-homotopic $O$-arc
$F' \subseteq U(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta') \setminus U(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta)$. Then $F' = U(C')$ for
a circuit $C'$ of $sk(\Gamma)$. Each edge of $C'$ lies in an edge $c$ of $\Gamma$ which has an end
not in $V(B)$, since $F'$ misses $U(sk(\Gamma) \cap \Delta)$; so $c$ is an edge of $A$, and
$c \subseteq D_1$. Thus $F' \subseteq D_1$, and $F'$ is null-homotopic, a contradiction. So
$\mathscr{T}'$ is respectful.
